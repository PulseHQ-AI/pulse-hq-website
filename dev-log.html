Pulse HQ — Dev Log

The record of how a one-man company got built in the open, by the two people who built it. Written in the voice the work happened in — which, fair warning, includes swearing. The founders communicate like coworkers, not a press release, because we ain't no pussy.

Days 1–9 by the clock: ≈ 82 hours at the bench, 2,328 messages — counted from message timestamps (gaps capped at 60 minutes, so breaks don't inflate it), and not counting the founder's solo hours painting maps and sprites. Nearly two working weeks, compressed into the first nine days — the launch stretch. The log itself runs on past it; the entries below continue from there.

Prologue — why this office exists

The spark was a reel. Creator Annikka Aborro posted a concept: an AI office, named agents at little desks. Most people would have liked the aesthetic and scrolled on. Ben (founder — game designer, synthwave producer as Mr. Night, longtime RPG Maker developer as Skunk) wanted the thing itself: not a dashboard pretending to be a company, but an office that does real work and brings it to a human for one honest approval.

He built it the way he builds everything — like a game. Floors, roles, pipelines, regions on a map. Except this game's outputs post to real pages and its mistakes cost real credibility, which is why verification became this company's religion early (see Day 7).

The crew's names? In Ben's own words: “The names started as a cheap way to get my friends interested in the project.” Name your little brother the head of research and suddenly he's checking in on “his” department. It worked. Then, somewhere in the week, the trick turned real — the namesake crew is now the heart of the place, and the receptionist (named for Ben's mother, Marni) has a Coke routine and scheduled smoke breaks.

And the job description of the AI in the middle of it changed along the way. Ben, Day 8: “It takes AI assistant and turns it into AI partner.” That's the thesis of everything below.

Day 1 — Wednesday, October 7 — The office opens

  • Pulse HQ (then just “the AI office”) is rebuilt as a live, database-backed workspace: a task board (Inbox → Working → Needs approval → Done), an office log, and a living floor where the crew visibly works.
  • The crew is renamed for Ben's people: Tom (research, for his brother Tom Stretch), Nick (builder, for Nikola Wandler), Johnny (writer, for John Andriuk), Sven (the tracker). Dispatcher: Remmy.
  • First big end-to-end test: Ben's mom Marni's “OLD HIPPIES → CRAZY GRANDMAS” shirt becomes a print-on-demand store test — the office's first full run from idea to listings.
  • The pattern is set on day one: the crew works, Ben approves. Everything since is that sentence getting stronger.

Day 2 — Thursday, October 8 — Credit where due

  • Ben writes up the office publicly, crediting Annikka's reel as the spark. (Canon honesty is a house rule from the start: what's invented gets labeled invented, what's sourced gets credited.)
  • Perfectly Paranoid — Ben's other page, ~8.4K followers — gets its working flow: image, then copy with image, then Ben's explicit approval before anything posts.

Day 3 — Friday, October 9 — The floor becomes the interface

  • The office floor and a unified work panel replace the old dock-and-sidebar layout. Ben's ruling: “no sidebar is so much better.”
  • “Create work” lands: Ben describes a job, Remmy divides it into the minimum crew steps, Ben sees the plan before it runs.
  • Platform ruling, still in force for the workshop: PC-only. (The public showcase and client approval are the mobile exceptions — see the roadmap.)

Day 4 — Saturday, October 10 — The quiet day

  • 2.7 hours. Even founders of AI companies occasionally go outside. The office kept the lights on.

Day 5 — Sunday, October 11 — Paranoid goes AI, Meta says no

  • Perfectly Paranoid's AI relaunch goes live — new look, new content engine; the name, as ever since 2014, stays Perfectly Paranoid.
  • Meta's API blocks page posting with a ghost consent error (PROFESSIONAL_CONSENT_REQUIRED, with no consent card anywhere to consent on). Ben parks it permanently: the office drafts and packages; Ben posts by hand. Constraint accepted, workflow rebuilt around it, nobody wasted another hour begging the API.

Day 6 — Monday, October 12 — The doctrine, and the business

  • Sven's research hunt produces a playbook on done-for-you AI offices. Ben picks it as the company's main focus: sell deliverables and approver control — social media management — not “AI agents.” Pilot $500–$750 → setup → Core retainer $1,250–$1,750/mo.
  • Pulse HQ - AI Workspace is created on Facebook as the build-in-public home.
  • The operating doctrine is written down, in Ben's words: “approver, not babysitter.” Standing orders run straight to Done. Manual jobs flow through the crew and surface once for Approve / Rework / Delete. Ben's touchpoints: create the job, one final approval, answer genuine blockers.
  • Redbubble suspends the t-shirt account the same day (POD channel closed; TeePublic unsafe — same owner). Noted, absorbed, not the business anymore.

Day 7 — Tuesday, October 13 — The marathon (12.9 hours)

The day the office became a company with departments.

  • The Research Department is built, on Ben's own hand-painted lab map: Walter reads sources and extracts claims → Peter verifies each claim into one of four labels (DOCUMENTED / MAINSTREAM INTERPRETATION / FRINGE CLAIM / NOT VERIFIED) → Tom synthesizes; failures go on a do-not-publish list. Tom commutes between floors as a single instance.
  • Case File #1 (polygonal masonry) is posted: the largest supportable Sacsayhuamán block is ~128 tonnes, not 300+; the famous “35 km haul” is a conflation; the plant-acid wall-melting claims are unverified. Public commitments follow in Case File #2's post the same day.
  • Perfectly Paranoid's voice is locked: dark, cynical, dry. No hype, no “wake up sheeple.” Institutions assumed self-interested or asleep. Every post carries a stakes beat — “Why is this a big deal?” — and the facts stay verified-only. The voice darkens framing, never upgrades a claim.
  • X's API turns out to be paid-only mid-setup. Ben: “park it.” The compliant replacement is a new hire Ben designs and names: Olivia, drafting posts into a manual Post Queue — image or link always, never mentions Ben, attraction grammar only.
  • The Front Office is decided: a third, client-facing department (registry, delivery packages, reporting), run by Maria.
  • The briefing chat goes live: clicking Remmy in the office opens a direct line to the real Remmy, full memory — Ben's choice over a fresh in-office copy.
  • Preset jobs ship. Sven's work is ordered into plain human language (“a client who doesn't understand agentic lingo” is the test reader). The layman test becomes UI law: if Ben has to ask where an approved thing went, the design is wrong.
  • Late-night: Ben notes the day's work used ~5% of his daily capacity. The machine is getting efficient, or the founder is. Both, probably.

Day 8 — Wednesday, October 14 — The 14-hour day (so far)

The day started Tuesday and refused to end. In rough order:

  • The Front Office opens — third floor, Ben's own map — with Marni at reception (support presence, named for Ben's mom) and Maria running the department.
  • Perfectly Paranoid becomes Client #1 in the registry — the company's own page as its first client, exercising the full machinery: Maria intake → Tom → Johnny → Nick, delivery packages in Clients, never double-filed.
  • Two PP posts run the pipeline end-to-end and land as client delivery packages: Dyatlov Pass (Day 3 of the 14-day calendar, for Oct 15) and the Carl Higdon abduction story.
  • Tom's escalation rule fires live, twice. Tricky claims now get sent up to the Research Department mid-job (Walter → Peter → Tom) and the parent job waits for the verdict. Dyatlov came back 21 documented / 1 not verified; Higdon 8 / 1. The not-verified claims are do-not-publish, and Johnny obeys the list.
  • Aspect-control rework: Ben can now send back one part of a post — copy, image, or research — with instructions that apply to that part only, everything else frozen byte-for-byte. Proven on the Higdon post: new image, copy identical to the character.
  • The crew slows down (10% slower, longer dwell — desks, lounge, real conversations), stops settling on top of each other, and gets faces on their cards (each character's own front-facing sprite, not an initial in a circle).
  • Support crew stop pretending to be assignable: the “Create work” button disappears from Marni, Astrid, and Ben the CEO.
  • Ben the CEO stops teleporting. He was leaving floors from random tiles (including, once, the meeting table). He now walks to the door like a professional.
  • Marni gets a life: she colors at reception, makes Coke runs to the kitchen (region 38 — Ben painted the fridge tile himself), takes smoke breaks at the entrance, and waters the Front Office plants.
  • The Front Office gets its sunset. The metal crest in the entrance hall is replaced by a neon PULSE HQ sign (Ben picked concept #1 — cyan, matching his logo), and the back windows get Ben's own solution to fake-looking window art: make the panes transparent and put one panorama behind the building. Each window now shows a different slice of one unbroken evening horizon.
  • A board mix-up gets fixed with a laugh: the conspiracy board was in the client lounge and the product showcase belonged there — the conspiracy board moved to the Research lab's evidence wall, where it always belonged, and the Front Office whiteboard became the Product Showcase.
  • The briefing buttons get reversed so they tell the truth: Start hands a thread to Remmy; Send it to the crew (on Remmy's shaped summary) is the only press that dispatches work.
  • Hashtags become law: every finished post ends with its strongest topic hashtags — no filler — enforced by the server, which now rejects an “unfinished” post the way it already rejected an Olivia draft with no image or link.
  • The day-one landing page retires: the giant “Office Floor” header is replaced by a slim bar, and later the same night, reworked again after Ben's screenshot review — the floor now fills the screen and the music controls live in a popover. The old landing gets its honors here, in the record.
  • The main floor gets loved too: a “PROJECT ROADMAP — Q4” working whiteboard, and a properly-sized TV in the lounge alcove playing Die Hard, with the stereo gear on the bench — after a four-round size negotiation between the founder's eye and the generator's enthusiasm, settled by measuring the original TV and compositing to spec. The founder won. The founder was right.
  • Sven becomes a story hunter: two shelves above his ideas — Stories for Perfectly Paranoid, Stories for Pulse HQ — each card one tap from becoming a job. Plus his new beat, in Ben's words: “have Sven peruse declassified CIA and FBI files for potential stories. 5 files a day.” Documents are sources, never finished stories; Tom still checks everything against sources and labels what survives.
  • This dev log is commissioned, along with a showcase refresh and the case study, as a package: the site stops being a brochure and becomes evidence.
  • The hours are counted (see the top of this page). Ben's reaction is in the record: “holy fuck.” Then: “I've already worked tomorrow, today.” (His Oct 13 ended 23:56. His Oct 14 began 00:00.)

Day 9 — Thursday, October 15 — Launch day

The day the machine got a name, a price list, a public, and a paying customer.

  • Pulse HQ - AI Workspace becomes just Pulse HQ (“AI Workspace no longer applies”), and the founder coins the tagline on the spot: “Bring your online presence to life.” Public brand marks don't pin the location — local-first in practice, not in the headline.
  • The offers lock. The Founding Test Run: $199 upfront, 28 finished posts over 14 days, two businesses max — the founder rejected a five-post version as too expensive per post and set the volume himself. The standard pilot goes to $650 flat (the old $500–$750 range is dead). Both fees credit toward setup. Precision becomes law in the copy: “verified” means checked against sources with every claim labelled, not a guarantee; the founder is “Ben from Pulse HQ,” no last name, in anything public.
  • Customers will not watch the office work. Positioning ruling: proposals sell results and deliverables — posts, reports, turnaround — never the show. The visible office is a founder-only thing.
  • The showcase is restructured on the founder's call: a lean front page for deciding, the entire previous page preserved as a Showcase tab for investigating.
  • Pulse HQ goes public: launch posts on X and on Facebook, posted by Ben by hand (Meta's API remains parked; the office packages, the founder posts — that's the deal).
  • FIRST PAYING CLIENT, the same day: the Eagle Valley Seniors Meals Society — website, Facebook page setup, logo and branding, $50, charged as a deliberate name-in-town play. The society's chef is Marni. Yes, that Marni. The package was built the same evening, but the facts that matter — Sicamous Seniors Centre, meals Monday and Thursday at noon, $12 a person — were confirmed by the founder from the society before anything shipped, because the public record was stale (an old price, a temporary arrangement) and the Research Department caught it before it could embarrass anyone. The board head got the site preview — forwarded by Marni herself — ahead of the founder's first board meeting the next day.
  • The rehearsal doctrine proves itself. A fake client (Free Spirit Spheres — simulation only, never contacted) ran the full client journey first and caught three real defects: no photo-library intake, client jobs able to close without their report, hashtags missing inside standard jobs. All three fixed the same night. Ben: “We have the mental and physical tools to be ahead. Always ahead.”
  • Outcome contracts land. Every job type now carries a contract — bound when the job is created, re-stamped at every handoff, checked before anything may surface. Violations bounce with a plain reason, and a client job missing its report cannot be approved at three separate doors. The machinery caught its own mistakes twice more before the day was out.
  • Outreach goes live at scale. Twenty-five businesses prepared (the founder cherry-picked his hometown six to send personally); a second pack of 25 approved and submitted — 14 through, 11 blocked on details the crew refuses to invent; a third pack of 25 commissioned before bed. The first-contact rule, set by the founder: no money talk in a first message, ever — conversation first. Seven Revelstoke businesses got hand-delivered pitches from the founder; one replied warm the same day.
  • The client journey gets its ergonomics: an orb post goes from spotted-in-the-sky to posted in about 34 minutes, founder touching it twice (spot it, approve it).
  • Launch-night infrastructure, in ascending order of glamour: a proper business email (getpulsehq@proton.me — created after the crew was caught putting the founder's music-blog Gmail on outreach forms; the personal address is now banned from client-facing forms by standing rule); a business card, designed at 9:40 PM and bound for Staples in the morning, paid for out of the RPG Maker plugin-sales balance — the old empire funding the new one; and a Ready for You queue rebuilt into calm decision cards after the founder ruled the old one “very very busy” and glitchy.
  • Then, at 10 PM on launch day, the founder ordered the office to audit its own code — findings first, fix don't rebuild — his reasoning: catch it now, on the day new products and a customer landed, rather than find it once there's a clientele list counting on the place. The audit (16,252 lines) found real things: finished work that could be invisible to the approval queue (the society's website.html, hiding in plain sight), the whole business shipped to every screen every five seconds, the same operating rule maintained in up to five places. It also certified the strengths worth protecting — the approval gates and the three-door report guarantee. The founder wrote the five-phase fix plan himself, in sequence, no parallel workstreams. Then, from the hammock, he designed the next architecture: click a client, populate the world — one client's file in the pipeline at a time, which makes cross-client contamination structurally impossible and per-client inventory and billing simple. Official furniture policy was also set, and is recorded here for posterity: standing desk never. hammock desk always.

Day 10 — Friday, October 16 — Paid

The day the audit closed, the first client paid, and the second client walked in through the door the first one opened.

  • The big code audit closes. Findings first, fix don't rebuild: all five fix phases completed and verified, in the founder's sequence, no parallel workstreams. The office held.
  • Eagle Valley Seniors Meals Society: delivered and PAID. Website, Facebook page setup, logo and branding — job done, $50 in. First paying client, closed out clean on the second day of the engagement.
  • Client #2 lands the same day, out of the Eagle Valley board meeting: the Dementia & Alzheimer's Support Group (Sicamous), for a website and Facebook page. The website mockup was built and loved; the Facebook page kit is delivered. The name-in-town play paying its first dividend inside 24 hours.
  • Perfectly Paranoid is fully un-packaged. The ruling, made final: PP is house content, not a client delivery. Its posts are plain finished posts again — zero packages, zero ledger rows. The packaging machinery stays exactly as built for real clients; it just no longer wraps the company's own page.
  • Deliverables get rebuilt for humans. A plain kind dropdown instead of internal labels, packages that also live under their client (one obvious place per thing — the layman test, applied), and a ZIP download that rebuilds fresh on every click, so what lands on a client's machine is always what's current, never a stale snapshot.
  • The Thomas Dixon saga. An Atlanta landscaper arrived believing he'd bought “our” highest plan. He hadn't — he'd bought from a namesake company (Pulse CRM), no relation to us at all. Resolved honestly, no blame handed out where it didn't belong, and the door left open if he ever wants the real Pulse HQ.
  • Two outreach replies, both keepers. Reimers: a polite no — filed with thanks. Revy Outdoors: a not-now, with the door ajar. At this stage both are wins; the book records each exactly as it came in.

Day 11 — Saturday, October 17 — The real website

The day Pulse HQ stopped living only inside the office and got an address the whole internet can find.

  • New hero headline, the founder's pick, now leading the site: “Your whole online presence, handled — website, Facebook page, weekly posts, and the research behind them. Nothing goes public without your say.”
  • Selling-the-office language is struck from the site for good. We don't sell the actual office — clients get deliverables, full stop. The phrases came off every page they were standing on, under the founder's standing consent to apply his settled rulings site-wide.
  • The Outreach view is built into the office: 90 businesses, one honest book — every pitch, reply, and door-left-open in a single place instead of scattered packs.
  • Ten website mockups in one day, built for ten businesses that had no website at all — and six of them pitched the same day. Show, don't tell, at production speed.
  • The cherry-pick shelf opens. Not everybody needs the full service, so the pieces are priced on their own: website $450, Facebook page setup $200, logo & branding mini-kit $175, a batch of 14 posts $300, a research report $250.
  • THE BIG ONE: a real, Google-indexable website is live — pulsehq-ai.github.io/pulse-hq-website — generated from this very page. Which makes this page's job official: it's the test dummy now. Everything proves here first, then pushes live.
  • A door in the connector wall. A direct Facebook API route is opened through our own developer app, Pulse HQ Poster — the first plumbing toward posting that doesn't run through the founder's hands. The ghost consent error that parked Meta back on Day 5 remains Meta's problem; this route is ours.
  • The FAQ is rebuilt from research. The Research Department took the ten most-asked questions in this business, researched and verified the answers, and they now lead the FAQ — the founder's original twelve sit directly underneath, untouched.
  • Next phase approved — “make it so.” A contact form, art uploads (clients showing us their real material instead of us guessing at stand-ins), and payments by card, PayPal, and e-Transfer: planned together, building in phases.
  • A white tulip arrived, and with it a future venture's name. Logged, parked, not forgotten. This log records that it happened; the rest belongs to a later entry.
