The Dev Log

Pulse HQ — Dev Log

The public record of how Pulse HQ was built: the milestones, the decisions, and what the work taught us — written for anyone considering trusting us with their online presence.

At a glance: a public, paying business, built in the open. Three departments — Main Office, Research Department, Front Office. A first client signed on launch day and a second the day after. A real website, live. Every entry below is the short version of a longer internal record; the work itself is exactly as described.

Prologue — why this office exists

The spark was a concept video by creator Annikka Aborro: an AI office, named agents at desks. We wanted the thing itself — not a dashboard pretending to be a company, but an office that does real work and brings it to a human for one honest approval. Credit where due is a house rule from the start: what's invented gets labelled invented, what's sourced gets credited.

The office was built the way its founder builds everything — like a game: floors, roles, pipelines, regions on a map. Except this game's outputs post to real pages and its mistakes cost real credibility, which is why verification became this company's religion early (see Day 7).

The thesis, set on Day 8 and still the thesis: this isn't an AI assistant. It's an AI partner — a crew that does the work, and a human who approves it.

Day 1 — Wednesday, October 7 — The office opens

  • Pulse HQ (then just “the AI office”) is rebuilt as a live, database-backed workspace: a task board (Inbox → Working → Needs approval → Done), an office log, and a living floor where the crew visibly works.
  • The working pattern is set on day one: the crew works, a human approves. Everything since is that sentence getting stronger.
  • First end-to-end test: a print-on-demand store run, idea to listings — the office's first full pipeline.

Day 2 — Thursday, October 8 — Credit where due

  • The office is written up publicly, crediting the concept video that sparked it.
  • Perfectly Paranoid — the company's own long-running page, ~8.4K followers — gets its working flow: image, then copy with image, then explicit approval before anything posts.

Day 3 — Friday, October 9 — The floor becomes the interface

  • The office floor and a unified work panel replace the old dock-and-sidebar layout.
  • “Create work” lands: describe a job, the dispatcher divides it into the minimum crew steps, and the plan is visible before it runs.

Day 4 — Saturday, October 10 — The quiet day

  • A short day. The office kept the lights on.

Day 5 — Sunday, October 11 — Paranoid relaunches, Meta says no

  • Perfectly Paranoid's relaunch goes live — new look, new content engine; the name, as ever since 2014, stays Perfectly Paranoid.
  • Meta's API blocks Page posting with a consent error that offers no consent screen to resolve it on. The constraint is accepted and the workflow is rebuilt around it: the office drafts and packages; publishing runs through approved channels only.

Day 6 — Monday, October 12 — The doctrine, and the business

  • Research into the done-for-you market sets the company's focus: sell deliverables and approver control — social media management — not “AI agents.”
  • Pulse HQ is created on Facebook as the build-in-public home.
  • The operating doctrine is written down: “approver, not babysitter.” Standing orders run straight to Done. Manual jobs flow through the crew and surface once for Approve / Rework / Delete. The human touchpoints: create the job, one end approval, answer genuine blockers.
  • A print-on-demand side channel closes the same day. Noted, absorbed — it was never the business.

Day 7 — Tuesday, October 13 — Departments

The day the office became a company with departments.

  • The Research Department is built: Walter reads sources and extracts claims → Peter verifies each claim into one of four labels (DOCUMENTED / MAINSTREAM INTERPRETATION / FRINGE CLAIM / NOT VERIFIED) → Tom synthesizes the brief. Claims that fail go on a do-not-publish list. This labelled-claims method is now the spine of every research deliverable we sell.
  • Perfectly Paranoid's voice is locked: dark, cynical, dry. No hype. Institutions assumed self-interested or asleep. Every post carries a stakes beat — “Why is this a big deal?” — and the facts stay verified-only. The voice darkens framing, never upgrades a claim.
  • A social platform's API turns out to be paid-only mid-setup, so it's parked. The compliant replacement is a new hire: Olivia, who drafts posts into a manual Post Queue — image or link always, attraction-oriented copy, never diary-style.
  • The Front Office is decided: a third, client-facing department (client registry, delivery packages, reporting), run by Maria.
  • The layman test becomes UI law: our surfaces must make perfect sense to a non-technical reader. If a client has to ask where an approved thing went, the design is wrong.

Day 8 — Wednesday, October 14 — The Front Office opens

  • The Front Office opens — the third floor — with Marni at reception and Maria running the department.
  • Perfectly Paranoid becomes the proving ground: the company's own page runs through the full client machinery first — intake, research, writing, image, delivery package — before any outside business is put through it. Two posts run the pipeline end-to-end this day.
  • The escalation rule fires live, twice. Tricky claims get sent up to the Research Department mid-job and the parent job waits for the verdict. The claims that don't survive checking are do-not-publish, and the writer obeys the list.
  • Aspect-control rework: any single part of a deliverable — copy, image, or research — can be sent back with instructions that apply to that part only, everything else frozen. Proven the same day.
  • Hashtags become law: every finished post ends with its strongest topic hashtags, no filler — enforced by the system, which rejects an unfinished post the way it already rejects a draft with no image or link.
  • The showcase, the case study, and this dev log are commissioned as a package: the site stops being a brochure and becomes evidence.

Day 9 — Thursday, October 15 — Launch day

The day the machine got a name, a price list, a public, and a paying customer.

  • Pulse HQ gets its name and tagline: “Bring your online presence to life.” Public brand marks don't pin the location — local-first in practice, not in the headline.
  • The offers lock. The Founding Test Run: $199 upfront, 28 finished Facebook posts over 14 days, two businesses maximum. The standard Pilot: $650 flat. Setup $1,250 and Core $1,450/month sit behind them. Both entry fees credit toward setup. Precision becomes law in the copy: “verified” means checked against sources with every claim labelled — not a guarantee.
  • Customers will not watch the office work. Positioning ruling: proposals sell results and deliverables — posts, reports, turnaround — never the show.
  • First paying client, the same day: the Eagle Valley Seniors Meals Society — website, Facebook page setup, logo and branding, at a deliberate community rate. A name-in-town play, taken on purpose. The facts that matter — Sicamous Seniors Centre, meals Monday and Thursday at noon, $12 a person — were confirmed directly with the society before anything shipped, because the public record was stale and the Research Department caught it before it could embarrass anyone.
  • The rehearsal doctrine proves itself. A simulated client ran the full client journey first and caught three real defects: no photo-library intake, client jobs able to close without their report, hashtags missing inside standard jobs. All three fixed the same night.
  • Outcome contracts land. Every job type now carries a contract — bound when the job is created, re-stamped at every handoff, checked before anything may surface. A client job missing its report cannot be approved at three separate doors.
  • Outreach goes live. The first-contact rule, set this day and never broken since: no money talk in a first message, ever — conversation first. A standing rule is set alongside it: client-facing work uses only the business address.
  • Launch night closes with the office ordered to audit its own code — findings first, fix don't rebuild — on the reasoning that it's better caught on the day the first customer lands than after a clientele list is counting on the place. Official furniture policy was also set, and is recorded for posterity: standing desk never. hammock desk always.

Day 10 — Friday, October 16 — Delivered, paid, and a second client

The day the audit closed, the first client paid, and the second client walked in through the door the first one opened.

  • The big code audit closes. Findings first, fix don't rebuild: all five fix phases completed and verified, in sequence, no parallel workstreams. The office held.
  • Eagle Valley Seniors Meals Society: delivered and paid. Website, Facebook page setup, logo and branding — job done, closed out clean on the second day of the engagement.
  • Client #2 lands the same day, out of the Eagle Valley board meeting: the Dementia & Alzheimer's Support Group (Sicamous), for a website and Facebook presence. The website mockup was built and loved on sight.
  • Perfectly Paranoid is settled as house content. Not a client delivery, not packaged, not invoiced — its posts are plain finished posts through the same machinery. The packaging machinery stays exactly as built for real clients; it just no longer wraps the company's own page.
  • Deliverables get rebuilt for humans. Plain-language labels instead of internal ones, packages that also live under their client (one obvious place per thing — the layman test, applied), and delivery downloads that rebuild fresh on every click, so what lands on a client's machine is always what's current.
  • A case of mistaken identity — a business that had bought from a namesake company, no relation to us — is resolved honestly, no blame handed out where it didn't belong, and the door left open.
  • Two outreach replies, both keepers. A polite no, filed with thanks. A not-now, with the door ajar. At this stage both are wins; the book records each exactly as it came in.

Day 11 — Saturday, October 17 — The real website

The day Pulse HQ stopped living only inside the office and got an address the whole internet can find.

  • New hero headline, now leading the site: “Your whole online presence, handled — website, Facebook page, weekly posts, and the research behind them. Nothing goes public without your say.”
  • Selling-the-office language is struck from the site for good. We don't sell the office — clients get deliverables, full stop.
  • The Outreach view is built into the office: ninety businesses, one honest book — every pitch, reply, and door-left-open in a single place.
  • Ten website mockups in one day, built for ten businesses that had no website at all — and six of them pitched the same day. Show, don't tell, at production speed.
  • The singles shelf opens. Not everybody needs the full service, so the pieces are priced on their own: website $450, Facebook page setup $200, logo & branding mini-kit $175, a batch of 14 posts $300, a research report $250.
  • A real, Google-indexable website is live — pulsehq-ai.github.io/pulse-hq-website — generated from the in-office showcase. Everything proves in the office first, then pushes live.
  • The FAQ is rebuilt from research. The Research Department took the ten most-asked questions in this business, researched and verified the answers, and they now lead the FAQ.
  • Payments and intake move forward: card payments through a hosted checkout, PayPal, and e-Transfer, planned together and built in phases. The Founding Test Run stays apply-first — two spots, a personal reply, no buy button.
