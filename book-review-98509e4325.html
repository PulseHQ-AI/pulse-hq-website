I FEEL FINE

Surviving the End of the World as We Know It

Chapter 1 — Feed Yourself for Cheap

Forget the freeze-dried buckets in the ads. People have fed themselves cheaply for thousands of years, and the foods that do it best are still the cheapest things in the store. If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it this one: calories first, variety second, vitamins third. Hunger is the emergency; everything else is a problem for next month.

How much food is "enough"?

There is no single number, and any book that gives you one without asking who's eating is guessing with your safety. Needs vary — by body size, age, health, workload, and even climate. So treat everything in this section as rough planning guidance, not a calculated requirement:

  • An adult sitting around sheltering burns roughly 1,600–1,800 calories a day.
  • An adult doing physical work — chopping, carrying, walking — needs 2,200 to 2,500, and sometimes more.
  • Children need less than adults, but a child is not a small adult: growing bodies need protein and fat out of proportion to their size, and they run out of reserves faster.
  • Older adults often eat less overall but need protein more, not less — it's what keeps muscle and strength.

The honest way to plan: estimate each person in your household separately, using about 2,000 a day for an average adult as the middle peg, adjust up for big bodies and hard work, and then err on the high side. Being a little over is a comfort. Being under is a slow emergency. For that average adult, a month is roughly 60,000 calories that don't spoil, don't need a fridge, and mostly don't need much cooking. That's the peg this chapter builds on — your household's real number is yours to work out, and the checklist at the end walks you through it.

Start with two weeks, not a month

A month of food sounds like a mountain, so don't start there. The first goal is two weeks — two weeks of food your household actually eats, stored where you live. A household that can manage two weeks has already changed its risk: most real emergencies are measured in days, and two weeks covers nearly all of them. A month is the second goal. Longer than that is a project with real costs in money, space, and rotation effort, and this book won't pretend otherwise.

Before you buy anything, take inventory — it's free, and it's the step that makes every later step honest:

  1. Empty the cupboards onto the table, once. Count what you already have that keeps: rice, pasta, oats, tins, peanut butter, all of it.
  2. Work out roughly how many days of eating it is for your household — not for "a person."
  3. Write it down, with the date. That number — food on hand, people it covers, days it lasts — is your starting line. Everything you add from here extends it.

And count the whole household: children, grandparents, anyone with dietary or medical needs (allergies, diabetes, food that's easy to chew and cook), and pets — a month's plan that forgets the dog is not a month's plan. Remember too that cooking this food takes water — roughly an extra litre or two a day for the pot and the washing up. Chapter 2 does water properly; just don't plan food as if it cooks itself dry.

The big four

Rice. The backbone. Cheap, and dry white rice kept sealed and dry stores for years — brown rice doesn't (its natural oils go rancid in about six months, which is why this book always means white rice). One pound of dry rice holds about 1,650 calories. A cup of dry rice cooks up to about three cups — roughly 600 calories on the plate.

Lentils. The beginner's bean, and the most underrated food in this book. Unlike other beans they need no soaking and cook in 20–30 minutes, which matters enormously when fuel is short. About 1,600 calories a pound, and they're protein-rich — paired with rice across a day, the two together give you complete protein. Red lentils cook fastest of all and basically fall apart into soup, which is a feature.

Oats. About 1,750 calories a pound. Breakfast, soup thickener, filler that makes a thin meal feel like a meal. They don't even strictly need cooking — overnight soaking in water gives you edible oats by morning, no fuel at all.

Potatoes. The one staple you can grow almost anywhere with almost nothing. One of the most productive beginner crops for the ground it takes, and they carry vitamin C — the exact nutrient a rice-and-beans diet is short on. If you have any patch of ground, sunny corner, or even a few big buckets of soil, potatoes are your garden. Everything else you grow is a bonus.

Here's the whole system as a shopping list, built on the 2,000-a-day peg above. This basket adds up to roughly 67,000 calories — about 2,200 a day, which gives a bit of headroom — with protein around 70 grams a day. That protein figure is enough for many adults at ordinary activity; it is not enough for everyone. Large bodies, heavy physical work, pregnancy, illness, and teenagers all need more — the cheap fix is another bag of lentils, more peanut butter, or a few more tins of fish.

Two honest limits on this basket. First, it's one average adult for one month — scale it per person using the guidance above, and remember the two-week goal comes first. Second, where the budget allows, add real fruit and vegetables — tinned, dried, or frozen-while-the-power-lasts — because a month on staples alone keeps you fed, not thriving. The staples are the floor, not the ceiling.

Storing it so it's still food when you need it

Food dies four deaths: water, air, heat, and pests. Beat all four and the big four keep for years.

  • Sealed. Out of the store bag, into airtight containers — food-grade buckets with tight lids are the classic, and they're the right place for a beginner to start. For storage measured in years, mylar bags with oxygen absorber packets, sealed and then bucketed, are the step up — worth learning once the basics are running, not where you begin.
  • Cool, dry, dark. A closet floor beats a garage. Heat and damp are the enemy; a steady cool room temperature is fine.
  • Pest-proofing. Pantry moths and weevils can arrive as eggs in store-bought grains. A few days in a deep freezer before sealing kills most of those life stages and is worth doing — but it isn't sterilisation and it doesn't replace sealing properly.
  • Rotation. This is the step everyone skips and the only one that guarantees the system works in year three: eat the oldest first, and replace what you ate on your next shop. Your survival pantry should just be your kitchen, one layer deeper — food you actually eat, cycling.
  • Old beans deserve a qualified note: beans stored for years are generally still safe to eat, but they get harder and take much longer to soften — some never soften properly at all — so they cost you extra fuel and water when you cook them. Lentils barely have this problem. Another point for lentils.

Cooking when fuel is short

In a long emergency, fuel can be scarcer than food. Four techniques stretch it:

  1. Lentils first. No soak, half an hour, done. In any fuel crunch they jump the queue ahead of every other bean.
  2. Soak before you cook. Beans (and rice, briefly) soaked overnight cook noticeably faster. Water is doing work so your fuel doesn't have to.
  3. Retained-heat cooking. Bring the pot to a hard boil for five minutes, then take it off the heat and bury it in blankets, a sleeping bag, or a box of towels, lid on. The trapped heat finishes the cooking over the next hour or two with no further fuel. This old trick — the "haybox" — can save real fuel, but how much varies with the pot, the food, and the insulation, so don't plan around a promised figure — and practise it on an ordinary weekend first. A cooking method you have never tested is a hope, not a method.
  4. The thermos and the cold soak. Oats go into a thermos with hot water at night and are breakfast by morning. Oats and couscous-style grains can also be cold-soaked entirely. Not every meal needs fire.

Two pieces of equipment earn their shelf space here: a pot with a tight lid (the lid alone saves fuel on everything), and — if any of your stored food comes in tins — a manual can opener. An electric opener is a paperweight in an outage, and a tin you can't open is not food. And test the whole system once while life is normal: cook one full day of basket meals on the equipment and fuel you'd actually have with the power out. Whatever goes wrong in the rehearsal is a cheap lesson; in the real thing it isn't.

What a day on the basket actually looks like

  • Morning: oats with a spoonful of peanut butter and a pinch of sugar stirred in. Tastes like breakfast, costs almost nothing.
  • Midday: lentil soup — lentils, water, salt, a spoon of oil, and any onion, carrot, or garlic you have. Red lentils dissolve into it and make it thick.
  • Evening: rice with beans or lentils over it, oil drizzled on top, salt, and now and then the tinned fish for protein and variety. Hot sauce, if your pantry has it, is worth its shelf space a hundred times over.
  • From day three onward: a handful of sprouts (below) on the side — cooked, for the reason the next section explains.

Sprouting: the cheap vegetable, with honest warnings

Dry lentils and beans are seeds, and seeds want to grow. Put two tablespoons of lentils or mung beans in a jar, cover the mouth with cloth or mesh, soak 8–12 hours, drain. After that, rinse and drain twice a day. In 2–4 days you have fresh sprouts — living food with some vitamin C, grown on a counter, in winter, in the dark, out of food you already had stored. In a long emergency this can genuinely help round out a diet of dry staples.

Now the warning this section owes you, plainly: raw sprouts are one of the riskiest fresh foods there is. The warm, damp conditions that grow a sprout also grow Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria, and rinsing does not reliably remove them. For children, older adults, pregnant people, and anyone with a weakened immune system, raw sprouts are a bad bet even in ordinary times. So this book's rule is: cook your sprouts. Stirred into soup or rice for the last minute or two, they keep most of what makes them worth growing and lose the risk that makes them dangerous. If you do eat them raw, use seed sold for sprouting, rinse faithfully, and treat the discard rule as law: if sprouts ever smell sour or feel slimy, throw them out and start again. Seeds are cheap; food poisoning is not.

Foraging: the honest rules

Free food grows everywhere, and so do poisonous look-alikes. The rules are short and absolute:

  1. Never eat a plant or mushroom you cannot identify with certainty. Not "pretty sure." Certainty. More foragers are hurt by confidence than by hunger.
  2. There is no taste test. You will sometimes hear that you can test an unknown plant by eating a small amount and waiting. You cannot. A small amount of the wrong plant can still poison you, and a plant you cannot name is not made safer by eating less of it. If it isn't identified with certainty, it isn't food.
  3. Learn three plants perfectly instead of thirty vaguely. The beginner's three: dandelion (the whole plant is edible, and it has no dangerous look-alike in the northern woods), plantain the weed (broad leaves in every lawn — not the banana relative), and stinging nettle, cooked — gloves to pick, and cooking removes the sting completely. That's the list. Clover is not on it: it has look-alikes and risks of its own and belongs to a later stage. Cattail belongs to the next level too, and only ever from clean water — Chapter 10 gives it the full treatment.
  4. Mushrooms are the advanced class. Skip them entirely until someone who truly knows teaches you in person, in your area.
  5. Contamination changes everything. After fallout or a chemical spill (Chapter 16), wild food rules are replaced by contamination rules — what's on the food matters more than what the food is.

The seven mistakes

  1. Buying food you don't eat. If you hate lentils in normal life, you'll hate them more in a crisis. Store what you'll actually cook on a tired Tuesday, because that's who'll be cooking it.
  2. All starch, no fat. A belly full of plain rice still feels like hunger. Fat is satiety and calories both — that's why the oil and peanut butter are in the basket.
  3. Forgetting salt. Food without it becomes a chore by day four, and your body needs it anyway. It costs almost nothing. Buy two bags.
  4. Brown rice for the deep shelf. Healthier today, rancid in six months. White rice is the storage rice.
  5. Leaving food in the bags it came in. Mice, moisture, and moths all send their thanks. Seal it.
  6. A freezer full of "plan." Frozen food without power is a 48-hour problem (Chapter 7), not a food supply. The shelf is the plan; the freezer is a bonus.
  7. Buying it once and never touching it. Unrotated emergency food is a donation to the garbage, five years from now. Eat the oldest, replace it, and the system never expires.

The smallest person eats differently

The basket above feeds an average adult. A household with a baby contains a person it cannot feed — someone who eats one thing, on a schedule they do not control, prepared to a standard the rest of the shelf never has to meet. The baby's supplies are not a subsection of the grocery list. They are a separate system, and this chapter prices it like one.

If the baby is breastfed, the system depends on the mother being supported and fed. Breastfeeding is a particularly reliable way to feed an infant during an emergency because it does not depend on a supply of formula, safe mixing water, or electricity for preparation. The plan's job is to keep the mother safe, hydrated, fed, and supported, and to protect her ability to breastfeed. She should drink to thirst and have reliable access to food and water; do not turn an assumed extra water allowance into a fixed medical rule. If breastfeeding is difficult, interrupted, or not possible, get help from a health professional or lactation support service as soon as practical.

If the baby is formula-fed, learn the hierarchy the CDC teaches, because in an emergency the order matters. Ready-to-feed formula is the safest option when formula is needed and safe water or clean preparation equipment may be limited. It is sterile before opening, already mixed, and needs no added water. It matters especially for babies under two months old, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system. Powdered formula is not sterile, and it requires safe water and clean feeding equipment. If powder is what you have: use bottled water or water made safe by Chapter 2's methods, follow the exact instructions on the container, measure water and powder accurately, and clean feeding equipment with safe water. For higher-risk babies, CDC guidance includes additional precautions for preparing powdered formula; follow those instructions and the baby's clinician's advice. Two rules have no exceptions — never add extra water to make it last, and never make homemade formula. Diluted formula may fail to provide the nutrition a baby needs and can cause dangerous electrolyte problems. Prepared formula is used within two hours of preparation and within one hour after feeding begins. Anything left in the bottle after a feed is thrown away.

Now the quantities, because "some formula" is not a plan. There is no single daily amount that fits every baby. Intake changes with age, weight, growth, and whether the baby also receives breast milk. Use the baby's normal daily intake as the starting point, record it before an emergency, and check the amount with the baby's health professional. For a rough shelf calculation, a baby drinking about a quart a day would need about three quarts for 72 hours and fourteen quarts for two weeks — but that is an example, not a universal ration. Ready-to-feed containers come in different sizes, so calculate from the actual label and the baby's usual intake. Price that honestly against the tin of powder, and then price the powder honestly too: every prepared ounce requires safe water, and bottles, nipples, and other feeding equipment require safe cleaning. Disposable cups can be useful when safe washing water is unavailable; the CDC includes them as an emergency option when bottles and nipples cannot be cleaned safely. Use a new cup for each feeding.

Diapers are the other arithmetic. A newborn may use around ten a day; an older baby may use fewer. Actual use varies by age and child. Count one normal day at your house and multiply by the days you are planning for. Ten a day is thirty for the 72 hours of Chapter 3 and a hundred and forty for two weeks — they are bulky, they are not optional, and running out creates a sanitation problem (Chapter 9) wearing a small disguise. Wipes by the same count, with extra allowance for messy days; the CDC's emergency list recommends at least two packs. Add rash cream, sealable bags for dirty diapers and clothes, and a change of clothes for the baby and one for whoever is holding the baby. Finish the kit with the small medical shelf: infant acetaminophen only if appropriate for the baby's age and situation, with the correct product concentration and weight-based instructions confirmed in advance by the baby's clinician. Do not give medicine to a young infant without checking first; a fever in a baby under three months needs prompt medical assessment. Add a safe sleep space: a firm, flat, separate surface designed for infant sleep, with the baby placed on their back. An adult's chest on a sofa is not a safe substitute. Babies also travel differently: a properly fitted carrier or sling may keep hands free, but it must be used according to its instructions, with the baby's face visible and airway clear. It is useful equipment, not a guarantee that a baby will stay calm in a crowd, stairwell, or shelter line.

Chapter 2 — Water: The Real First Emergency

Chapter 1 was about food because food is what people panic about. But food is not the emergency. You can go roughly three weeks without food and live. Without water, the old rule of thumb says three days — but treat that as a warning, not a window you are safe inside: in heat, hard work, or illness, and for children and the elderly, serious trouble can arrive well inside the first day. The three weeks for food is the same kind of number — a rough outer limit for a healthy adult at rest, not a schedule to plan against. Water is the first emergency, the second emergency, and the thing half the other chapters quietly depend on: you cannot cook the rice, flush the toilet, wash the wound, or keep the disease chapter at bay without it.

How much you actually need

Plan on 4 litres per person, per day, for drinking, cooking, and basic hygiene. That number has an honest pedigree. The American planning figure is one US gallon per person per day (FEMA and the Red Cross); a US gallon is really 3.785 litres, so treat this book's 4 litres as what it is — a rounded planning figure, rounded up, which is the safe direction to round. Canada's preparedness guidance lands in the same place, broken down as two litres for drinking and two more for cooking and hygiene. Drinking alone runs 2–3 litres a day for an adult in mild weather; heat, work, illness, pregnancy, and nursing all push it up, and children and the elderly dehydrate faster than everyone else.

Three days is the floor every government plans around. The real target is two weeks. At 4 litres a day, two weeks for one adult is about 56 litres — less space than you think: it's roughly one big storage tote. A household of four runs 16 litres a day, about 220 litres for two weeks, which is why this chapter is also about finding water, not just storing it.

The water already in your house

Before you buy a single bottle, know what's already stored in your walls:

  • The hot water tank. A typical tank holds somewhere around 150–225 litres (40–60 US gallons) of drinking water, sitting there right now — but typical is doing real work in that sentence, because tanks vary. So learn yours now, as a preparation exercise, on an ordinary day: find the drain valve at the bottom, the intake valve, and the breaker or gas valve that feeds it, and read the manufacturer's draining instructions while everything still works. If the supply is ever cut or suspect: turn off the power or gas first (the water inside can scald, and heating an emptied tank damages it), close the intake valve so dirty water can't back in, then draw from the drain valve, opening a hot tap somewhere upstairs to let it flow. Two cautions: this is the storage tank — it is not a heating boiler, and you should never drain anything you can't positively identify; and if the supply was chemically contaminated, assume the tank's contents are too. Done right, this one source can carry a household for days.
  • The pipes. Open the highest tap in the house, then drain from the lowest one. You'll get a few more litres.
  • Toilet tanks. The tank, never the bowl — several litres per tank. Treat this water as questionable and run it through the treatment steps below before drinking it, and skip it entirely if the tank has chemical cleaner pucks or blue tablets in it.
  • The freezer and pantry. Ice cubes, the juice from canned fruit and vegetables, any liquid foods — all drinkable.
  • Not these: swimming pools and hot tubs (fine for washing and flushing, not for drinking — the chemical load is the problem), water beds (chemical treatments), radiators (chemicals again), and anything with a chemical smell or a rainbow sheen. No home treatment in this chapter makes chemically contaminated water safe. That's a hard line, and it's there because it's true.

Storing water so it stays water

  • Use food-grade containers, prepared the CDC way: wash with soap and rinse completely, then sanitise with a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach — the label should say 5–9% sodium hypochlorite — in about a litre (one quart) of water. Cap the container, shake it so the solution wets every inside surface, wait at least 30 seconds, pour it out, and let the container air-dry before you fill it. That solution is for the container; pour it out, never drink it. Fill to the container's own fill line if it has one — not every container wants to be filled to the brim.
  • Ordinary tap water from a treated municipal supply needs nothing added — it's already disinfected. Fill, seal, store.
  • Cool, dark, off the floor — same rules as food. Label every container with the date.
  • Rotate every six months — as maintenance, not because a safety deadline expires. Properly stored water doesn't turn dangerous at month seven; it goes flat and stale, and the rotation is how you inspect the containers, catch the slow leak, and keep the habit alive. It's Chapter 1's food rule with a date on it — there, rotation runs continuously, the oldest eaten and replaced as you go; here, the same habit is pinned to six months. The emergency supply is just your household, one layer deeper.
  • Don't store water in old milk jugs — the plastic degrades and the seams leak, usually at 3 a.m., months later. Proper water containers are one of the few genuinely worth-buying items in this book.

Making questionable water safe

Everything you collect — rain, stream, lake, a neighbour's well you know nothing about — gets treated before it goes in a person. But read the limits first, because they outrank everything after them.

The hard limits. Boiling, bleach, and the filters in this chapter deal with germs. None of them removes chemicals, fuel, heavy metals, or salt — and none of them removes radioactive contamination either; fallout follows its own rules (Chapter 16), and "I treated it" is not one of them. If officials issue a Do Not Drink or Do Not Use advisory, that advisory outranks every method below: use bottled or known-safe water until it's lifted. Boiling chemically contaminated water can actually concentrate what's in it. Germs are the usual problem, so the methods matter — just never pretend they fix everything.

Disinfection: boiling and bleach

Boiling (the gold standard). Bring it to a full rolling boil for one minute — and if you're above about 6,500 feet (2,000 metres), three minutes: that's the CDC's figure, and water boils cooler up high. (Canadian guidance publishes two minutes at altitude; the full rolling boil is the point either way.) Let it cool, covered. Done. It tastes flat afterwards; pouring it back and forth between two clean containers puts air back and fixes most of that. Boiling is also the one method with no dose to get wrong, which is why it stays first choice whenever you have fuel.

Bleach (the no-boil option). Use regular, unscented household bleach — and check the label: the dose below is for bleach containing 5–9% sodium hypochlorite, with nothing scented or splash-less added. If your bottle's percentage falls outside that range, use the disinfection dilution printed on its own label instead of the dose below. Use a fresh bottle, too: bleach loses strength as it ages, so replace your emergency bottle about once a year whether you've opened it or not. The published emergency dose:

  • Clear water: 8 drops (about ⅛ teaspoon) per gallon / 4 litres.
  • Cloudy water: double it — 16 drops.
  • Stir, then wait 30 minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine. Treat that smell as a check, not a guarantee: if there's no chlorine smell at all, dose once more and wait 15 minutes more. If it smells strongly, let it stand a while or pour it between two clean containers — the taste fades. Precision you can't measure isn't precision; that's another reason boiling ranks first.

Filtration: what a filter does and doesn't do

Filters are not one thing, and "it has a filter" tells you almost nothing — the rating on the label is the whole story. A filter is only as good as the contaminants it is certified to remove.

  • What the good ones do: a camping filter rated for cysts — look for NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 on the spec sheet, or an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller — removes bacteria and the protozoa: Giardia, and the stubborn one, Cryptosporidium. Crypto matters because chlorine barely touches it: bleach alone is not reliable against it. Boiling kills it outright, and a cyst-rated filter removes it. Against those two, it's covered; against bleach alone, it isn't.
  • What most don't do: a standard portable filter does not remove viruses, and does not remove chemicals, unless it is specifically built and rated to — check, don't assume.
  • How they stack: filter first to clear the cloudiness and take out the protozoa, then bleach for the bacteria-and-virus side — belt and suspenders is exactly right for water you'll give a child.

Found water — what now?

A frightened beginner should be able to answer one question immediately: I've found water. What now? Walk it in order:

  1. Is it under a Do Not Drink / Do Not Use advisory, or chemically suspect — chemical smell, rainbow sheen, near industry or heavy runoff? → Do not drink it. No home method in this chapter fixes it. Find another source; use bottled or known-safe water meanwhile.
  2. Let it settle, then strain it through a clean cloth. Every later step works better on clear water.
  3. Got fuel? Boil it (one minute; three above 2,000 metres). Done — this is the safest endpoint in the chapter.
  4. No fuel: run it through a cyst-rated filter, then add the bleach dose and wait the 30 minutes.
  5. No filter either: bleach still kills bacteria and most viruses, but not Cryptosporidium — so if there is any way to boil, boil. If you truly can't, bleach, wait, and know exactly which gap you're accepting.

Finding water outside

  • Rain is the cleanest free source most of us get. Collect it off a roof into clean containers — roof water is for washing and flushing unless you treat it like wild water (roofs collect bird droppings and grit; after the first minutes of a downpour have rinsed the roof, it's better, but treat it anyway).
  • Streams and lakes: moving water over stagnant, upstream over downstream — but moving is a preference, not a promise: a clear, fast stream faithfully carries whatever is upstream of it. Treat all of it, always. "It looks clean" is how giardia introduces itself.
  • Snow and ice: melt it first. Eating snow costs your body the heat it takes to melt it — in a cold emergency that's spending warmth you can't afford. Melt, then treat like wild water if it's ground snow.
  • Avoid water near industrial sites, farm runoff, roadsides with a sheen, and anything downstream of a disaster area if you have any other choice.
  • Dew and condensation tricks exist (plastic sheeting over a hole, cloth on grass at dawn) and yield sips, not litres. Know they exist; don't plan around them.

Spending water like it matters

  • Flush with grey water. Water from washing can flush a toilet manually — pour it in the bowl or fill the tank. Every flush spends several litres — around five in a modern toilet, thirteen or more in an older one — several times a day.
  • Hands before everything. The cheapest disease prevention in this whole book is handwashing with a little water and soap at the moments that matter: before food, after the toilet. Chapter 9 exists because this fails.
  • Cook once, use twice. Rice and pasta water goes into the soup. Chapter 1's one-pot meals were quietly water policy too.
  • Sponge baths, not showers. A litre and a basin keeps a person clean enough for health. Dignity counts, and a washcloth bath covers it.
  • Catch your leaks. In a grid failure with freezing weather, a burst pipe can drain your whole stored supply and ruin the house — know where your main shutoff is now, on an ordinary day, and how to drain the pipes if the heat dies. Chapter 7 comes back to this.

Chapter 3 — The First 72 Hours: The Order of Operations

Every disaster in this book — storm, blackout, earthquake, the dramatic ones further in — starts with a gap between the event and organised help reaching you. In that gap, you are part of the response. Governments increasingly recommend that households be prepared to look after themselves for at least 72 hours. US guidance has used that minimum for years — FEMA and ready.gov both build on it — and Canada's guidance does too. The European Union's household guidance also recommends 72 hours, and Finland has built national preparedness guidance around the same period. Three days of looking after yourselves is not a prepper fantasy anymore; it's a practical minimum. This chapter is the order to do it in.

The order matters more than the gear. People get hurt in the first hours of emergencies doing third things first — driving out for supplies when the water was already in the house, lighting the whole place with candles when a neighbour was bleeding quietly in the next room. So here is the sequence, and the reasoning, in plain order.

First: people, and stop the house hurting anyone

Account for your people. Who is in the house, who isn't, who is hurt. If someone has life-threatening bleeding, apply firm direct pressure and get emergency help. Severe bleeding needs immediate attention, but it is not a reason to ignore an immediate danger such as fire, structural collapse or a gas leak. Deal with the threat that can kill someone right now, then work through the next priorities. Chapter 14 covers the details; the point here is to recognise an emergency and act.

Make the house stop being a hazard. If you smell gas, get everyone out immediately. Don't operate switches, use flames or do anything that could create a spark. Call 911 and the gas utility from a safe location. Don't re-enter until the appropriate authority says it is safe. If water is pouring from a burst pipe, use the main shutoff you found in Chapter 2, but only if you can reach it safely. Never approach an electrical panel through standing water.

If the building has been damaged, don't try to decide whether it is safe by looking at cracks in a wall. A shifted foundation, sagging roof, major structural damage or movement after an earthquake can mean the building is unsafe. Leave if there is a serious concern, and don't re-enter until it has been assessed by qualified people. The disaster isn't over just because the noise stopped, and the decision in Chapter 4 may already have been made for you.

One warning about your own head, early, because it belongs here: fear can make the first-hour mistakes. It can speed you up exactly when the situation rewards going slowly and in order. Chapter 22 is the whole chapter on that. The one-line version, for now: fear is information, not orders.

Second: water and warmth, the two real clocks

By now you know the priorities from Chapters 1 and 2: water, warmth, shelter and food all matter, but there is no universal countdown for how long a person can survive without them. Cold exposure can become dangerous quickly, depending on temperature, wind, wet clothing, shelter and a person's condition. Dehydration also varies with heat, exertion, illness and fluid loss. Don't use a rule of three as a safety clock.

Water: use the household supply you know is safe first — stored water and other sources described in Chapter 2. Follow local advisories. A flood or earthquake does not automatically make every tap-water supply unsafe, but damage, flooding or a loss of pressure can contaminate some systems. If the water looks, smells or tastes unusual, or you have reason to suspect contamination, don't drink it until you have checked official instructions. Clear water is not proof that it is safe.

Warmth or cool: in cold weather, one warm room, everybody in it, doors closed and gaps reduced, can be easier to keep warm than a whole house. In heat, use shade, reduce exertion, improve airflow when outdoor air is safe, and drink safe water. Chapter 8 is the deep version; in the first hours, get everyone into the safest temperature conditions you can manage and keep checking on them.

And the blackout rule that never stops being true: generators, camp stoves, and charcoal stay outside, always. Never operate them in a house, garage, shed, tent or other enclosed or partly enclosed space. Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless and can kill. Place generators at least 6 metres (20 feet) from buildings, with exhaust directed away from doors, windows and other openings. Use working carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup.

Third: information — find out what actually happened

A disaster feels enormous from inside it. The radio can help shrink it to size, provided you have power, a usable signal and a station still broadcasting.

Battery or crank radio on, official broadcasts first. What happened, how wide it is, what's being told to people in your area, and where aid is available. Chapter 6 is the full communications chapter. The radio is another route to information, not a guarantee that every report is correct.

Text, don't call, when it makes sense. Text messages may get through when voice calls cannot, because they can use less network capacity. This is not guaranteed. Don't delay an emergency call when someone needs urgent help. Keep messages short, reduce unnecessary phone use, and save battery for essential communication and alerts.

One out-of-area contact. Everyone in the household can check in with the same person outside the affected zone, if networks allow. That person can pass messages between household members. Local networks may be disrupted, and an out-of-area call or message is not guaranteed to get through.

Fourth: neighbours, before you need them

Somewhere in the first day — not necessarily the first hour — check on the people near you, if it is safe to do so. Who lives alone? Who may need help getting around? Who has a medical device or medication that depends on electricity or refrigeration? Who has useful skills or equipment and is willing to help?

Ask rather than assume what people need or can offer. Respect their privacy; medical details and personal circumstances are theirs to share. Emergencies are survived by streets, not just by households, and the time to learn your street's shape is while people can still talk calmly. Chapter 19 is built on this. A few conversations now can make a real difference later.

Fifth, on purpose: food

Food comes fifth because, for many people, it can wait while immediate threats are dealt with. That does not mean everyone can safely miss meals: infants, people with certain medical conditions, and anyone who depends on regular food or medication may need attention sooner.

Chapter 1's pantry is there to use. Eat perishable food safely and in the right order. An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about four hours without power. A full freezer may hold its temperature for about 48 hours, and a half-full one about 24 hours, if the doors stay closed. These are estimates, not guarantees. Use a thermometer if you have one. Perishable food that has been above 4°C for more than two hours should be discarded. Never taste food to decide whether it is safe.

Eat plainly, and don't start rationing without a reason. Keep track of what you have, how much people need and how long it is likely to last. Calm planning is better than panic, but don't mistake an ordinary adult missing one meal for a rule that applies to everyone.

The bag, and the stay

You need two different preparations, and confusing them wastes money.

The go-bag is for leaving quickly when it is safe and necessary to evacuate: water, medications, phone charger and battery bank, light, radio, essential documents or secure copies, cash in small bills, a warm layer, and the out-of-area contact written on paper. Include glasses, hearing aids, mobility or medical equipment needs, and supplies for children and pets. Keep it somewhere accessible near an exit you can actually use.

The stay kit is everything Chapters 1 and 2 built — water storage, the pantry, sanitation supplies, and suitable water-treatment equipment. It matters when staying is safe. The bag is for leaving; the house is where you may be able to shelter. An evacuation order means leave. Don't treat the stay kit as a reason to ignore one. Chapter 23 compresses the starting preparations into one shopping trip.

Days two and three: settle

If the event is still going on day two, the emergency becomes a routine: water use is monitored, people stay in the safest available temperature conditions, and the radio is checked at set times rather than left on all day. Batteries are a supply too. Keep an inventory of what you have and how long it may last. Revisit the stay-or-go decision using reliable information and current conditions, not fear or wishful thinking.

Check on people who may be alone, medically vulnerable or unable to get what they need. Write things down — what you used, what you promised a neighbour, what official instructions said and when you heard them. Memory is the first supply that runs out.

Chapter 4 — Stay or Go

Chapter 3 kept deferring one decision, and here it is. Staying home is often the sensible starting plan: your water, food, tools, and neighbours may already be there. But sometimes home itself becomes the dangerous place. The mistake is almost never simply staying or going — it's deciding late, or deciding on rumour. This chapter is how to decide early, on evidence, and then commit.

First rule: the official word outranks your guess

If authorities order your area evacuated, leave immediately. They may have information about the flood, fire, spill or other hazard that you cannot see from your street. Don't wait for conditions to look dangerous before following an evacuation order.

And the rule cuts both ways: if officials tell your area to shelter in place — which may be the instruction during a chemical spill or radiological emergency — then stay inside and follow their instructions. The correct response depends on the substance, the wind, the plume and the building. Don't assume every chemical incident calls for evacuation, or that every one calls for sheltering indoors. Running from an invisible hazard can mean running through it.

The exception is an immediate danger already inside your house: fire, a building coming apart, or water rising through the floor. Then you don't wait for permission. Get out if you can do so safely, and sort it out from somewhere safe.

What forces you out

Fire — in your home, or close enough that authorities advise evacuation or conditions make remaining unsafe. With wildfire, leave early when evacuation is advised or the fire threatens your route. Roads can become congested or close, and waiting can leave you with fewer safe options.

Flood water entering the house, or rising around it with more expected. Never walk or drive through floodwater.

A damaged building — suspected gas leak, structural movement, serious damage or a roof that may fail. Leave if the building may be unsafe; don't try to certify it by looking at cracks.

A chemical spill with an official evacuation order for your area. Follow the specific instructions issued for that incident.

Deep cold with no heat and no safe way to keep people warm (Chapter 8), especially with infants, older adults or people with medical needs.

No water, and no safe source available. This is usually a developing problem, not an automatic first-day evacuation trigger. Consider your household's needs, the likely duration, available help and whether a safer destination is actually reachable.

What punishes leaving

Fallout. After a nuclear detonation, get inside a substantial building as quickly as possible and stay sheltered for at least 24 hours, or longer if officials advise it. The 24-hour figure is not an automatic signal that it is safe to leave. Follow official instructions, and don't leave shelter unless there is an immediate danger inside the building or another urgent reason to move. Chapter 16 explains time, distance and shielding.

A storm in progress. The dangerous hours are now; roads, winds, debris, downed lines and flooding can make travel more dangerous than sheltering where you are. Follow local warnings.

Blizzard and whiteout. A house may provide better shelter than a vehicle, but that depends on the building, temperature, heating and immediate hazards. A stranded vehicle can become dangerous; so can a house without safe heat. Don't assume either is always the safer choice.

Rumour of unrest, with no verified information. Chapter 22 covers how bad information spreads in a crisis. Leaving a sound home for an unconfirmed scare can be a bad trade. Don't delay immediate safety action to investigate a rumour, but don't make a major move on unverified claims when you have time to check.

Nowhere better to go. "Away" is not a destination. A car is not automatically safer than a house, and the open is rarely a sensible destination. Think about where you can actually reach and shelter safely.

The deciding questions

Ask them in order, out loud, with the radio on (Chapter 6):

What is the danger, and where is it moving? Not "is it bad" — which way is it going, and are we in the path?

How much time do we really have? If an official order says leave now, don't spend time debating. If danger is immediate, act. If there is time, use it to make a safer decision.

Where exactly would we go? A named place, with a named person expecting you if you can manage it. If you can't name a destination, work out the safest available option before leaving, unless staying itself is immediately dangerous.

Can everyone actually make the trip? Mobility, medication, infants, pets, fuel and transport. The plan has to fit the household you have, not the one in a movie.

What does each hour of delay cost? Leaving early can preserve options. Waiting may mean losing the route, the transport or the time needed to help someone leave safely.

Which adds up to the chapter's rule: leave early when the evidence or authorities tell you to, or commit to staying while it remains safe. The dangerous option is the late, panicked middle — roads jammed, fuel gone, the hazard arriving while you're between shelters.

If you go

Two routes, known in advance. Know your main road and an alternative. Where possible, avoid relying on the same bridges, low crossings or other vulnerable points. Check current road closures and official directions before choosing a route, if time allows.

Take the go-bag (Chapter 3), medications, essential documents or secure copies, cash in small bills, phone charger and battery bank. Include the needs of children, pets and anyone who uses mobility or medical equipment.

Fuel is a useful habit: a fuller tank gives you more options. Keep it at least half full when you can afford to. Don't make a fuel stop if doing so would delay an evacuation or take you toward danger.

Never drive or walk through floodwater. Even shallow moving water can knock a person off their feet. Around 15 cm (6 inches) of fast-moving water can do so; around 30 cm (12 inches) can float many small cars; and around 60 cm (2 feet) can carry away many vehicles. These are illustrations of the danger, not safe thresholds. Water hides the road beneath it — or the absence of road. Turn around; find another route or follow official advice.

Leaving the house: don't delay departure to shut off utilities. Follow local instructions. Don't make shutting off natural gas part of your evacuation routine — that is the utilities' standing advice in much of the US, and in British Columbia too. The utility or a qualified person must handle turning gas back on after it has been shut off. If you suspect a gas leak, leave immediately without operating switches or creating sparks, and call from a safe place. Shut off water or electricity only if it is safe, accessible and appropriate to the situation. Never approach an electrical panel through water. Lock up only if doing so does not delay departure. Tell your out-of-area contact where you're going when you can. A note for responders may say that the household has evacuated, but don't put your destination, phone number or personal details where anyone can read them.

The vehicle is a system, not a taxi

"If you go" above treats the car as the way out. This section treats it as what it actually is in a bad week: a machine with failure modes, a shelter with rules, and a supply store on wheels — if it was stocked before it was needed. A vehicle earns its place in the plan the same way the shelf does, by being maintained on ordinary days.

The kit that lives in it, permanently: jumper cables long enough to reach the battery terminals safely, or a jump pack — which joins the rotation list, because a jump pack that has not been charged and tested in a year may be useless when needed. Follow the manufacturer's charging and storage instructions. A spare tire that is actually inflated, a jack, and the lug wrench, plus one practice session in the driveway on a calm afternoon: the side of a highway in rain is the worst classroom on earth, and many people learn there that their spare is flat and their jack is a rumour. Some vehicles have no spare, or have a temporary spare with specific speed and distance limits; know which you have. A tire gauge. Work gloves, a flashlight, basic tools. A tow strap only if you know its rated capacity, attachment points, and safe use — an unsuitable strap or attachment can fail dangerously. Whatever your jurisdiction wants you to carry by way of warning triangles or flares. And the fluids are checked the way Chapter 13 audits the cabinet: oil, coolant, washer fluid, on the maintenance schedule and according to the owner's manual, not only when a warning light appears.

The winter layer goes in before the first freeze and comes out after the last one: a blanket or sleeping bag for every seat, a small shovel, a bag of sand or suitable traction material for use under a spinning tire, a scraper and brush, extra warm clothes, water in containers that can tolerate freezing, and food that can be stored safely in the vehicle. A plastic bottle can split when its contents freeze, so choose containers accordingly and check them. Fuel follows this book's standing habit: keep the tank above half where practical, because the evacuation queue, the detour, and the closed station all spend the same gasoline. Maintenance is the unglamorous core of the whole section: cold reduces a battery's available cranking power, but age alone does not tell you exactly when a battery will fail. Have it tested as it ages and replace it when testing or the manufacturer's guidance indicates. Tire pressure falls as temperatures drop; check it cold and inflate to the vehicle manufacturer's specified pressure. Belts, hoses, fluids, tires, and lights belong on the regular maintenance schedule. The cheapest breakdown is the one prevented in the driveway in October.

If the car becomes the shelter — breakdown, blizzard, a road that simply stops — the rules are strict because the failure mode is invisible. Stay with the vehicle when it is safe to do so; it is shelter, and rescuers can search for it. If you have phone service, call 911 or the local emergency number and give your location. Run the engine periodically for heat. The National Weather Service recommends about ten minutes each hour in a winter storm, but conditions, vehicle, fuel, and temperature matter; use the least engine time needed to stay warm. Before every run, check that the tailpipe is clear of snow and check it again if snow is blowing or drifting. A blocked exhaust can allow carbon monoxide to enter the vehicle. Crack a window slightly while the engine runs, preferably on the side sheltered from the wind. If anyone develops headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, or confusion, get everyone into fresh air immediately and call 911; carbon monoxide poisoning is an emergency. Do not wait to see whether the symptoms pass.

Make the vehicle visible to rescuers: turn on the hazard lights when appropriate, tie a bright cloth to the antenna or another visible point, and turn on the dome light at night while the engine is running. After the snow stops, raising the hood can signal that you need help, if doing so is safe. And the rule with no exceptions: never run the engine in a garage, attached or not, door open or not, "just for a minute" or not. Carbon monoxide can build to deadly levels without warning.

If you stay

Commit fully — half-staying is just going, later and worse.

Fill what you safely can while the water runs. Store drinking water only when the supply is considered safe and there is no Do Not Use advisory. Follow local instructions if there is a boil-water or other water advisory. Fill suitable containers; don't assume a bathtub is clean or that every source is safe.

Secure the outside: bring in or tie down anything the wind could throw, but don't go outside if conditions make that unsafe.

Pick your room. Storm: an interior room away from windows. Cold: the safest warm room of Chapter 8. Chemical spill: follow the incident-specific shelter instructions. Fallout: Chapter 16's rules, exactly.

Run the routine from Chapter 3: radio at set times, inventory, water discipline — and check on neighbours (Chapter 19) when it is safe to do so. Staying makes your street a team, not an excuse to ignore the people around you.

Destinations, honestly ranked

Family or friends outside the affected area. Known, free, humane — and reciprocal: your house is somebody else's plan, too. Make these arrangements on an ordinary day, not in the event.

Official reception centres and shelters. They may provide shelter, information and support. Bring medications and essential documents. Ask about pets if you can, but don't delay an urgent evacuation to find out; arrangements vary.

A hotel, if money and vacancies allow. Both can disappear quickly, so don't build your entire plan around finding a room after an evacuation begins.

Your car. A last resort, not a general shelter plan. Park clear of trees, power lines and places where water collects. Never run the engine in a garage or enclosed space. If stranded in snow, keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow before running the engine, open a window slightly, and use the engine only as needed for warmth. Carbon monoxide can build up even in a vehicle outdoors. If the car is in immediate danger, leave it for safer shelter.

The decision isn't once

Conditions change; so may the answer. Set times to decide again — "at the noon broadcast, we look at it fresh" — so the household isn't re-litigating it every hour. But don't wait for a scheduled review if an alert, order or changing conditions demand action.

And drop the sunk cost: having stayed through Tuesday obliges you to nothing on Wednesday. If the water reaches the door, the brave decision you made yesterday is not a contract. Go.

Pets come too

Every plan in this chapter so far has quietly assumed the household is all human. For a large share of readers it isn't, and the animal members are the ones most often planned for last and lost first. So the rule goes here, at the front of the section, where it can govern the rest: the plan includes the animals, or the plan is fiction. People may refuse evacuation orders or delay leaving because they will not abandon a dog. Emergency agencies warn that animals left behind can be lost, injured, or killed. Plan as if you will take them, because that is what the plan is supposed to make possible.

Food and water, in the same units as everyone else's. A pet eats every day of an emergency, not just the dramatic ones. Ready.gov recommends at least three days of food and water per animal; this book's floor for the humans is Chapter 3's 72 hours, building toward Chapter 1's two weeks, and the animals should be included in the same planning standard. Size the water honestly. A useful starting estimate for a dog is about an ounce of water per pound of body weight per day, but actual needs vary with food, activity, temperature, health, and individual animal. A 50-pound dog might need roughly a litre and a half a day, before extra demand from heat or exertion. Cats' water needs vary too, and wet food contributes to their intake. Use your own animal's normal intake and your veterinarian's advice rather than treating a cup a day as a universal figure. Store the food they already eat, in a sealed container, rotated like the people's shelf. An emergency is the worst possible day to discover what a sudden diet change does to an animal's stomach.

Carriers, leashes, and the practice run. Every animal that can be carried gets a carrier that fits it, labelled with its name and your contact number, stored where the go-bags live — not in the attic, not lent to a cousin. Dogs need secure, well-fitted collars or harnesses and a leash; a panicked dog may back out of equipment that has worked perfectly well on ordinary walks. Cats go in carriers, full stop: a frightened cat carried in arms is a cat you will be looking for under a porch while the water rises. And the carrier cannot be a stranger. An animal that has only ever seen its carrier on vet day may fight it on evacuation day. Leave it out, open, with familiar bedding or a blanket in it, on ordinary days, until it is furniture.

The paper and the chip. A copy of vaccination and licensing records, recent photographs of the animal — including one of you with it — and the vet's number go in the household documents from Chapter 5. A photo of you together can help establish ownership and distinguish your animal from others; it is not formal proof by itself. If the animal is microchipped, the chip only helps if the registration carries your current contact details. Check them on an ordinary day, the way you check smoke alarms. Medicines ride with the people's: a supply of any daily medication, with the prescription and dosing instructions, from the same continuity thinking as Chapter 13. For cats, add the unglamorous supplies: a small litter pan, litter, and bags. For everything, add waste bags. A shelter, a friend's spare room, and a motel are more workable destinations when the animal arrives with its own food, equipment, records, and sanitation.

The honest limits. Public emergency shelters in many places cannot accommodate ordinary pets inside the human shelter. Under US federal law, qualifying service animals are admitted with their handlers; pets are not service animals and may be housed separately or directed elsewhere. Local arrangements vary. That is a reason to make the destination calls now, in Chapter 4's ranking exercise, with the animal named in the question: which friend, which family member, which motel on the route takes dogs? Motels that waive pet rules during evacuations exist and are a kindness, not an entitlement. And if the worst version arrives — you are ordered out and the animal cannot be found — the decision is made in advance, here, on paper: you go. An animal left behind is left inside, never chained or penned outside, with food and water placed where they are as safe as possible and a visible note on the door or window saying what animals are inside. Do not assume that food and water will last or that rescuers will be able to reach them. That paragraph is a failure plan, not a plan. Everything above it exists so you never use it.

Chapter 5 — Law, Rights, and Order

Here is a thing nobody puts in survival books, because it isn't dramatic: the law does not evaporate in a disaster. It changes shape — emergencies can give governments extra powers and put extra duties on everyone — but courts, police, property, and paperwork don't simply disappear. What you do during the worst week of the decade may be judged in the calm years after it. This chapter is how to come through a collapse with your rights, your property, and your good name intact.

One honesty note up front: this is orientation, not legal advice. Laws vary by state and country, and emergency powers depend on the legislation and circumstances involved. Where this chapter and an official source disagree, the current law and authoritative official guidance take priority.

What changes in a declared emergency

Emergency orders can carry the force of law. Emergency legislation may authorise governments to issue orders ordinary laws do not permit, such as evacuations, curfews, road closures, business closures or restrictions on travel and gatherings. The powers available depend on the jurisdiction, the legislation and the situation. Some restrictions can also be imposed under ordinary laws without a formal emergency declaration. Disobeying a lawful order can be an offence. Chapter 4's rule lives here: if authorities lawfully order you out, leave.

Your rights don't evaporate. The US Constitution continues to apply to government action in an emergency — no declaration suspends it. Rights can still be limited by laws that meet constitutional standards, and emergencies have historically stretched those standards; the argument happens later, in court, not at the roadside. In Canada: the Charter continues to apply, subject to reasonable limits under section 1, and the notwithstanding clause in section 33 reaches only certain rights and freedoms, under specific conditions. Emergency powers are not unlimited, but don't assume every power automatically expires when a declaration ends. The practical translation: an order you think is wrong is challenged through legal channels, with a record, when it is safe to do so — not by arguing at the roadside.

Requisition may exist. Some emergency laws allow authorities to take, use or control property for response purposes, but the powers and compensation rules vary. Compensation is not necessarily automatic or full. If it happens to you, cooperate where it is safe, record who gave the instruction, the date and time, what was taken and its condition, and ask for written documentation if available. Records can support a claim; they do not guarantee payment.

Checkpoints and being stopped

In a serious event you may meet police, military personnel or other officials staffing checkpoints. They may be tired, under pressure and watching for risks. An argument can make a tense situation worse.

Slow, visible, calm. Keep your hands where they can be seen. At night, turn on the interior light if it is safe to do so. Follow instructions about the engine and vehicle. Answer simply and truthfully when asked.

Carry identification if you have it, and keep important documents accessible (Chapter 7). Identification can help establish who you are, but requirements and checkpoint powers vary. It is not universally compulsory in every situation, and it does not guarantee passage back into an evacuated area.

Comply with clear, lawful safety instructions where it is safe to do so. If an instruction seems wrong or unlawful, don't turn the checkpoint into a confrontation. Note who gave it and when, if you can do so safely, and seek advice or challenge it later through appropriate channels. Don't lie, threaten officials, interfere with their work or try to force your way through.

Don't run a checkpoint or escalate a confrontation. There is no useful argument to win at the roadside. Prioritise safety, follow lawful directions and address disputes afterwards.

Records: the paperwork that pays you back

After a disaster there may be a second disaster made of forms: insurance claims, government disaster-assistance programmes and compensation claims for property used or taken during the response. All of them may require evidence, and evidence is made during the event, not after.

Photograph your home and belongings now, on an ordinary day — rooms, valuable items, serial numbers and the general condition of the property. Keep a copy somewhere outside the house, such as a secure cloud account or a copy held by someone you trust. This can make later claims easier; it is not a guarantee of coverage.

During and after: document losses as they happen. If it is safe, take photographs before cleaning up. Record dates, what was damaged, and any costs you incur. Keep receipts for expenses such as temporary accommodation, fuel and emergency supplies. Assistance programmes have different eligibility and documentation rules; a receipt does not guarantee reimbursement.

Write down names and numbers. Officials you dealt with, claim numbers, dates, times and the neighbour who witnessed the tree fall. Memory is the first supply that runs out (Chapter 3); paper doesn't.

Insurance: the contract you read before the event

The records section above is about evidence. This one is about the other half of the paperwork — the policy itself, which most households meet for the first time on the worst week of their decade, in the exclusions section, at the adjuster's kitchen-table visit. Read yours now, on a boring day. A policy is a contract with a list of what it pays for and a longer list of what it does not, and the second list is where disasters can hide.

What a standard policy actually covers depends on its wording, but homeowners policies commonly cover sudden accidental events such as a burst pipe, a kitchen fire, or a tree falling on the roof in a storm. Fire, including wildfire, is commonly covered. That does not mean every fire-related loss is paid: limits, exclusions, deductibles, cause-of-loss disputes, and the condition of the property still matter. In fire country, the fight may not be whether fire is named; it may be whether an insurer will offer the coverage at a price a household can pay.

The three famous holes:

Flood. In the US, standard homeowners and renters policies generally do not cover flood damage. Flood insurance is available separately through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) in participating communities and through private insurers. For a qualifying one-to-four-family home, NFIP building coverage is capped at $250,000 and contents coverage at $100,000; renters can buy NFIP contents coverage up to $100,000. Those are policy limits, not a promise that every loss will be paid. A new NFIP policy generally has a 30-day waiting period, with exceptions. Do not assume you can buy coverage when a storm is already approaching. Flood definitions and exclusions matter: water entering through a basement, sewer, drain, or groundwater may be treated differently depending on the cause and policy. In Canada: overland flood coverage is available from many insurers as an add-on or separate coverage, but it is not automatically included in every home policy. Sewer backup and overland flood are distinct risks; ask about both.

Earthquake. Standard US homeowners policies generally exclude earthquake damage. Coverage may be available as a separate policy or endorsement. Deductibles can be a percentage of the insured dwelling limit or value, rather than the flat dollar amount familiar from an ordinary claim. The percentage and the basis used to calculate it vary. If the deductible is 10 percent of a $500,000 insured dwelling limit, the first $50,000 of covered damage is effectively below the deductible. Read the actual wording and calculate the dollar amount. In Canada: earthquake coverage is also commonly an endorsement or separate policy; deductibles and terms vary by insurer and province.

Sewer backup. Water coming back through a sewer, drain, or sump pump may be excluded unless the policy includes specific water-backup coverage. Some insurers offer an endorsement, often with a separate limit and deductible. There is no universal price or standard limit, and flood insurance does not necessarily cover sewer backup. Ask the insurer exactly which sources of backup are covered, what the limit is, and whether a flood must also be involved. This is a high-value phone call if your home has a basement or relies on a sump pump.

Two more rules that surprise people. Renters: the landlord's policy protects the landlord's insured interest in the building. It generally does not cover your belongings. Your possessions need your own renters policy or they may be uninsured. And gradual damage — the slow leak, the rot, the wear — is often excluded or limited. A policy is not a maintenance plan, and a long-running problem does not become a sudden accident because it finally becomes visible.

When the event comes anyway: report promptly, protect the property from further damage where it is safe to do so, and keep every receipt. Follow the policy's instructions, take photos before cleanup when safe, and write down every name, claim number, and important conversation. Do not make permanent repairs or discard damaged property before the insurer has had a reasonable chance to inspect it, unless immediate action is needed to prevent further harm. Government disaster assistance may also be available, but it is not insurance: eligibility, grants, loans, deadlines, and appeal rights depend on the programme and the event. In the US, FEMA assistance is not a general rebuilding guarantee. In Canada: federal and provincial disaster assistance programmes have their own eligibility rules and deadlines. Navigate all of it the way Chapter 19 navigates people — politely, in writing, and without assuming generosity or malice. Assume process.

The offences that are still offences

Looting is theft. A disaster does not make goods in an evacuated store free for the taking. Property may be damaged or abandoned temporarily, but that does not automatically remove the owner's rights.

Other people's property stays other people's. The abandoned-looking house on your street is somebody's home. Entering without permission may be an offence, even if the streetlights are out and nobody appears to be there.

Curfews, closures and rationing rules may be legally binding when imposed under valid authority. The details and penalties vary by jurisdiction.

Price-gouging and hoarding laws vary by place and circumstance. Some jurisdictions restrict excessive pricing or certain forms of stockpiling during emergencies; others have different rules or no equivalent general prohibition. Don't assume every high price or large purchase is automatically illegal. Check the applicable rules before buying or selling scarce essentials. Better still, be the neighbour who trades fairly (Chapter 19).

Fraud follows disasters like weather. Fake contractors, aid workers, charities and officials may ask for money, personal information or access to accounts. A badge, uniform, logo or caller ID is not proof of identity. Don't hand over money or sensitive information because someone claims to represent an agency. Verify through a contact method you already know is genuine. Real help doesn't demand gift cards.

Helping without creating liability

Good Samaritan protections vary. Every US state has its own law, and the scope of protection differs from state to state. In Canada, provinces have their own laws too, and Quebec's rules differ from the other provinces. Don't assume one rule applies everywhere. Help in good faith, within your training and ability (Chapter 14), and don't take risks beyond what you can safely manage.

Check on neighbours freely; enter homes carefully. A welfare check is what good streets do (Chapter 19). If someone may be hurt inside and does not answer, call emergency services or the appropriate local authority if available. Don't force entry merely because someone hasn't answered. An immediate, visible threat to life is a different situation; use your judgement without putting yourself in danger.

If you organise — a street rota, a shared generator schedule — write it down. Who agreed to what, when, and who is responsible. Record contributions and shared expenses clearly. Disaster cooperation can sour over promises remembered differently; paper helps neighbours stay neighbours.

Why this chapter exists

Because lawful, cooperative behaviour is not just ethics — it's part of a practical survival strategy. Clear records, calm communication and fair dealing can make it easier to work with officials, neighbours, insurers and assistance programmes. They don't guarantee that you'll be waved through, helped first or compensated fully. Order isn't the opposite of survival. Most days, order is survival — the thin shared agreement that lets a hundred strangers share one street, one water supply and one recovery without it turning into Chapter 20.

Chapter 6 — Communication: Hearing the Truth, Reaching Your People

In Chapter 3, information came third — right after people and water — because in the first hours, not knowing is its own emergency. This chapter is the full kit: how official word reaches you when the networks are down, how your household finds each other, and how to tell the difference between news and noise. Almost everything in it is cheap, and most of it you can set up this week.

The radio: the one device that can work without the network

When phones and internet fail, radio may still be standing. It needs no mobile network or account, and a battery radio can use very little power. But reception depends on your location, the receiver, its power supply, the broadcaster and the condition of the transmitter.

Get a battery or hand-crank AM/FM radio. Crank models can be useful, but don't assume the crank or a small solar panel will provide unlimited power. If yours takes batteries, store spares with it (Chapter 7 counts batteries as a supply). Test the radio and make sure you can tune the stations you intend to use.

Know your stations before you need them. Identify local news and emergency broadcasters, and learn your NOAA Weather Radio frequency — the National Weather Service broadcasts warnings and hazard information around the clock, and a radio with the NOAA bands and an alert tone is the one dedicated channel built for exactly this. Write the frequencies on a card taped to the radio. Check which services actually operate where you live. In Canada: CBC Radio is a mainstay emergency broadcaster, and the Weatheradio service has been discontinued — don't buy a radio expecting it to receive current Canadian Weatheradio broadcasts.

Phones and TV still help — while they last. In the US, Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) push emergency alerts to compatible phones in the alerting area — no sign-up, and the most serious alerts cannot simply be opted out of; the Emergency Alert System carries the same warnings across radio and television. In Canada, Alert Ready does the same job through wireless phones on LTE or newer networks and participating broadcasters. Read the alert and follow its instructions; don't assume it is only a pointer to a broadcast.

Listen on a schedule. In a long event, set listening windows — the top of the hour, morning and evening — instead of running the radio all day (Chapter 3). But an urgent alert or immediate safety instruction takes priority over your schedule. Information is a supply; budget it like water.

Reaching your own people

Text first, when it makes sense (Chapter 3's rule, worth repeating). Text messages can use less network capacity than voice calls and may get through when calls cannot. They are not guaranteed to send or arrive. Keep them short: who, where, OK or need help. Don't delay an emergency call to 911 because you are trying text messages first.

The out-of-area contact is your household's switchboard (Chapter 3). Everyone reports to the same person outside the zone; that person relays. Put their number on paper in wallets and go-bags — a dead phone makes memorised numbers of everyone except the one you wrote down.

Two meeting points, agreed in advance. One just outside your home (for fire — across the street or a named neighbour's porch, if safe), one outside your neighbourhood (for when the whole area is closed). Choose places away from traffic and likely hazards. Everyone, including kids, should know both and how to reach them safely.

Stretch the phone you have. Battery is information. Dim the screen, close unnecessary apps and use low-power mode. Airplane mode can save power when you don't need connectivity, but it also prevents calls, messages and wireless emergency alerts from arriving while enabled. A power bank in the go-bag is useful. Charge from a vehicle only when it is outdoors and the exhaust cannot enter a building or enclosed space; follow Chapter 8's carbon monoxide precautions. A traditional corded landline may work during a household power outage if the line and telephone exchange remain powered. VoIP and cordless phones usually need household power and working network equipment.

Paper still works. A note for responders or household members can say that you have left and when, without revealing your destination, phone number or other personal details to anyone passing by. Message boards at shelters and reception centres may help (Chapter 4), but don't assume every centre has one or that messages will be delivered. Check with staff where possible.

Radios that talk back

FRS walkie-talkies need no licence in the US. Their GMRS cousins — the same channels at higher power — do: an FCC licence. In Canada it flips: both FRS and GMRS are licence-exempt, power capped at 2 watts, no repeaters. Either way, buy radios certified for the country you are in. Their useful range depends on buildings, terrain, antenna height and interference. In built-up areas it will be much shorter than the range printed on the box; open ground can improve it, but no distance is guaranteed. For a street coordinating in a blackout (Chapter 19), test them between the places you intend to use them. Agree on a channel in advance, and remember that other people may hear your conversations.

Amateur (ham) radio is the deeper end: licensed operators, equipment that can communicate over long distances, and a community that often supports emergency communications. Listening requires no licence. Transmitting normally does — an FCC amateur licence in the US, a Canadian certificate in Canada. The rules contain a narrow emergency provision: when normal communications are down and life or property is in immediate danger, an amateur station may use any means at its disposal. It is not a blanket permission to transmit on any frequency or with any equipment whenever you declare an emergency. Don't build your plan around an assumed exemption. Getting licensed on an ordinary day is a worthwhile project if this interests you.

Scanners may let you listen to some local radio traffic, but they cannot receive every system. Some services use digital, trunked or encrypted communications. A scanner is not a guarantee that you will hear police, fire or emergency services.

Satellite messengers (the subscription kind) can send messages and SOS signals beyond mobile coverage where the provider's satellite network is available and the device has a suitable view of the sky. Coverage, delivery, subscriptions and emergency response vary. They're the one genuinely expensive item in this chapter — if your life regularly takes you beyond cell coverage, they may be worth it; for the household kit, the radio and paper plan come first.

News versus noise

A crisis manufactures rumour faster than information, and acting on rumour is how Chapter 22's mistakes happen. Three habits:

Official sources first, in their own words. Follow emergency alerts and instructions from the authority responsible for the incident. A broadcaster relaying an official bulletin is useful, but check the source and time when possible. A screenshot or repost is not the original instruction.

Verify when time allows — but don't delay urgent action. Follow an official emergency alert or direct instruction from the responsible authority. Don't wait for a second source before evacuating, sheltering or taking another urgent safety action. For rumours, unofficial instructions and major claims, seek independent confirmation through a trusted channel when there is time. Two websites repeating the same unverified post are not two independent sources.

Write down what you heard, when, and from whom. Include the time, source and whether the information was firsthand or repeated. Rumours mutate as they travel — including through you. The note (Chapter 3) is how you catch yourself improving a story.

Radio is an independent route to information when mobile data and websites are unavailable. It is not a truth detector. Broadcasters can make mistakes, repeat rumours or be affected by the same disruption as other services. Know your local stations, listen for official messages and compare important information with the responsible authority when possible.

When you cannot reach anyone

Sometimes the phone does not connect, the radio is silent, and nobody answers. Don't spend the whole emergency chasing a signal.

Try again at an agreed time rather than calling continuously.

Leave a short message with your name, location, status and intended next check-in, without sharing unnecessary personal details.

Go to the agreed meeting point only if the route is safe and doing so does not conflict with an evacuation or shelter instruction.

If the household has a communication tree, pass on the source and time of any information, and correct earlier messages when new facts arrive.

If someone is in immediate danger, use the safest available way to seek emergency help. Do not wait for a scheduled check-in.

Chapter 7 — When the Power Grid Fails

This is the most likely chapter in the book. You may never meet fallout or an impact winter, but you may well meet this one — most people already have. The grid fails in ice storms, heat waves, wildfires, floods and plain bad luck, and it fails in stages: what matters in hour one is not what matters in week two. Here is the whole curve.

The first hour

Don't open the fridge. The single most valuable act in a blackout is a door left shut (the clocks are below). Look with your ears first.

Find out whose outage it is. Check whether the problem is your breaker panel, your street or the wider region. If it is safe, look outside for signs of a wider outage. Report it to the utility if its reporting channel works, then get off the phone (Chapter 6's battery rules are now in force).

Protect sensitive electronics. Unplug computers, TVs and other equipment if you can do so safely. Power can return with surges or fluctuations that damage appliances. A suitable surge protector can help, but it is not a guarantee. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when power returns.

Water, if the supply is safe. A grid outage does not automatically make municipal water unsafe. Some systems rely on powered pumps or treatment equipment; others have backup systems. Follow local advisories. If officials say the supply is safe and there is no Do Not Use advisory, fill suitable containers while water is available. If an advisory is issued, follow its instructions. In freezing weather, find the pipe plan from Chapter 2 before cold becomes a burst pipe.

The food clocks

Memorise these numbers; they are standard food-safety guidance:

A closed fridge keeps food cold for about 4 hours.

A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours; a half-full one, about 24 — if the door stays shut.

Perishable food should stay at 4 °C (40 °F) or below. If food has been above that temperature for more than 2 hours, discard it. In temperatures above 32 °C (90 °F), the limit is 1 hour. The 2-hour rule is about how long food has been above 4 °C, not simply how long the power has been out.

Eat in the order Chapter 3 set: fridge first, freezer second, shelf last. Ice from the freezer can move into the fridge to help keep it cold; keep the fridge door shut and use a thermometer if you have one. After about 4 hours without power, discard refrigerated perishables unless you can confirm they have stayed at or below 4 °C. Frozen food that still contains ice crystals or measures 4 °C or below can generally be refrozen or cooked. Never taste food to decide whether it is safe. And the rule that ends all arguments: when in doubt, throw it out. Food poisoning in a grid failure, with the clinic dark too, is not a thrifty trade.

Light without fire

Headlamps and LED lanterns are the blackout's real lighting. A headlamp per person beats a drawer of candles on most measures: hands free, no flame and efficient battery use. How long batteries last depends on the device, battery and brightness setting; test yours.

Candles are a fire risk doing light's job. If you must burn one, use a wide, stable holder, keep it well clear of curtains, bedding and children, and never leave it unattended or burning while people sleep. Keep matches and lighters away from children.

Glow sticks on stairs, at the bathroom door and in kids' hands can make a dark house easier to navigate. They are a short-term aid, not a substitute for reliable lighting.

Money, cooking, and the practical layer

Cash is useful when electronic payments fail. Card networks, e-transfers and ATMs may depend on power and connectivity. Keep a modest household cash supply in small bills (Chapter 23 sizes it), but don't assume every shop will be open or able to make change.

Cook with Chapter 1's fuel discipline — lentils first, haybox, one-pot meals. Camp stoves and barbecues cook outside only; a garage with the door open is not outside (Chapter 8's carbon monoxide rule).

Generators, if you have one: operate them outdoors, at least 6 metres (20 feet) from the house and other occupied buildings, with exhaust directed away from doors, windows and vents. Never run one in a garage, porch, basement, shed or other enclosed or partly enclosed space. Install and maintain carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup. Let the generator cool before refuelling. Store fuel only in approved containers, away from living spaces and ignition sources, and follow local fire rules. A generator you can't supply with fuel past day two is a very loud day-one decision; size your expectations to your fuel.

Connect generators safely. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or household wiring without a properly installed transfer switch. Have a qualified electrician install the equipment and follow the generator manufacturer's instructions.

Week two: the problems change

If the outage stretches, the emergency stops being electrical and becomes logistical:

Water systems vary. Some water supplies depend on powered pumps or treatment equipment; others have backup power, gravity-fed storage or other arrangements. Pressure may fall or service may stop, but a blackout does not mean every municipal system will fail. Follow local advisories and use stored water when needed. Grey-water discipline — using suitable used water for flushing where appropriate — can help conserve safe water, but never use contaminated water for drinking or food preparation.

Sewage and garbage may be disrupted. Chapter 9's sanitation rules take over if collection or wastewater services fail. Keep waste contained and away from living spaces, food and water. Follow local public-health instructions for disposal; don't assume ordinary garbage collection will accept human waste.

Medicines meet the fridge problem. Some medications need refrigeration; many tolerate room temperature longer than people fear — but "many" is not "yours." Ask your pharmacist or the manufacturer what temperature range and outage plan apply to each medication. Don't assume a cooler and ice will keep it within range. Chapter 13 is the deep version. Plan backup power for essential medical devices such as CPAP machines, oxygen equipment and powered mobility devices. Know their actual power needs and realistic battery runtime, and arrange alternatives before an outage.

Apartments have extra dependencies. Elevators, electronic entry systems, water pressure and building pumps may fail even when the unit itself remains powered by a backup system. Know how you would manage stairs, access and essential equipment if these services stop.

Routine is the real infrastructure. Chapter 3's settled days — listening windows, inventory, neighbours — are what week two runs on, with Chapter 22 for the head that runs them.

Documents and records: the paper grid

A long outage can unplug the information grid too: banks, registries, insurers and pharmacies may depend on systems that are no longer reachable. The household that can prove things on paper has another way to keep moving:

One waterproof folder, kept findable: identification for every household member, health cards, insurance policies, property documents or copies, medication list and key phone numbers on paper (Chapter 6).

Copies in two other places: secure copies on your phone and a paper or digital set with someone you trust outside your area — the same redundancy rule as Chapter 18's library, applied to your own life. Keep sensitive information protected.

Account information written down securely. A service number, policy number or account reference may help when an app won't open. Do not keep passwords, PINs, banking credentials or account recovery codes in an ordinary documents folder. Store recovery information separately and securely.

When the lights come back

Wait and reconnect carefully. Power can return unevenly or fluctuate. Leave sensitive electronics unplugged until supply appears stable, then reconnect appliances gradually. Ten minutes is a cautious pause, not a guarantee that the supply is stable.

Audit the food by the clocks above, not by optimism. Then restock what the outage ate.

Write down what failed — your own house's after-action report, ten honest lines: what ran out, what surprised you, what you'd buy first. Every real blackout is a free rehearsal for a worse one, if you take the notes.

Chapter 8 — Staying Warm, Staying Cool

Chapter 3 put the clocks in order, but there is no reliable countdown for how long a person can survive dangerous cold or heat. Conditions matter: temperature, wind, damp clothing, shelter, activity, age and health all change the risk. Temperature doesn't announce itself like fire or flood. It seeps. This chapter is how you protect your body's narrow operating range with cheap tools, in both directions.

Cold: how hypothermia actually works

Hypothermia is not just "feeling cold." It is your core temperature dropping, and it can degrade the exact equipment you'd use to notice it: judgment, coordination and the ability to look after yourself. Learn the signs and you may spot trouble in others before they recognise it themselves.

The umbles — stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. A person getting clumsy, slurred, clumsy-fingered or uncharacteristically irritable may be getting dangerously cold. Treat the change seriously.

Shivering is a warning, not a measuring tool. Shivering is one way the body produces heat, but its presence or absence cannot tell you exactly how severe hypothermia is. Someone who becomes confused, drowsy, weak or less responsive needs urgent help, whether or not they're still shivering.

Warm them gently and from the core. Get them out of wind and wet conditions, remove wet clothing carefully, and insulate them with dry layers and blankets. Warm the trunk first, using wrapped warm packs if available. Handle a severely cold person gently. Call 911 if they are confused, very drowsy, worsening or seriously unwell. Do not make them walk around to warm up.

Warm drinks only if they are fully alert and swallowing normally. A warm sweet drink may help someone who is conscious and able to swallow safely. Do not give food or drink to someone who is confused, drowsy or unconscious.

No alcohol. It can create a feeling of warmth while increasing heat loss and impairing judgment. It is not a treatment for cold.

Frostbite: skin may become numb, pale, waxy or unusually firm. Protect the area and seek medical advice. Never rub it, massage it or use snow. Do not thaw frozen tissue if it could freeze again before medical care. If refreezing is not a risk, warm the area in warm, not hot, water. Avoid direct dry heat, which can burn numb skin. Do not walk on frostbitten feet if you can avoid it.

Cold: the household defence

One warm room (Chapters 3 and 4): choose the smallest suitable room, ideally with everyone in it — bodies produce heat. Close doors and reduce drafts with towels or other materials. Cover windows at night if that helps reduce heat loss. A tent pitched indoors or a blanket fort over the sleeping area can reduce the space you need to keep warm, but it does not create heat or guarantee safety. Insulate underneath people as well as over them. Never use a stove, generator, barbecue or other outdoor combustion appliance inside a tent, fort or room.

Layers, and the cotton rule. Use a base layer to manage sweat, a middle layer to trap air and an outer layer to block wind and moisture. Cotton can hold moisture and lose insulating value when wet; wool and many synthetic fabrics often retain more warmth when damp. The practical rule is simple: stay as dry as you can.

Insulation under you matters. The ground and floor draw heat away through direct contact. Sleeping pads, spare blankets or flattened cardboard under the sleeping place can help. Keep bedding dry. Dry socks and a hat may make sleeping more comfortable.

Change out of sweaty or wet clothes. Damp fabric can chill you as you cool down. Keep a dry layer for sleeping and change into it before settling in.

Hot water bottles — filled with hot, not boiling, water, wrapped in cloth and placed where they cannot burn the skin — can help warm a bed. Check the bottle for cracks, wear and deterioration. Keep it away from direct contact with vulnerable or numb skin, and never use a damaged bottle.

Heat: the other direction

Heat exhaustion and heatstroke are different emergencies. Heat exhaustion can involve heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache and cool or clammy skin. Move the person to a cooler place, loosen unnecessary clothing, cool them with water and airflow, and offer cool water if they are fully alert and able to drink. Heatstroke is a medical emergency. Warning signs include confusion, unusual behaviour, poor coordination, collapse or loss of consciousness. Skin may be hot, but a person with heatstroke can still be sweating. Call 911 and start rapid cooling immediately: remove excess clothing, wet the skin or clothing, fan the person and use cool water or ice packs where available. Do not give fluids to someone who is confused, unconscious or unable to swallow safely. Do not wait for sweating to stop.

Never leave a child or an animal in a parked car. Not for two minutes. A parked vehicle can heat to dangerous temperatures quickly, even when the weather outside does not feel extreme.

Work like the old farmers: do heavy work during cooler parts of the day, seek shade and rest during the hottest hours, and drink regularly. Thirst is useful information, but it is not a perfect measure of what your body needs. Avoid forcing excessive amounts of water.

Cool the house cheaply: open windows when outside air is cooler and close and shade them when the day heats up. Use curtains or blinds to block direct sun. A lower room may be cooler, but check the actual conditions. Cool cloths, a cool shower or a basin of cool water can provide relief; they do not make prolonged dangerous heat safe.

Check on the elderly (Chapter 19's standing order), and on anyone whose health, mobility or medication may affect their ability to cope with heat. Some medications and health conditions affect sweating, thirst or temperature regulation. Ask a pharmacist or clinician about individual risks and any precautions.

Carbon monoxide: the rule that outranks all others

Carbon monoxide can kill during a blackout. It is a gas with no colour, no smell and no warning of its own, produced by fuel-burning equipment. You cannot detect it with your senses.

Combustion happens outside unless the appliance is specifically designed and approved for indoor use. Generators must operate outdoors, at least 6 metres (20 feet) from buildings, with exhaust directed away from doors, windows and vents. Camp stoves and barbecues belong outside. Charcoal must never be burned indoors, in a garage or in a tent. An open garage door does not make a dangerous space safe.

Never warm up a vehicle in an attached garage, even with the garage door open. Keep vehicle exhaust pipes clear of snow and other obstructions.

Use fuel-burning heaters only as their manufacturer specifies. Some are designed for indoor use, but only under particular ventilation, clearance and operating requirements. Follow the instructions exactly. Never bring an outdoor heater indoors.

Know the symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion and unusual drowsiness. If a CO alarm sounds, or you suspect exposure, get everyone outside into fresh air immediately and call 911 from outside. Do not re-enter until authorities say it is safe. Symptoms affecting several people at once are a serious warning, but one person becoming ill can also be exposure.

Battery-backed carbon monoxide alarms belong in the home, installed and maintained according to the manufacturer's instructions. Test them as directed and replace batteries or the alarm itself when required. They are not a substitute for safe appliance use.

Chapter 9 — Sanitation and Disease: The Unglamorous Chapter

Here is the pattern nobody puts on the cover: in long disasters, the event itself can be only the beginning. What follows can be just as dangerous — waste with nowhere to go, water that carries it back, and hands that connect the two. Cholera after earthquakes, diarrhoeal disease after floods: the risk is real, but outbreaks are not inevitable. This chapter is plumbing for when there is no plumbing, and it is worth more than any dramatic chapter in this book.

Hands: the whole game, played five times a day

One habit does an enormous amount of work. Wash hands with soap and safe water: after using the toilet, after handling waste, before preparing food, before eating, and after caring for anyone sick. These moments matter because hands can carry germs from waste to food, water and people.

Soap and water are the first choice. A jug, a basin and a bar of soap do the job; a "tippy tap" — a suspended bottle that tips a thin stream — can make handwashing possible with a small amount of water. Use enough water to wet and rinse your hands, and scrub all surfaces with soap for at least 20 seconds.

No soap? Clean water and thorough rubbing are better than doing nothing, but ash and sand are not reliable substitutes for soap. Ash can irritate or damage skin, and neither ash nor sand should be treated as a disinfectant. Find soap as soon as you can.

Hand sanitizer is a supplement, not a substitute. Use a product with at least 60% alcohol when soap and water are unavailable and hands are not visibly dirty. Sanitizer works poorly on visibly soiled hands and is less effective against some germs, including norovirus. Soap and water remain the better choice after toilet use and when dealing with diarrhoea or vomit.

The toilet, when the toilet dies

While water still runs and the plumbing is working, flush with suitable grey water (Chapter 2), if local instructions allow it. When it doesn't — no water, frozen or broken pipes, a sewer system down — you need a contained alternative. A bucket toilet can be a practical household option, but it is not a universal relief-camp system and it does not solve disposal.

The two-bucket toilet. Use sturdy, stable buckets with close-fitting lids and strong liners that fit securely. One bucket can be used for urine and another for solids if you have the space and can manage them safely. Keeping urine separate can reduce odour and make the solids easier to handle. For solids, a small amount of suitable dry cover material, such as sawdust or dry soil, can help control odour and flies. Keep the toilet covered between uses.

In a pinch, one bucket and a liner may provide temporary containment. Keep it stable, covered and away from food, sleeping areas, children and pets. Put a handwashing station nearby, with soap and safe water. Do not overfill the bucket or handle its contents unnecessarily.

Containment is not disposal. Bagging waste does not make it safe for ordinary garbage collection, and local services may not accept human waste. Contact local public health or emergency authorities for instructions on storage and disposal. Keep waste away from wells, streams, gardens and places where runoff could carry contamination.

Digging, for longer events: a pit or latrine may be appropriate in some settings, but it is not a universal household solution. Soil, groundwater depth, flooding, slope, nearby wells and local rules all matter. Keep any pit at least 30 metres (about 100 feet) from water sources. Where groundwater is high, ground is wet or flooding is possible, do not dig without local advice. In suitable wilderness settings, a 15–20 cm cat hole may be used for a single person's waste where local rules permit; it is not a prolonged household sanitation plan.

Waste: getting it away from the living

Bag it, contain it, stage it safely. Use strong, suitable bags and close them securely. Keep contained waste in a designated place away from living areas, food, children, pets and water, until authorities or local services provide safe disposal instructions. Wear disposable gloves if available, or use a barrier when handling bags. Wash hands with soap and safe water immediately afterwards.

Do not bury bags near water or gardens, and do not burn mixed household waste. Burning plastic, treated materials and sanitary products can release harmful smoke. Do not assume burial is safe simply because waste is unbagged; human waste needs an appropriate sanitation system and suitable site. Follow public-health instructions.

Garbage discipline: rotting food waste draws animals and flies, and flies can carry germs onto food and surfaces. Keep food scraps contained, sealed and away from living spaces. Separate ordinary garbage from human waste and contaminated cleanup materials. Do not mix them into one disposal stream unless local instructions say to. The tidy camp is not being fussy. The tidy camp is reducing exposure.

The sick room: isolation without a hospital

When illness comes anyway, the household becomes a tiny hospital, but a home sick room is not a hospital and cannot replace medical care. The aim is to reduce transmission while watching for signs that someone needs help.

One room, one caregiver where possible. Give the sick person their own space if the house allows it, and separate cups, utensils, towels and bedding. A designated caregiver can reduce the number of people exposed, but make sure there is a backup if that person becomes ill or needs rest. Keep children and pets away from waste and contaminated items.

Air and distance for respiratory illness: improve ventilation where possible, and use well-fitting masks if available. Both patient and caregiver can wear one if they can do so safely. Keep unnecessary visitors away, and avoid sharing close indoor air for longer than necessary.

Diarrhoea can cause dangerous dehydration. Oral rehydration solution replaces water and salts lost through diarrhoea and vomiting. Commercial oral rehydration packets are preferable when available; follow the packet's exact water instructions. If none are available, a standard home mixture is 1 litre of safe water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar and ½ level teaspoon of salt. Measure carefully. Too much salt can be dangerous. Give small, frequent sips to someone who is alert and able to swallow. Do not use taste as a measurement test. Chapter 14 covers danger signs that need urgent medical attention: inability to drink, repeated vomiting that prevents fluids staying down, confusion or unusual drowsiness, very little urine, sunken eyes, severe weakness, or a child who is limp or difficult to wake. Blood in the stool also needs prompt medical assessment.

Clean first, then disinfect. Remove visible dirt and body fluids using gloves and disposable materials if available. Then disinfect surfaces with a product suitable for the surface and the suspected germ, following its label for dilution and contact time. If using bleach, follow the product's instructions and never mix it with ammonia, acids or other cleaners. Do not use household disinfectant on skin or wounds.

Handle laundry carefully. Do not shake soiled bedding or clothing. Keep it away from clean laundry and food, wash with detergent using the warmest suitable setting, and dry it thoroughly. Bag waste and contaminated disposable materials separately where possible. Wash hands after handling anything from the sick room.

Food hygiene in a dirty world

The food-safety clocks of Chapter 7 still rule; a dirty kitchen makes them harder to manage, not less important. Cook food thoroughly, eat it promptly, keep it at safe temperatures and protect it from flies and other pests — a cloth over food is equipment, not garnish.

Washing up on little water: scrape food scraps into waste first. Wash with soap and safe water, rinse with safe water, and let dishes air-dry on a clean rack or surface. If you use separate basins, keep the wash and rinse water clean enough for their purpose. A shared drying towel can transfer contamination; air-drying is preferable.

Anyone with diarrhoea or vomiting does not prepare food for others. Keep them out of food preparation while ill and for at least 48 hours after symptoms stop, particularly after suspected norovirus. Wash hands carefully and clean contaminated surfaces. Discard food contaminated by vomit or faeces. No exceptions for politeness. This one rule can help prevent a whole-household outbreak.

When sanitation itself becomes an emergency

A backed-up sewer, overflowing toilet, contaminated well or widespread failure of waste collection is not just an inconvenience. It can make a home or neighbourhood unsafe.

Avoid contact with sewage and floodwater. Keep children and pets away. Cover cuts and wounds, wear protective gloves and boots if cleanup is unavoidable, and wash thoroughly afterwards. Do not enter standing water where electrical hazards may be present.

Stop using plumbing that is backing up. Further flushing or running water may worsen an overflow. Contact the utility, landlord or local authorities for instructions.

Know when to leave. If sewage is entering living areas, safe water is unavailable, waste cannot be contained, or illness is spreading and the household cannot manage safely, contact public health or emergency services. Relocation may be safer than trying to improvise a sanitation system indefinitely.

Work with the community. Shared facilities, collection points and disposal arrangements need clear instructions and responsible management. A household solution cannot safely handle a neighbourhood's waste by itself.

Chapter 10 — Foraging

This is the deepest skill in the book: knowledge that lives in a person, not a pantry. It is also the chapter with the sharpest edge. Plants fed and healed humanity for millennia, and a short list of them can kill you by suppertime. So this chapter is built backwards from most plant writing: safety architecture first, plant list second, and the medicine kept humble throughout. Traditional uses are labelled traditional, doses stay conservative, and the limits get said out loud. A field guide for your own region — a paper one, on the Chapter 18 shelf — is this chapter's required companion. A chapter can start you; it cannot certify you.

The certainty rule, above everything

From Chapter 1, and it governs every word below: never eat or dose a plant you cannot identify with certainty. Not "looks like." Certainty means identifying the exact species, the part you intend to use, and whether that part is edible or suitable for the intended use at this time of year. Build that certainty properly:

Multiple features, not one. Examine leaves, stems, flowers or fruit, growth pattern, habitat and season. Compare the whole plant with a reliable regional guide, including its known look-alikes. Matching several features makes a candidate; it does not automatically prove an identification. If the specimen is incomplete or you cannot rule out a dangerous look-alike, leave it alone.

Know the local killers first. Every region has dangerous plants that resemble edible ones. Across the West — the US Pacific Northwest and western Canada alike — water hemlock and false hellebore are among the plants worth learning to recognise. The carrot family includes both familiar food plants and highly poisonous species. If you cannot identify a member of that family precisely, do not eat it.

Apps and AI are second opinions, never verdicts (Chapter 18's rule, and nowhere does it matter more). A photo identification can be confidently wrong, especially when the exact species is the difference between food and poisoning.

A person beats a picture. Learn from a knowledgeable local instructor who can identify plants in person and explain regional look-alikes. Experience helps, but no person's confidence replaces a complete identification. Regions differ; books generalise; local knowledge matters.

The entries: fifty plants, one format

The fifty entries below follow one format: where it grows, how to identify it, what is eaten and how it is prepared, the entry's own do's and don'ts, and the look-alikes that matter. Read the whole entry, every time — the safety lives in the combination, and no single feature in any entry is a permission slip.

Greens and potherbs

1. Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)

Region: Widespread across temperate North America. Where it grows: Open, disturbed ground, lawns, fields, and roadsides.

Identify: A basal rosette of deeply toothed leaves, usually with irregular lobes pointing back toward the base; a thick taproot; and a single yellow flower head on a leafless, hollow stalk. The stalk releases white milky sap when broken. The flower head later becomes a spherical seed head. Confirm the whole plant: no single feature is enough.

Eat: Young leaves, flower parts, and roots are traditionally eaten. Prepare: Wash leaves thoroughly; older leaves are often bitter and are better cooked. Remove the green base of flower heads if it tastes too bitter. Roots can be scrubbed, sliced, roasted, and ground for a coffee-like drink; this is a flavour substitute, not coffee.

Do / Don't: Do harvest only from ground you know has not been treated with pesticides or contaminated by chemicals, road runoff, or animal waste. Don't assume a lawn is safe because it looks clean. Bitterness is not a reliable safety test.

Look-alikes: Cat's-ear (Hypochaeris radicata) and hawkweeds can resemble dandelion, especially before flowering. Cat's-ear commonly has hairy leaves and branched, solid flower stalks; dandelion has a single unbranched hollow stalk per flower. These differences help, but confirm the complete plant. Do not rely on the claim that dandelion has no poisonous look-alikes of consequence.

2. Broadleaf Plantain (Plantago major)

Region: Widespread across North America. Where it grows: Lawns, paths, compacted ground, gardens, and other places disturbed by people.

Identify: A basal rosette of broad oval leaves with several strong veins running lengthwise from the leaf base toward the tip. The veins may become stringy when a leaf is torn. A leafless flower stalk carries a narrow spike of tiny flowers and later seeds.

Eat: Young leaves are eaten raw or cooked; seeds can be collected and used as food. Prepare: Wash carefully. Older leaves become tough and fibrous; cook them thoroughly or use only the youngest tender leaves.

Do / Don't: Do identify the plant positively and harvest from clean ground. Don't treat traditional medicinal use as proven treatment. A crushed leaf is not a sterile wound dressing, and this book does not recommend applying wild plants to open wounds.

Look-alikes: Narrowleaf plantain (Plantago lanceolata) is a related species also used as food. Young rosettes of other plants can be confusing; wait for the characteristic veins and flower spike if identification is uncertain. The banana called plantain is unrelated.

3. Stinging Nettle (Urtica dioica)

Region: Widespread across much of North America, with distribution varying by subspecies and region. Where it grows: Moist, nutrient-rich ground, stream banks, woodland edges, pastures, and disturbed sites.

Identify: An upright plant with opposite, toothed leaves, usually oval to heart-shaped, and stinging hairs on the stems and leaves. The stem is commonly four-angled or square in cross-section. Small greenish flowers grow in clusters from the leaf axils. Do not use the sting as an identification test.

Eat: Young leaves and tender shoot tips, preferably before flowering. Prepare: Wear gloves and long sleeves when harvesting. Cook thoroughly, steam, or dry the leaves before eating. Blanching or cooking removes the sting when done properly; do not assume a brief dip always makes every part safe to handle or eat. Use as a cooked green.

Do / Don't: Do take only young, healthy growth from clean ground. Don't eat raw stinging nettle or gather old, coarse plants as a food staple. Do not assume every plant called nettle is the same species.

Look-alikes: Wood nettle (Laportea canadensis) is a related stinging plant with alternate leaves rather than the opposite leaves of common nettle. White dead-nettle (Lamium album) is not a true nettle and does not sting. Neither leaf shape nor the presence or absence of a sting should replace complete identification.

4. Lamb's Quarters (Chenopodium album)

Region: Widespread across North America. Where it grows: Gardens, cultivated ground, compost edges, fields, and disturbed soil.

Identify: Leaves vary in shape, often becoming more triangular on mature plants. Young leaves and the undersides may have a pale, mealy coating. Stems may be green or reddish, and small greenish flowers grow in dense clusters. The mealy coating is a useful clue, not proof of identity.

Eat: Young leaves and tender tips are traditionally eaten, usually cooked. Seeds have also been used as food after careful cleaning and preparation. Prepare: Wash thoroughly and cook the leaves like other greens. Seed preparation is more involved; do not assume that simply grinding gathered seeds makes them ready to eat.

Do / Don't: Do eat it as one part of a varied diet. Lamb's quarters contains oxalates, and plants growing in some conditions can accumulate nitrates. Avoid large, repeated servings, especially if you have been advised to limit oxalates. Do not harvest from contaminated or sprayed ground. Don't use the pale coating as your only identification mark.

Look-alikes: Other goosefoots and related Chenopodium species can resemble it. Some are edible, but that does not make every relative interchangeable. Confirm the whole plant using a regional field guide before harvesting.

5. Purslane (Portulaca oleracea)

Region: Widespread in warm and temperate parts of North America. Where it grows: Gardens, cultivated ground, pavement cracks, and sunny disturbed soil.

Identify: A low-growing plant with smooth, fleshy leaves, often oval or spoon-shaped, and smooth reddish or green stems. Small yellow flowers may appear in the leaf axils or at stem tips. A broken stem has clear, watery sap. Confirm the combination of growth habit, leaves, stems, and flowers.

Eat: Leaves and tender stems are eaten raw or cooked. Prepare: Wash carefully to remove soil and grit. It grows close to the ground, where contamination is easy.

Do / Don't: Do remember the useful exclusion clue: milky white sap means leave it alone. Clear sap does not, by itself, prove that a plant is purslane. Identify the complete plant before eating it.

Look-alikes: Prostrate spurges (Euphorbia species) can grow in similar mats. They generally have thinner leaves and release milky sap when broken. Some spurges can cause irritation or illness. Never identify purslane by sprawl or sap colour alone.

6. Chickweed (Stellaria media)

Region: Widespread across North America, especially in cool seasons. Where it grows: Moist garden soil, shaded ground, cultivated beds, and disturbed places.

Identify: A soft, sprawling plant with opposite small leaves and delicate stems. Its white flowers have five deeply divided petals, often appearing to have ten. A line of hairs commonly runs along one side of the stem; the line changes position at the nodes. Use the flower, leaves, stem, and growth habit together.

Eat: Young leaves and tender stems are traditionally eaten raw or cooked. Prepare: Wash carefully and use fresh. It can be added to salads or wilted into soups.

Do / Don't: Do identify it while flowers and stem features are available. Don't identify it by small white flowers alone, or assume that every sprawling plant with tiny leaves is chickweed.

Look-alikes: Scarlet pimpernel (Lysimachia arvensis, formerly Anagallis arvensis) can resemble chickweed in growth habit but has different leaves and usually orange-red or sometimes blue flowers. It is poisonous, and it is not a food substitute. Flower colour alone is not a sufficient separator; compare the whole plant.

7. Red Clover (Trifolium pratense)

Region: Widespread across North America. Where it grows: Lawns, pastures, meadows, and field edges.

Identify: Leaves are arranged in groups of three leaflets, commonly with a pale chevron or crescent marking on each leaflet. The flower heads are rounded clusters of pink to purple individual flowers. Stems and leaves are often hairy. Confirm the flower structure and the rest of the plant.

Eat: Flowers and young leaves are traditionally used as food. Prepare: Wash carefully. Flowers can be used fresh or dried; young leaves are better cooked if they are tough.

Do / Don't: Do treat food use and medicinal use as different questions. Concentrated red-clover supplements may interact with medicines, including some blood-thinning treatments. Ask a pharmacist or clinician before using red clover medicinally if you take medication or have a relevant health condition. Don't treat an occasional correctly identified food portion as equivalent to taking a concentrated supplement.

Look-alikes: White clover (Trifolium repens) is a related species also used as food. The pale chevron is a clue, not proof. Confirm the three-part leaf, flower head, growth habit, and full plant.

8. Curly Dock (Rumex crispus)

Region: Widespread across North America. Where it grows: Fields, disturbed ground, roadsides, ditches, and damp field edges.

Identify: Mature plants have long, narrow leaves with distinctly wavy or curled margins. The plant develops a tall flower stalk with clusters of small flowers that mature into rusty-brown seeds. The root is often yellow-orange inside, but root colour is not a safe identification test.

Eat: Young leaves have traditionally been eaten as a cooked green. Seeds have been collected and ground as a flour extender. Prepare: Wash leaves thoroughly and cook them. Boiling and discarding the water may reduce some soluble oxalates, but it does not make unlimited quantities safe. Seed cleaning and preparation require care.

Do / Don't: Do eat only modest portions. Dock leaves contain oxalates; people with kidney disease or a prescribed oxalate restriction should avoid them unless their clinician says otherwise. Don't eat large quantities raw or treat boiling as a complete detoxification method.

Look-alikes: Other docks (Rumex species) can resemble curly dock. Some are traditionally eaten, but species and preparation differ. Sorrel is a related genus, not simply a smaller version of curly dock. Confirm the species rather than relying on a rusty seed head.

9. Wood Sorrel (Oxalis species)

Region: Widespread across North America, with species varying by region. Where it grows: Woods, gardens, lawns, and shaded or partly shaded ground.

Identify: Leaves are usually divided into three heart-shaped leaflets, each with a distinct notch at the tip. The leaflets may fold downward. Flowers have five petals and may be yellow, white, pink, or violet, depending on species. Confirm the plant's leaf and flower structure.

Eat: Leaves and flowers of correctly identified Oxalis species are traditionally used in small amounts as a sour flavouring. Prepare: Wash thoroughly and use sparingly as a garnish or seasoning, not as a staple green.

Do / Don't: Do keep portions small. Wood sorrel contains soluble oxalates, which can be harmful in large amounts. People with kidney disease or a prescribed oxalate restriction should avoid it unless their clinician advises otherwise. Don't gather it by the bowlful or use sour taste as an identification test.

Look-alikes: Clovers have three leaflets but usually lack the distinct heart-shaped notch. Black medic has oval leaflets and different flowers. These comparisons help, but confirm the whole plant. The word "shamrock" is used for several unrelated plants and is not an identification.

10. Miner's Lettuce (Claytonia perfoliata)

Region: Western North America, especially the Pacific Coast and parts of the interior West. Its range extends into British Columbia and other parts of western Canada. Where it grows: Moist, often shaded ground in cool seasons, including woodland edges, gardens, and disturbed sites.

Identify: Mature flowering plants have a distinctive pair of stem leaves fused into a rounded, leaf-like disc surrounding the stem. Small white to pale pink flowers rise above it. Young plants have basal leaves that are often spoon-shaped or oval. The stem-leaf disc is a strong identification feature, but leaf shape varies among forms and related species.

Eat: Tender leaves and stems are traditionally eaten as a spring green. Prepare: Wash thoroughly and use fresh or cooked. Harvest only after the plant has been positively identified.

Do / Don't: Do harvest selectively from abundant, clean patches. Miner's lettuce can occasionally accumulate soluble oxalates; avoid treating it as a large daily staple. Don't assume that every small, succulent spring plant is miner's lettuce.

Look-alikes: Related Claytonia species can resemble it, and some have different leaf arrangements. Redmaids and other small spring plants may also be confused with young plants. The fused stem-leaf disc is useful, but identify the basal leaves, flowers, and whole plant as well. Do not rely on the claim that no dangerous plant can share any of its features.

11. Garlic Mustard (Alliaria petiolata)

Region: Established mainly in eastern and central North America, with scattered and expanding populations farther west. Where it grows: Forest edges, woodland understories, trailsides, gardens, and disturbed ground.

Identify: A biennial. First-year plants form low rosettes of rounded or kidney-shaped leaves with scalloped edges. Second-year plants grow upright, with alternate, increasingly triangular and sharply toothed leaves. Small white flowers have four petals arranged in a cross. Crushed leaves have a strong garlic-like smell. Confirm the life stage, leaf arrangement, flowers, and smell together.

Eat: Young leaves, flowers, and young seed pods have been used as food. Prepare: Wash carefully. Young leaves are commonly used in small quantities as a strongly flavoured green or condiment; older leaves are more bitter. Cooking can soften the flavour but does not replace identification.

Do / Don't: Do check local invasive-species rules before harvesting. In some areas, garlic mustard is actively controlled, but harvesting is not automatically an effective control method. Avoid spreading its seeds: clean shoes, clothing, and equipment before leaving an infested site. Don't make it a large daily staple.

Look-alikes: Some native plants have similar leaves, including violets and members of other plant families. A garlic smell is a useful clue, not a complete identification. Confirm alternate leaves on the flowering stem and the four-petalled flowers before eating.

12. Violet (Viola species)

Region: Widespread across North America, with species varying by region. Where it grows: Woods, lawns, stream banks, and moist or partly shaded ground.

Identify: Leaves commonly grow from the base on individual stalks and are heart-shaped, though shape varies among species. Flowers have five petals, usually with the lower petal forming a spur at the back. Flower colour varies: purple, blue, white, or yellow. Confirm the flower and leaf structure together.

Eat: Leaves and flowers of correctly identified edible Viola species are traditionally eaten. Prepare: Wash carefully. Young leaves can be eaten fresh or cooked; flowers are commonly used fresh as a garnish.

Do / Don't: Do identify the species and eat only the parts established as food for that species. Don't assume every plant called violet is interchangeable, or that roots and seed pods are food. Avoid harvesting ornamental plants unless you know they have not been treated with pesticides.

Look-alikes: Lesser celandine (Ficaria verna) has glossy leaves and yellow flowers and is not a substitute for violet; it is poisonous, especially when raw. Ground ivy (Glechoma hederacea) has rounded, scalloped leaves on creeping stems. Flower petal counts and leaf arrangement help, but neither one feature nor flower colour alone is enough to establish identity.

13. Fireweed (Chamaenerion angustifolium)

Region: Northern and western North America, including Canada, Alaska, and much of the northern United States; it also occurs farther south in mountain regions. Where it grows: Open forests, clearings, disturbed ground, and areas recovering after fire.

Identify: An upright perennial that can grow well over a metre tall. It has long, narrow leaves arranged alternately on the stem, and a tall cluster of magenta to pink flowers. Flowers generally open from the bottom of the cluster upward. The leaf veins tend to curve and join near the leaf margin. Confirm the leaf arrangement, flower structure, and growth habit together.

Eat: Young shoots, young leaves, and flowers have traditional food uses. Prepare: Young shoots can be cooked like asparagus. Young leaves may be used fresh or dried for tea; fermented-leaf tea is a traditional preparation. Flowers have been used in jellies and as a garnish. Harvest only positively identified plants.

Do / Don't: Do take young, tender shoots before they become woody, from clean ground. Don't harvest from sprayed roadsides, contaminated sites, or areas where collection is prohibited. Do not assume every pink-flowered plant is fireweed.

Look-alikes: Purple loosestrife (Lythrum salicaria) can have similar pink flower spikes, but it has different flower and leaf structures. Its leaves are commonly opposite or in whorls, and its stems are often square or angular. Fireweed has alternate leaves. These are comparison clues, not a stand-alone safety test; identify the complete flowering plant before harvesting.

Roots, bulbs, and shoots

14. Cattail (Typha species)

Region: Widespread across North America. Where it grows: Marshes, ponds, wetlands, and shallow water, often in dense stands.

Identify: Mature plants have long, upright, strap-like leaves and a distinctive cylindrical flower spike. The female portion becomes the familiar brown, dense "cattail"; the male flower portion sits above it when present. A mature stand is much easier to identify than isolated young shoots.

Eat: Several parts of correctly identified cattails have traditional food uses: young shoots, immature flower spikes, pollen, and the starchy rhizomes. Prepare: Peel young shoots to the tender inner portion and wash thoroughly. Immature flower spikes may be cooked and eaten like corn. Pollen can be collected from mature male flower portions and used as a flour addition. Rhizomes require substantial cleaning and processing to separate usable starch from fibres. Do not assume every part is ready to eat straight from the water.

Do / Don't: Do harvest only from water and sediment known to be clean. Wetlands can contain sewage, agricultural runoff, industrial chemicals, and other contaminants; cooking does not remove all chemical pollutants. Do not harvest young shoots from an unidentified stand.

Look-alikes: Blue flag (Iris versicolor) and yellow flag iris (Iris pseudacorus) grow in wetlands and can resemble young cattail shoots. Iris species are poisonous. Their leaves commonly form a flattened fan, while cattail leaves arise in a different arrangement. The absence of a brown cattail head on a young plant proves nothing: harvest shoots only when mature cattails in the same stand establish the identification, and confirm the stand's other features.

15. Wild Onion And Wild Garlic (Allium species)

Region: Species of wild Allium occur across much of North America, but the species and their ranges vary by region. Where it grows: Meadows, woods, lawns, rocky ground, and riverbanks, depending on species.

Identify: True Allium plants have an onion or garlic smell when their leaves or other parts are crushed. Other useful features vary by species but can include narrow basal leaves, bulbs, and rounded clusters of small flowers. Identify the complete plant, including leaves, bulb, flowers, and growth habit. Never dig or taste a plant to identify it.

Eat: Some correctly identified wild Allium species have edible leaves, flowers, and bulbs. Which parts are used depends on the species. Prepare: Wash thoroughly and use only the parts known to be edible for the positively identified species.

Do / Don't: Do treat the onion smell as an exclusion clue, not proof of edibility. If a plant that looks like wild onion lacks the characteristic onion or garlic smell, do not eat it. But a smell alone does not make an unknown bulb safe. Do not taste-test an unidentified bulb or rely on a single feature.

Look-alikes: Death camas (Toxicoscordion species) is a dangerous look-alike. It has grass-like leaves and an underground bulb, and all parts are poisonous. It does not have the characteristic onion smell of Allium, but smell is not a substitute for positive identification. Other toxic plants can also grow among wild onions. Identify the full plant before harvesting; if the flowers, leaves, and bulb cannot be checked against a reliable regional key, leave it in the ground.

16. Ramps (Allium tricoccum)

Region: Eastern North America, from the Great Lakes and southeastern Canada south through the Appalachian region. Where it grows: Rich, moist deciduous forests, often on shaded slopes and in woodland patches. It is a spring ephemeral: its leaves die back by early summer.

Identify: One to three broad, smooth leaves rise from a bulb, usually with distinct leaf stalks. The leaves are often present before the tree canopy fills in. A crushed leaf has a strong onion-garlic smell. Confirm the leaf arrangement, stalks, bulb, smell, habitat, and season together. Do not identify ramps from smell alone.

Eat: Correctly identified leaves and bulbs. Prepare: Wash thoroughly. Use the leaves and bulb like a strong leek or onion, cooked or raw in modest amounts.

Do / Don't: Do harvest as if you want the patch alive in ten years, because you do. Ramps grow slowly, and removing bulbs can kill plants and reduce the colony. Taking one leaf while leaving the bulb is less damaging, but repeated harvesting can still weaken a patch. Follow local harvest limits and landowner rules. Some jurisdictions restrict commercial or bulb harvesting. Don't buy wild bulbs without asking where they came from.

Look-alikes: Lily-of-the-valley (Convallaria majalis) and false hellebore (Veratrum species) can grow in similar woodland habitats and are poisonous. Lily-of-the-valley has leaves that may emerge in pairs and later bears white, bell-shaped flowers. False hellebore has strongly pleated leaves that wrap around the stem. Neither has the characteristic onion smell. That smell is an exclusion clue, not proof: the entire plant must agree with a reliable regional identification. If there is no clear onion smell, do not eat it.

17. Burdock (Arctium minus and Arctium lappa)

Region: Introduced and widespread across much of North America. Where it grows: Waste ground, field edges, roadsides, farmyards, and disturbed soil. Its hooked burrs travel on clothing and animal fur.

Identify: A first-year plant forms a large basal rosette of broad, heart-shaped leaves, often with a woolly or pale underside and long stalks. In its second year, it sends up a tall, branching flower stalk with purple thistle-like flower heads that develop into hooked burrs. Species differ in size and detail; identify the whole plant rather than relying on leaf size or a hollow stalk.

Eat: The correctly identified first-year taproot. Young flower stalks are eaten in some traditions, but they require careful peeling and preparation. Prepare: Dig roots from a positively identified first-year plant, or from a plant whose identity was established when it flowered the previous year. Scrub, peel if needed, slice, and cook thoroughly. Young stalks must be peeled to remove the tough outer fibres and cooked.

Do / Don't: Do make sure you know the plant before digging. A large rosette is not enough. Avoid plants from sprayed verges, contaminated ground, or locations where the soil may contain pollutants. Don't eat burrs or seeds.

Look-alikes: Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) forms a basal rosette and is poisonous. Young foxglove leaves can be confused with other large rosette plants; differences in hair, leaf shape, and stalk do not make a safe one-feature test. Do not dig an unfamiliar rosette because it looks like burdock. Rhubarb is a garden plant with large leaves and substantial stalks; its leaves are not edible. Confirm burdock from its full growth pattern, ideally including its characteristic burrs.

18. Jerusalem Artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus)

Region: Native to central and eastern North America; cultivated and naturalized elsewhere. Where it grows: Fields, riverbanks, roadsides, garden edges, and disturbed ground. It can spread from discarded tubers.

Identify: A tall perennial sunflower with rough leaves, yellow flower heads, and underground clusters of irregular, knobbly tubers. The leaves are generally opposite lower down and alternate farther up the stem. The flowers and tubers together are useful identification features; the tuber alone is not.

Eat: Tubers. Prepare: Scrub thoroughly; peeling is optional. They can be roasted, boiled, mashed, pickled, or eaten raw when correctly identified. Cooked tubers are often easier for people to tolerate.

Do / Don't: Do warn the table about inulin. It is a fermentable carbohydrate that can cause gas, bloating, and discomfort, particularly in people who are not accustomed to it. Start with a small serving. Cooking does not reliably remove the inulin or guarantee that a person will tolerate the tubers. Don't dig from an unidentified stand or assume every sunflower-like plant has edible tubers.

Look-alikes: Other sunflowers can resemble the above-ground plant, but do not assume that their roots are food. Confirm the species from its leaves, stem, flowers, growth habit, and tubers together.

19. Chicory (Cichorium intybus)

Region: Introduced and widespread across much of North America. Where it grows: Roadsides, dry waste ground, field edges, and open disturbed areas.

Identify: A stiff, branching plant with blue flower heads on a tall stem. The flowers have strap-shaped petals with squared or notched tips. Basal leaves form a rosette and can resemble dandelion leaves, but are often rougher and more deeply lobed. The plant develops a substantial taproot.

Eat: Young leaves and the root. Prepare: Use young leaves in mixed greens or cook them to reduce bitterness. Older leaves are tougher and more bitter. Roots can be dug, scrubbed, sliced, dried, roasted, and ground as a coffee substitute or extender.

Do / Don't: Do choose clean ground. Roadside plants may be exposed to herbicides, vehicle pollution, and contaminated runoff. Don't assume a bitter root drink is nutritionally or chemically equivalent to coffee; it is a different drink with a different composition.

Look-alikes: Dandelion and other members of the daisy family can resemble chicory when young. Dandelion usually has a single flower head on a leafless, unbranched stalk; chicory develops a branching stem with several flowers. That distinction is useful once the plant is mature, but it is not a reason to eat an unidentified rosette. Confirm the plant before harvesting.

Fruits and berries

20. Blackberry And Raspberry (Rubus species)

Region: Widespread across North America, with different species in different regions. Where it grows: Woodland edges, fence lines, clearings, burns, stream margins, and other disturbed areas.

Identify: Brambles with arching or trailing canes, usually prickly, compound leaves, and five-petalled white or pink flowers. The fruit is an aggregate of small drupelets. Raspberries generally separate from their receptacle when picked, leaving a hollow centre; blackberries retain the receptacle inside the fruit.

Eat: Fully ripe berries. Prepare: Eat fresh, dry, freeze, or cook into preserves and other foods. Ripeness varies by species; look for fully developed colour and fruit that separates readily without force.

Do / Don't: Do identify the plant as a bramble before eating. Check the cane, leaves, flowers, and fruit together. Wash fruit before eating, especially when it grows low to the ground or near animal activity. Don't eat an unfamiliar aggregate fruit simply because it resembles a blackberry.

Look-alikes: True Rubus berries are edible, but that does not make every dark, clustered, or aggregate-looking wild fruit safe. Mulberries grow on trees rather than bramble canes. Other plants can have clustered berries that look superficially similar. If the plant is not clearly a Rubus bramble, leave the fruit alone.

21. Wild Strawberry (Fragaria virginiana and Fragaria vesca)

Region: Both species occur in North America, with ranges that vary by species. Where it grows: Open woods, meadows, trail edges, banks, clearings, and sunny patches.

Identify: Low-growing plants with three toothed leaflets, white flowers with five petals and yellow centres, and small red fruits. The seeds sit on the outside of the fruit in small pits. Confirm the leaf, flower, growth habit, and fruit together.

Eat: Ripe berries. Prepare: Eat fresh, dry, or cook into preserves. A handful takes time to gather; that is part of the bargain.

Do / Don't: Do confirm the plant before eating. Don't use taste as an identification test. Don't expect garden-sized fruit; wild strawberries are usually much smaller.

Look-alikes: Mock strawberry (Potentilla indica) has yellow flowers and red fruits with a different surface and texture. It is generally considered non-toxic, but it is not a true strawberry. Barren strawberry also has yellow flowers and does not produce the same fleshy fruit. Flower colour is useful, but confirm the whole plant before harvesting.

22. Blueberry And Huckleberry (Vaccinium species; Gaylussacia species)

Region: Widespread across North America, with species varying by region. Where it grows: Depending on species, acidic woodland soils, barrens, bog edges, open forests, burned ground, and mountain slopes.

Identify: Woody shrubs with small, simple leaves and bell- or urn-shaped flowers. The berries vary in colour and shape by species. Many have a small crown or calyx at the blossom end, but that feature is not unique to edible blueberries. Confirm the shrub, leaves, flowers, fruit, and regional species together.

Eat: Ripe berries from positively identified edible species. Prepare: Eat fresh, dry, freeze, or cook as you would other berries. Seed size and texture vary between species.

Do / Don't: Do pick berry by berry, with the shrub clearly identified. Don't strip an unfamiliar mixed patch on autopilot. Several unrelated plants produce dark berries, and colour alone is not an identification.

Look-alikes: Pokeweed has dark berries in hanging clusters on conspicuous reddish or magenta stems; the plant is poisonous, and it is not a blueberry shrub. Some poisonous nightshades produce dark berries, but their growth form and flowers differ. Do not rely on the crown alone. If the plant's leaves, flowers, woody growth, and fruit do not all agree with a regional Vaccinium or Gaylussacia guide, do not eat it.

23. Elderberry (Sambucus canadensis and Sambucus nigra)

Region: Sambucus canadensis is native to much of eastern North America; S. nigra is European and cultivated or naturalized in parts of North America. Other elderberry species occur in western regions. Where it grows: Moist edges, stream banks, fence lines, woodland margins, and disturbed ground. Growth form varies by species.

Identify: The species covered here are woody shrubs with opposite, pinnately compound leaves: several leaflets arranged along a central stalk. They produce broad, flat-topped clusters of small flowers, followed by clusters of dark purple to black berries. Confirm the woody plant, leaf structure, flower cluster, and fruit together.

Eat: Properly identified ripe berries and flowers from edible species. Prepare: Remove leaves, stems, and unripe berries. Cook the ripe berries thoroughly before eating or preserving them. Flowers are used in some traditional foods, but identify the species and remove as much green stem material as practical.

Do / Don't: Do cook the berries and exclude green fruit and plant debris. Elderberry plants contain cyanogenic glycosides, with higher concentrations reported in leaves, stems, and unripe fruit. Raw berries can cause nausea, vomiting, and other gastrointestinal symptoms. Do not assume cooking makes every part of the plant edible. Don't use a general elderberry entry to identify an unfamiliar shrub with dark berries.

Look-alikes: Water hemlock is a poisonous herbaceous plant that may grow in wet places and can have clusters of white flowers. It does not have the woody shrub form and pinnate leaf structure of elderberry. Pokeweed, also poisonous, has different leaves, stems, and fruit clusters. Red-berried elder species require separate identification and preparation knowledge; this entry does not cover them. Never identify elderberry from its flower cluster alone.

24. Wild Grape (Vitis species)

Region: Native grape species occur across much of North America, with species and ranges varying by region. Where it grows: Climbing over trees, fences, woodland edges, and riverbanks. Mature vines may develop shredding bark.

Identify: A woody climbing vine with broad, usually toothed leaves, tendrils, and hanging clusters of fruit. Leaf shape and lobing vary by species. Grape fruits contain seeds, commonly more than one, but seed number and shape should be checked only as part of a full identification.

Eat: Properly identified ripe grapes. Some species also have edible young leaves when correctly identified and prepared. Prepare: Eat ripe fruit fresh or cook into juice and jelly. Young leaves used for food are generally blanched or cooked.

Do / Don't: Do examine the whole vine before eating. Check the tendrils, leaf attachment, leaf shape, fruit cluster, and seeds together. Don't eat dark berries from a climbing vine just because they look like grapes. Do not use the seed test by itself.

Look-alikes: Canada moonseed (Menispermum canadense) is a poisonous vine with berries that can be mistaken for wild grapes. It lacks the characteristic grape tendrils; its leaves have a different attachment and shape, and each fruit contains a single crescent-shaped seed. The berries can cause serious poisoning. Grape leaves generally have toothed margins and the leaf stalk attaches at the edge of the leaf blade; moonseed leaves attach differently. These are clues, not a substitute for identifying the entire vine. If the vine, tendrils, leaves, fruit clusters, and seeds do not all agree, do not eat the fruit.

25. Serviceberry (Amelanchier species)

Region: Widespread across North America. Saskatoon (A. alnifolia) is especially important in western and northern regions; other serviceberries are common in the east. Where it grows: Open woods, slopes, prairie coulees, stream edges, and woodland margins. It may be a shrub or small tree.

Identify: A woody shrub or small tree with oval leaves and clusters of white flowers in spring. The flowers have five narrow petals. The fruit develops into small blue-purple berries, often with a small crown at the blossom end. Leaf shape, growth form, flowers, and fruit all matter.

Eat: Ripe berries. Prepare: Eat fresh, dry, or cook into pies, preserves, and other foods. Serviceberries have a long history as food among Indigenous peoples of North America; dried berries have been used in foods including pemmican.

Do / Don't: Do wait for fully ripe fruit and confirm the shrub before harvesting. Don't identify serviceberry from the blue-purple colour or crown alone. Do not make the flavour or an alleged almond note an identification test.

Look-alikes: Chokecherry also produces dark fruit on a woody plant, but its fruit hangs in elongated clusters and each fruit contains a hard pit. Hawthorn is generally thornier and has different leaves and fruit. These differences help, but neither fruit should be identified by a single feature. Confirm the flowers, leaves, growth form, and fruit.

26. Chokecherry (Prunus virginiana)

Region: Widespread across much of North America, especially in open woodland, prairie margins, coulees, and disturbed areas. Where it grows: Edges, fence lines, open woods, stream margins, and thickets. It may be a shrub or small tree.

Identify: A woody shrub or small tree with oval, finely toothed leaves. White flowers grow in elongated clusters in spring. The fruit develops in hanging clusters and ripens from yellow or red to dark purple or black, depending on stage and variety. Bark and leaf details vary with age.

Eat: The ripe fruit's flesh. Prepare: Cook into jelly, syrup, or sauce. Strain out the pits and avoid crushing or grinding them into the food. The flesh can be very astringent, especially before full ripeness.

Do / Don't: Do keep pits, leaves, and bark out of food. Like other Prunus species, chokecherry contains cyanogenic compounds, particularly in seeds and vegetative parts. Do not chew or crush the pits. Wilted leaves can be especially hazardous to livestock. Don't assume that a bird eating the fruit makes every part safe for people.

Look-alikes: Black cherry (Prunus serotina) is a related species with edible fruit flesh but toxic seeds; it requires its own identification. Buckthorn fruits can also be dark and berry-like but belong to a different plant group and are not a substitute food. Confirm the elongated flower clusters, leaves, woody growth, and fruit structure before harvesting.

27. Wild Apple And Crabapple (Malus species)

Region: Found across much of North America wherever cultivated apples have been planted or escaped. Where it grows: Old farmsteads, abandoned orchards, fence lines, woodland edges, and planted areas.

Identify: A small to medium-sized tree with alternate, toothed leaves and five-petalled white or pinkish flowers in spring. The fruit is an apple. When cut across, the core usually shows five chambers containing seeds. That structure supports identification, but the tree, leaves, flowers, and fruit must agree.

Eat: Properly identified ripe fruit. Prepare: Sweet-fruited seedlings can be eaten fresh. Small, tart crabapples are often better cooked into jelly, sauce, or preserves. Apples naturally contain pectin, which can help a jelly set.

Do / Don't: Do wash fruit and check for rot, mould, and animal damage. Don't make a project of eating the seeds. Apple seeds contain cyanogenic compounds; avoid deliberately chewing or consuming large quantities of them. Ordinary accidental seed ingestion is not a reason to panic.

Look-alikes: Hawthorn is often a smaller, thornier tree with different leaves and smaller fruit. Its fruit has hard stones rather than the characteristic apple core. The five-chamber core is useful, but it is not a substitute for identifying the tree. Avoid unidentified fruit from ornamental trees or shrubs.

28. Rose — for the hips (Rosa species)

Region: Widespread across North America, with native and introduced species. Where it grows: Woodland edges, prairies, stream banks, roadsides, and mountain slopes. Usually a thorny shrub with arching canes.

Identify: Woody stems with prickles, alternate pinnate leaves, and five-petalled flowers, commonly pink or white. After flowering, the plant develops a fleshy red, orange, or sometimes darker hip containing numerous seeds and fine internal hairs. The details vary by species.

Eat: Properly identified ripe hips and, from correctly identified roses, petals. Prepare: Remove the seeds and internal hairs before eating the flesh. For tea, syrup, or other cooked preparations, simmer the hips and strain carefully through fine cloth. Check for remaining hairs before serving.

Do / Don't: Do treat the internal hairs as an irritant. They can irritate the mouth and digestive tract. Do not swallow the seedy core. Hips are also important wildlife food, so leave some on the plant. Don't harvest from sprayed or contaminated areas.

Look-alikes: No single feature makes an unfamiliar thorny shrub a rose. Mountain ash has compound leaves and clusters of fruit on a tree, not rose hips on a thorny shrub. Other prickly shrubs may resemble roses at a distance. Confirm the leaves, flowers or persistent flower parts, stems, and fruit structure together.

29. Prickly Pear (Opuntia species)

Region: Southwestern deserts, the Great Plains, and dry interior regions, with some species extending into southern Canada and eastern North America. Where it grows: Dry, sunny, often rocky or sandy ground. Some species are native well beyond the desert southwest.

Identify: A cactus with flattened, fleshy pads joined in branching segments. The pads have clusters of areoles, which may carry large spines, tiny barbed glochids, or both. Flowers are often showy; the fruit, called a tuna, is oval and may be red, purple, yellow, or green depending on species and ripeness.

Eat: Correctly identified young pads and ripe fruit from edible Opuntia species. Prepare: Glochids must be removed before handling the food directly. Use tongs and suitable protective gloves. Remove spines and glochids using a method appropriate to the fruit or pad; do not assume rinsing alone is enough. Prepare pads by trimming and cooking. The fruit can be peeled and the flesh eaten, but its seeds are hard.

Do / Don't: Do take glochids seriously. These tiny barbed structures detach easily and can lodge in skin, eyes, and other sensitive tissue. Avoid rubbing your face or eyes while handling the plant. Keep tools and work surfaces separate from ready-to-eat food until they have been cleaned. Don't generalize this entry to other cacti. It covers Opuntia, not cactus species as a whole.

Look-alikes: Other Opuntia species may have similar uses, but identification and preparation still matter. Other cacti are not automatically edible. If the plant does not have the characteristic flattened pads and areoles of Opuntia, this entry does not apply.

Nuts, seeds, and trees

30. Acorns (Quercus species)

Region: Widespread wherever oaks grow across North America. Where it grows: Oak woodland, mixed forest, parkland, and planted landscapes. Autumn is the main gathering season.

Identify: Acorns are nuts held in a cup-like cap on an oak tree. Oak leaves vary widely. White oak group species generally have rounded leaf lobes; red oak group species generally have pointed lobes with bristle tips. These groups differ in average tannin levels, but individual species and acorns vary.

Eat: The nutmeats, after proper leaching. Prepare: Remove the caps and shells. Discard mouldy, rotten, or insect-damaged nuts. Break the kernels into pieces or grind them, then leach the tannins using repeated changes of water until the bitterness and astringency are substantially reduced. Cold-water and hot-water methods exist; the method and duration depend on the acorn and how finely it is ground. Dry the leached meal thoroughly before storage.

Do / Don't: Do leach before using acorns as food. Tannins make unprocessed acorns intensely bitter and can cause digestive upset. Leaching is a food-processing step, not an optional flavour adjustment. Don't assume that a few days is always enough, or that a single taste proves the meal is ready. Don't gather from sprayed or contaminated areas, and leave a share for wildlife.

Look-alikes: Buckeyes and horse chestnuts are not acorns. Their nuts grow in different husks and come from trees with palmately compound leaves, rather than the alternate leaves and acorn cups of oaks. They are poisonous and cannot be made safe by acorn-style leaching. Identify the whole tree, not just the fallen nut.

31. Hickory (Carya species)

Region: Mainly eastern and central North America, with species ranges varying. Where it grows: Deciduous woods, slopes, bottomlands, and woodland edges. Shagbark hickory is especially recognizable by its loose, peeling bark.

Identify: A tree with alternate, pinnately compound leaves, usually with several leaflets. The fruit develops inside a thick husk that splits into sections as it matures, exposing a hard-shelled nut. The number and shape of leaflets and husk details vary by species.

Eat: Kernels from correctly identified edible hickory species. Shagbark and shellbark hickories are valued for their sweet nuts. Prepare: Gather sound fallen nuts, remove the husks, and allow the nuts to cure in a dry, ventilated place. Crack the shells with a tool and remove the kernels.

Do / Don't: Do identify the tree and nut before collecting a large quantity. Bitternut hickory is notably bitter and not a good food nut; pignut hickory is also less desirable as food. Taste is not a substitute for identifying the species. Don't crack nuts with your teeth.

Look-alikes: Walnuts have different husks and nuts. Hickory husks usually split into sections; walnut husks generally do not split in the same way. Buckeyes and horse chestnuts have different leaves and fruit structures. Confirm the leaflets, bark, husk, and nut together.

32. Black Walnut (Juglans nigra)

Region: Eastern and central North America. Where it grows: Rich woods, farmyards, stream banks, and planted landscapes. A large tree with a heavy crop of green-husked nuts.

Identify: A large tree with alternate pinnately compound leaves, usually with many leaflets. The round green fruit has a thick husk around a hard, deeply ridged shell. The husk darkens as it breaks down and can stain skin and clothing. A chambered pith in a cut twig can support identification, but do not use it alone.

Eat: The kernel. Prepare: Gather sound fallen nuts. Remove the husks while wearing gloves, wash the shells, and cure the nuts in a dry, well-ventilated place. Once cured, crack the shells with a hammer or vise and remove the kernels.

Do / Don't: Do cure nuts before cracking; freshly harvested nuts can be harsh and difficult to process. Wear gloves and protect clothing and work surfaces from the staining husks. Don't confuse a black walnut with an unidentified large brown nut. Juglone from walnut trees can affect some nearby garden plants, but that is a gardening consideration, not an indication that the nuts are unsafe to eat.

Look-alikes: Butternut (Juglans cinerea) is a related species with edible nuts and a different fruit shape and husk. Hickory nuts have different husks and shells. Buckeyes and horse chestnuts have palmately compound leaves and different fruit structures. Confirm the tree, leaves, husk, and shell together.

33. Hazelnut (Corylus americana and Corylus cornuta)

Region: American hazelnut occurs mainly in eastern and central North America; beaked hazelnut extends across northern and western regions. Where it grows: Thickets, woodland edges, open woods, and slopes. Usually a multi-stemmed shrub.

Identify: A shrub with alternate, rounded to oval leaves, often double-toothed and somewhat hairy. Male catkins appear before or around leaf-out. The nuts develop inside leafy, papery husks. American hazelnut has a shorter, more open husk; beaked hazelnut has a long, tubular beak around the nut.

Eat: The ripe kernels. Prepare: Gather mature nuts as the husks begin to dry and brown. Remove the husks, dry the nuts in a ventilated place, and crack the shells to reach the kernels.

Do / Don't: Do check for insect damage and discard nuts with holes, mould, or spoiled kernels. Wildlife competes heavily for hazelnuts, so expect a small harvest. Don't assume an unfamiliar nut on a shrub is a hazelnut just because it has a papery covering.

Look-alikes: The leafy husk is a useful feature, but it is not enough by itself. Confirm the shrub's leaves, growth form, catkins, and nut husk together. If the husk, leaf, and season do not agree with a regional hazelnut guide, leave it alone.

34. Pine (Pinus species — white pine foremost)

Region: Pines occur across most of North America. Where it grows: Forests, dry slopes, sandy ground, and rocky sites, depending on species.

Identify: An evergreen tree with needles in bundles (clusters). Bundle count is a species clue: eastern white pine has five needles per bundle. Cone shape, bark, needle length, and bundle count together help identify the species. Do not identify a pine from needle count alone.

Eat: Needles of positively identified species traditionally used for tea; inner bark and young cones are recorded as foods in some traditions; pine nuts come from particular western pinyon species. These are not interchangeable uses across all pines. Prepare: For a confirmed edible species, steep chopped needles in hot water and strain. This book does not provide a recipe for unidentified conifers.

Do / Don't: Do identify the species, not just "a pine." Some pine needles have caused reproductive problems in livestock, but that evidence does not establish the effect of pine-needle tea in humans. Because human pregnancy safety is not established, avoid pine-needle tea during pregnancy. Never use an evergreen you have not identified to species as food or tea.

Look-alikes: Yew (poisonous gallery) is an evergreen shrub or small tree with flat, single needles and red, fleshy arils surrounding poisonous seeds. Yew does not produce pine cones. Do not use "red berries and no cones" as the only test: identify the whole tree or shrub, including its foliage and reproductive structures, before ruling anything safe.

35. Birch (Betula species)

Region: Northern and temperate North America. Where it grows: Moist woods, stream banks, and wetland edges; paper birch is a common northern pioneer tree.

Identify: A tree with bark that may be white and peeling, as in paper birch, or darker and marked with horizontal lines, as in sweet and yellow birch. Leaves are alternate, toothed, and often roughly triangular or oval. Catkins appear in season. Bark, leaves, twigs, and catkins together help identify the species.

Eat: Sap in early spring, drunk fresh or boiled toward syrup; birch sap is much less sugary than maple sap, so it takes substantially more to make syrup. Sweet birch twigs and leaves have a wintergreen flavour and have been used for tea. Prepare: Tap only a healthy, correctly identified tree, using a small, clean tap and following local guidance.

Do / Don't: Do recognize that sweet birch contains methyl salicylate, a salicylate compound related to the chemistry of aspirin. Concentrated preparations are not harmless flavourings, and people with salicylate sensitivity or relevant medication concerns should avoid medicinal use without professional advice. Don't strip bark around a living trunk; girdling can kill the tree.

Look-alikes: Bark alone is not an identification. Confirm the leaves, twig structure, and catkins in season. If the tree cannot be identified confidently, do not tap it or use its bark as food.

36. Spruce (Picea species)

Region: Northern forests across North America, and mountain forests in the West. Where it grows: Cool forests, including extensive boreal woodland.

Identify: An evergreen tree with single, stiff needles attached individually to small woody pegs. A detached needle often feels four-sided and can roll between the fingers. Cones hang downward. Check the needle attachment, needle shape, and cones together.

Eat: The soft new spring tips of certain correctly identified spruce species are used as food and flavouring. Spruce needles have also been used for tea. Do not assume every ornamental or wild spruce is suitable for every traditional use. Prepare: Use only positively identified spruce tips; they may be eaten in small amounts, pickled, or used as a flavouring.

Do / Don't: Do pick sparingly, taking a few tips from scattered branches and leaving the growing leader intact. Don't gather from sprayed ornamentals, roadsides, or trees whose identity is uncertain.

Look-alikes: Yew (poisonous gallery) has flat, soft needles and red arils; it does not make spruce cones. Fir needles are generally flatter and attach without the small pegs typical of spruce; fir cones stand upright on the branches. Do not use this comparison as a substitute for identifying the tree.

37. Staghorn Sumac (Rhus typhina)

Region: Eastern and central North America. Where it grows: Dry edges, old fields, roadsides, and slopes; it often spreads into thickets.

Identify: A large shrub or small tree with stout twigs covered in dense, velvety hairs. Leaves are long and pinnate, with toothed leaflets, and often turn scarlet in fall. The fruit forms dense, upright, red, hairy clusters that may persist into winter. Identify the shrub from its twigs, leaves, growth habit, and fruit together.

Eat: The ripe red fruit clusters are used to make a tart drink. Prepare: Soak the clusters in cold water, rub gently to release flavour, and strain carefully through cloth to remove hairs and debris. Avoid prolonged hot extraction, which can produce a more astringent drink.

Do / Don't: Do strain carefully; the fine hairs can irritate the mouth and throat. Don't use any sumac with white or pale berries. Do not decide that a plant is edible from berry colour alone.

Look-alikes: Poison sumac (Toxicodendron vernix, formerly Rhus vernix) has pale berries and grows in wet habitats; its leaflets are smooth-edged, unlike the toothed leaflets of staghorn sumac. Staghorn sumac has hairy twigs and red fruit clusters. Confirm all of these features, not just one. Tree-of-heaven can have pinnate leaves but does not produce staghorn sumac's red fruit clusters and has a different growth form. Poison-sumac distinctions are not a reason to handle an uncertain plant.

38. Maple (Acer species)

Region: Sugar production is especially associated with eastern and northern North America, though maples grow across much of the continent. Where it grows: Mixed forests, farmyards, parks, and towns.

Identify: Maples have opposite branching and opposite leaves. Leaves are often lobed, though their shape varies by species. Their paired winged fruits, commonly called keys or samaras, are a useful confirmation. Identify the tree from its branching, leaves, bark, and fruit together.

Eat: Sap from sugar maple and certain other maples is boiled to syrup. The amount of sap required varies with its sugar concentration; roughly forty litres of sap for one litre of syrup is a common working estimate, not a fixed rule. Seeds of some identified maples have been eaten after preparation in traditional foods, but this is not a general instruction to eat maple keys. Prepare: Tap in late winter or early spring when freezing nights and thawing days produce sap flow.

Do / Don't: Do tap only healthy, adequately sized trees and follow current local forestry or extension guidance for tap size, number, and placement. Don't tap small, stressed, or privately owned trees without permission.

Look-alikes: Box elder is a maple (Acer negundo) and can produce usable sap, but its leaves are pinnately compound rather than the familiar simple maple leaf. Buckeyes and horse chestnuts are not maples; their leaves are palmately compound and their fruits are enclosed in capsules. Their seeds are poisonous and are not maple keys. Identify the whole tree before collecting sap or seeds.

39. Basswood (Tilia americana)

Region: Eastern and central North America. Where it grows: Rich woods, river bottoms, and as a planted shade tree.

Identify: A large tree with broad, heart-shaped leaves whose bases are often asymmetrical. Clusters of fragrant, pale flowers hang from a distinctive narrow, leaf-like bract. In bloom, the flowers attract many bees. Confirm the tree from the leaves, flower clusters, bract, and overall growth habit.

Eat: Flowers and young leaves. Prepare: Flowers may be used fresh or dried for a mild tea. Young, tender leaves have been eaten fresh in salads. Use clean flowers and leaves from a positively identified tree.

Do / Don't: Do take only a modest amount of flowers and leaves, leaving plenty for pollinators and the tree's reproduction. Don't gather from sprayed street trees or trees beside contaminated roads.

Look-alikes: Mulberry leaves can be heart-shaped, but mulberry leaves are often more variable in shape and do not have basswood's distinctive flower cluster and bract. Do not identify basswood from a heart-shaped leaf alone.

40. Wintergreen (Gaultheria procumbens)

Region: Eastern North America. Where it grows: Acidic woods, often beneath pines and oaks; a low evergreen groundcover.

Identify: A creeping plant only a few inches tall, with glossy, oval, evergreen leaves, small white bell-shaped flowers, and round red berries that may persist into winter. Crushed leaves have a strong wintergreen scent, but scent is only a supporting clue. Confirm the leaf, flower, fruit, and creeping growth habit together.

Eat: Leaves have been used for a modest tea; ripe berries have been eaten in small amounts as a flavouring or trail food. Prepare: Use only positively identified wintergreen. Keep preparations mild; this is a flavouring plant, not a medicinal substitute for measured medicine.

Do / Don't: Do remember that wintergreen contains methyl salicylate. The concentrated essential oil can cause serious poisoning, including after relatively small amounts. This book does not cover making or using the oil. Avoid medicinal-strength preparations, and seek professional advice before using wintergreen with aspirin or other salicylate-containing medicines.

Look-alikes: Partridgeberry is another red-berried groundcover, but its leaves, flowers, and paired fruit structure differ. Mountain laurel is a woody shrub with much larger leaves, not a creeping groundcover. Do not identify wintergreen by red berries alone.

Teas, flavour, and the medicine-adjacent

41. Mint (Mentha species)

Region: Native and introduced mints occur across North America. Where it grows: Often in damp ground, stream banks, ditches, and garden escapes.

Identify: True mints generally have square stems, opposite leaves, and a recognizably minty scent when crushed. These features help identify the mint family, but they do not identify a plant as an edible Mentha. Confirm the species using a regional field guide.

Eat: Leaves of positively identified edible Mentha species, including spearmint, peppermint, and suitable wild mints. Prepare: Use fresh or dried leaves in food and tea.

Do / Don't: Do identify the plant as a food species, not merely as a member of the mint family. Pennyroyal is not a food substitute. Avoid concentrated pennyroyal oil; it can cause severe poisoning. Don't gather mint-family plants from polluted ditches or runoff areas.

Look-alikes: American pennyroyal (Hedeoma pulegioides) and European pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium) are not interchangeable with culinary mint. Pennyroyal oils are particularly hazardous. A minty smell and square stem are not sufficient evidence that a plant is safe to eat. If the species cannot be identified, leave it.

42. Yarrow (Achillea millefolium)

Region: Widespread across North America, including native and introduced populations. Where it grows: Meadows, open ground, lawns, and roadsides.

Identify: Finely divided, fern-like leaves; a firm, often hairy stem; and flat-topped clusters of small white or pink flower heads. The leaves have a distinctive aromatic smell, but smell is not an identification test. Confirm the complete plant, including its leaf arrangement, stem, and flower structure.

Eat: Yarrow has a history of traditional use as a bitter herb or tea, rather than as a vegetable. This book treats it as an optional traditional-use plant, not a routine food. Prepare: Do not use it medicinally or make a strong tea from a plant identified only by its fern-like leaves.

Do / Don't: Avoid yarrow during pregnancy because of traditional cautions and insufficient safety evidence. People allergic to plants in the daisy family may react to it. Do not use it as a daily medicinal habit or as a substitute for medical treatment.

Look-alikes: Poison hemlock (poisonous gallery) also has divided leaves and white flower clusters, but is a different, highly poisonous plant. It is generally much larger, with a smooth, often purple-blotched stem. Yarrow is smaller, with finely divided leaves and a different flower-head structure. These are not reliable single-feature tests: identify the whole plant. Queen Anne's lace and other carrot-family plants create additional confusion. If the plant is not positively identified, do not gather it.

43. Willow (Salix species)

Region: Willows occur across North America, with many species. Where it grows: Wet ground, including stream banks, pond edges, and ditches.

Identify: A shrub or tree with flexible twigs, usually narrow leaves, and catkins in spring. Leaf shape and margins vary substantially among species. The genus can be difficult to identify to species, so use several features: leaves, buds, twigs, catkins, and growth habit.

Eat: Willow is not included here as a food. The bark of some identified willows has a traditional history of use for pain and fever because it contains salicylates, including salicin. This book records the tradition but gives no dose or home preparation. Bark strength varies, and an unmeasured preparation is an unmeasured drug. Prepare: Not provided.

Do / Don't: Do treat willow bark as a medicinal substance, not a harmless wild tea. It may carry aspirin-like risks, including allergy and bleeding concerns. Do not give willow bark to children or teenagers with a viral illness because of the Reye's-syndrome precaution associated with salicylate medicines. People taking blood thinners or other medicines, or with relevant medical conditions, should not use it without professional advice. Don't call it "nature's aspirin" as if natural meant safer.

Look-alikes: Many wet-ground shrubs can resemble willows, especially without catkins or leaves. Species identification can be difficult. Since this entry offers no food use, there is no reason to take a risk: if the plant is uncertain, leave it alone.

44. Bee Balm (Monarda species — wild bergamot, M. fistulosa, and scarlet bee balm, M. didyma)

Region: Eastern and central North America, with species ranges varying by region. Where it grows: Meadows, woodland edges, stream banks, and gardens.

Identify: A mint-family plant with a square stem, opposite toothed leaves, and distinctive shaggy flower heads. Wild bergamot is often lavender or purple; scarlet bee balm has vivid red flowers. Leaf scent varies, and should be treated as a supporting clue rather than proof of identity. Confirm the species from its full structure and flowers.

Eat: Leaves and flowers of positively identified edible Monarda species. Prepare: Use fresh or dried leaves for tea; use flowers sparingly as a flavouring. The plant has a strong, aromatic taste.

Do / Don't: Do begin with a weak preparation to assess flavour and personal tolerance. Don't gather from sprayed garden beds, roadsides, or contaminated sites. Do not assume every plant called bee balm is one of the two species covered here.

Look-alikes: Other Monarda species share the general form but are not covered by this entry. Identify the plant to species before use. A square stem and aromatic leaves identify neither a particular Monarda nor its suitability as food.

45. Evening Primrose (Oenothera biennis)

Region: Widespread in much of North America. Where it grows: Disturbed ground, field edges, roadsides, and open sites. It is a biennial.

Identify: In its first year, the plant forms a low rosette. In its second year, it sends up a tall flowering stalk with yellow, four-petalled flowers and elongated seed pods. The flowers often open in the evening, though timing varies with conditions. Identify the plant from its full life cycle, flowers, leaves, and mature pods.

Eat: Traditional uses include first-year roots, young leaves, flowers, and seeds. Only use positively identified Oenothera biennis. Prepare: Roots are harvested from confirmed first-year plants and cooked. Young leaves and flowers have also been used as food. Seeds may be used as a food ingredient, but this entry makes no medicinal or supplement claims.

Do / Don't: Do begin with a small serving; individual tolerance varies, and some people find the root strongly peppery or irritating. Don't dig an unidentified rosette. Don't gather plants from roadsides or treated ground.

Look-alikes: First-year rosettes can resemble many unrelated plants, including burdock. Do not dig a rosette based on leaf shape alone. Sundrops are close relatives in the same genus, and species identification requires flowers, leaves, and growth habit. If the plant has not been identified confidently, leave it in the ground.

46. Wild Sunflower (Helianthus species, including H. annuus)

Region: Sunflowers are native to North America, with different species occupying different regions; common sunflower is now widely cultivated and naturalized. Where it grows: Sunny fields, disturbed ground, and field edges.

Identify: Sunflower species vary. Many have rough leaves and stems, and flower heads with yellow ray florets around a central disk. The leaves may be opposite lower down and alternate higher on the stem, depending on species. Identify the plant as a particular Helianthus species using a regional key; a yellow composite flower or spiral seed head is not enough.

Eat: Seeds from positively identified edible sunflower species. Prepare: Allow the heads to mature on the plant until the seeds are developed. Harvest, dry, remove the seeds, and winnow away plant debris. Seeds may be eaten raw or roasted once clean and fully mature.

Do / Don't: Do confirm the species before collecting seed. Don't assume every yellow, sunflower-like flower produces edible seeds. Avoid plants from sprayed or contaminated sites.

Look-alikes: Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus — entry 18) is a perennial relative, but its traditional food harvest is the tuber. Many unrelated members of the daisy family have yellow ray flowers. Sneezeweed and other composites are not sunflowers. Flower resemblance and seed-head appearance alone do not establish identity.

47. Milkweed (Asclepias syriaca — common milkweed only)

Region: Eastern and central North America. Where it grows: Fields, roadsides, meadows, and open woodland edges.

Identify: Common milkweed is a stout, upright plant with broad, opposite leaves, often with a prominent pale midrib and a softly hairy underside. It produces clusters of pinkish flowers and later forms large, warty pods containing seeds with silky threads. Broken leaves or stems release milky sap. Identify the complete plant and confirm the species; milky sap alone is not enough.

Eat: Traditional food uses include very young shoots, young leaves, flower buds, and immature pods of common milkweed. This entry covers Asclepias syriaca only. Other milkweed species are not covered. Prepare: Traditional preparations are reported to use repeated boiling in fresh changes of water, with the cooking water discarded each time — but no home process has a tested endpoint, so this book gives no preparation method for milkweed. There is no number of water changes this book can point to and call sufficient. Treat the traditional uses above as history, not as instructions to follow.

Do / Don't: Do treat this as a preparation-dependent food with a narrow identification boundary. Start with a small serving even when using a traditional preparation. Milkweeds contain cardiac-active compounds and can cause serious poisoning. People with heart conditions, or taking heart medicines, should not experiment with milkweed as food without advice from a qualified clinician. Don't eat it raw, merely blanch it, or transfer this entry to another species.

Look-alikes: Dogbane can resemble milkweed in growth habit and also produces milky sap. It is not a food substitute. Differences in stem, leaves, flowers, and fruit must be considered together. If the plant is not unmistakably common milkweed, leave it alone.

48. Daylily (Hemerocallis fulva)

Region: An Asian ornamental plant escaped and naturalized in parts of North America. Where it grows: Roadsides, old homesteads, ditch banks, and former gardens.

Identify: Daylilies form clumps of long, arching, strap-like leaves from the base. Flower stalks rise separately and are leafless, carrying orange, trumpet-shaped flowers with six tepals. Each flower lasts about a day. Confirm the clump, leafless flower stalk, and flower structure together.

Eat: Traditional food uses include young shoots, flower buds, and flowers of correctly identified daylilies. Prepare: Buds and shoots are cooked; flowers may be used fresh or dried. Dried daylily buds are sold as "golden needles" in some Asian cuisines. This does not mean other plants called lilies are edible.

Do / Don't: Do treat daylily as a food that can cause individual reactions. Some people experience nausea, vomiting, or diarrhoea, particularly after raw or large servings. Cook the first serving and try only a small amount. Don't eat unidentified lily-like plants or assume a plant is safe because it has orange flowers.

Look-alikes: True lilies (Lilium species) have leaves attached along a leafy flowering stem; daylily flower stalks rise separately from the basal leaf clump. This structural difference is useful, but the plant still needs positive identification. True lilies can be dangerous to pets, especially cats. Keep unidentified lily-like plants away from children and animals.

49. Sassafras (Sassafras albidum)

Region: Eastern North America. Where it grows: Woodland edges, old fields, and fence rows; it may form thickets of small trees.

Identify: A small to medium tree with several leaf shapes on the same plant: unlobed oval leaves, mitten-shaped leaves with one lobe, and three-lobed leaves. Crushed leaves or twigs may have a root-beer-like scent. Identify the tree from its leaves, twigs, bark, and growth habit together.

Eat: This book does not recommend eating sassafras in any form. Ground dried leaves (filé) are a traditional seasoning and thickener in Choctaw and Louisiana cooking, and root-bark tea has a long folk history; both are recorded here as history, not as recommended foods. Safrole, a major component of sassafras oil, caused cancer in animal studies and is not permitted as an added food ingredient in the United States, and US regulations treat sassafras bark or tea used to deliver safrole as adulterated food. No amount of safrole has been shown safe, and the leaves' lower safrole content compared with the root is not proof that a leaf preparation is safe. Prepare: No preparation is given.

Do / Don't: Do learn to identify sassafras — it is common, distinctive, and worth knowing on sight. Don't eat it in any form: no root tea, no distilled oil, no leaves as filé, and don't treat a small or occasional serving of any preparation as proven safe. The traditional history does not remove the safrole concern.

Look-alikes: The combination of variable leaf shapes and aromatic twigs is helpful, but no single leaf shape or smell confirms the tree. Use a regional tree guide and confirm the whole plant before collecting leaves.

50. Hawthorn (Crataegus species)

Region: Hawthorns occur across North America; Crataegus is a large and difficult genus. Where it grows: Old fields, woodland edges, hedgerows, and thickets.

Identify: A thorny shrub or small tree, often with lobed or toothed leaves, clusters of five-petalled flowers in spring, and small apple-like fruits called haws in fall. Fruits contain hard stones. Thorn length, leaf shape, flowers, and fruit vary among species. Confirm the plant as hawthorn using the whole tree or shrub, not the fruit alone.

Eat: Ripe haws from positively identified hawthorn species have traditional food uses. Prepare: Cook ripe fruit and strain the pulp to remove stones and tough material. The pulp can be used in sauces or jellies. Do not chew or deliberately crush the stones; as with other members of the rose family, seeds and stones may contain cyanogenic compounds.

Do / Don't: Do take the medication warning seriously. Hawthorn is used in some herbal products for cardiovascular purposes, but evidence for many claimed benefits is limited, and interactions with medicines are possible. Anyone taking heart or blood-pressure medication should consult their clinician or pharmacist before using hawthorn medicinally or eating it regularly as a concentrated preparation. A casual taste of a correctly identified fruit is not the same thing as medicinal use. Don't gather from sprayed hedgerows.

Look-alikes: Crabapples generally lack hawthorn's characteristic thorns, while chokecherry fruits form elongated clusters. These are supporting differences, not stand-alone tests. Identify the shrub from its leaves, flowers, fruit, and growth habit.

The poisonous gallery

Twelve plants this chapter will not feed you. Each one is here because it shadows something edible above — learn the shadow before you trust the plant it follows.

Water hemlock (Cicuta species) — A highly poisonous wetland plant found around marshes, streams, and ditches. It has divided leaves and clusters of small white flowers. Its roots may form chambers and tuber-like structures, but digging or examining a root is not a safe identification method. It shadows elderberry and other white-flowered plants in wet places. The poisonous compounds can cause severe, potentially fatal seizures. Rule: do not gather wild, white-umbelled plants from wet ground for food.

Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) — A tall, introduced biennial found on roadsides, waste ground, and stream edges. It has finely divided leaves, smooth stems often marked with purple blotches, and white flowers in umbrella-shaped clusters. It shadows yarrow and the wild-carrot family of confusion. All parts are poisonous; there is no safe food preparation. Rule: a smooth, purple-blotched stem with divided leaves is a serious warning, but never use the absence of blotches to declare a plant safe.

Death camas (Toxicoscordion species; formerly included in Zigadenus) — A grass-like plant with narrow leaves and clusters of pale, star-shaped flowers, growing from a bulb in meadows and open woods. It shadows edible wild onions and ramps because the bulbs can grow in similar habitats. Death camas does not have the characteristic onion or garlic smell of Allium, but smell alone cannot establish that another bulb is safe. Rule: never eat an unidentified wild bulb; learn the whole plant in flower before any harvest.

False hellebore (Veratrum species) — A poisonous plant of wet woods and meadows, with large, strongly pleated leaves that wrap around the stem. It can emerge in spring in the same habitats as ramps and may be mistaken for them. All parts are poisonous. Rule: broad, pleated leaves are a stop sign, not a plant to investigate by tasting or handling.

Canada moonseed (Menispermum canadense) — A climbing vine with grape-like leaves and dark berries, shadowing wild grape. It does not have grape tendrils, and its leaf stalk attaches to the underside of the blade rather than at the edge. Each fruit contains a single crescent-shaped seed. The berries are poisonous. Rule: identify a grape-like vine from its tendrils, leaf attachment, fruit cluster, and seed structure; never eat an unidentified vine's berries.

Deadly nightshade and other nightshades (Atropa belladonna) — Deadly nightshade has bell-shaped flowers and solitary, glossy dark berries on a branching plant. Other nightshade species also produce dark berries, and some are poisonous. They can shadow blueberry and huckleberry patches. Rule: dark berries must be identified to species from the whole plant, including flowers and leaf arrangement; "small and black" is not an identification.

Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) — A garden escape whose first-year rosette of soft, wrinkled leaves can resemble other broad-leaved rosettes, including burdock. Foxglove contains potent cardiac glycosides; an unmeasured amount can be dangerous. Rule: never dig an unidentified rosette. Know the plant through its full life cycle or leave it alone. Keep garden foxglove away from children and pets.

Pokeweed (Phytolacca americana) — A large plant with thick, often magenta stems and hanging clusters of dark purple berries. It can shadow elderberry and blueberry because of its fruit colour and clusters. Raw pokeweed is poisonous; the root is especially dangerous. Traditional preparations of young shoots require repeated processing, and mistakes have caused poisoning. This book does not teach that preparation. Rule: magenta stems and hanging dark-berry clusters are a warning; do not harvest pokeweed as food.

Poison ivy, poison oak, and poison sumac (Toxicodendron species) — These are not foods. Poison ivy and poison oak commonly have three leaflets, but "leaves of three" is only a warning rhyme, not a complete identification. Poison sumac is a wetland shrub with pinnate leaves and pale berries. The plants contain urushiol, which can remain on tools, clothing, and gloves. Burning them can release the irritant in smoke. Poison sumac shadows staghorn sumac, but the two must be distinguished by the whole plant, including habitat, leaflets, twigs, and fruit. Rule: learn to recognize these plants before foraging, avoid contact, and never burn them.

Giant hogweed (Heracleum mantegazzianum) — A very large, invasive member of the carrot family, with enormous leaves, a tall stem that may have purple markings, and broad clusters of white flowers. Its sap can cause severe skin injury when exposed skin is subsequently exposed to sunlight. It occurs in parts of British Columbia, including moist disturbed areas and stream corridors. Do not touch, cut, or weed-whack it; report it through the appropriate local invasive-species authority. Cow parsnip is a native relative and can also cause skin reactions. Rule: do not approach or handle suspected giant hogweed.

Blue flag and yellow iris (Iris versicolor and I. pseudacorus) — Wet-ground irises with sword-like leaves and distinctive blue or yellow flowers. Their rhizomes are not food and can cause poisoning. They shadow cattail because both grow in wet places and have underground structures. Cattail leaves and iris leaves differ in cross-section, but that is not a safe substitute for identifying the flowering plant. Rule: do not dig wetland rhizomes unless the plant has been positively identified in flower.

Yew (Taxus species) — An evergreen shrub or small tree with flat, dark needles and red, fleshy arils surrounding a single seed. It shadows pine and spruce because it is also an evergreen, but yew does not produce cones. The seed and most other parts of the plant contain toxic compounds that can disrupt the heart. Although the fleshy aril itself is generally described as non-toxic, the seed inside is poisonous and can be swallowed accidentally. Rule: treat the entire yew plant, including its red arils, as untouchable food material.

Not from a book

Camas (Camassia) bulbs can grow beside death camas bulbs, sometimes close together. The flower differences that help identify them are gone by the time bulbs are harvested. A page cannot make that harvest safe. Wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) belongs to a family that includes poison hemlock and other dangerous plants. A mistaken identification can be fatal. Its identification requires more than a flower resemblance or a single stem feature. These are learn-in-person plants: with a knowledgeable teacher, in the field, across a full growing season, or not at all. A book that claims to make them safe from the page would be lying to you. This book would rather leave two good foods out than print that lie.

The bulb rule, in full

Wild bulbs are where some of the continent's most dangerous plant-identification mistakes happen, because poisonous plants can grow beside edible ones and have bulbs that resemble food. Death camas (Toxicoscordion species, including Toxicoscordion venenosum) grows in meadows and other open habitats where people may also find wild onions and edible camas. Its bulb and grass-like leaves can resemble those of edible plants. It has caused serious poisonings, including cases where several people were hospitalised after eating it by mistake. All parts of death camas are poisonous. It can cause vomiting and diarrhoea, dangerously low blood pressure and heart rate, and potentially fatal poisoning.

A wild onion or garlic plant has a strong onion or garlic smell when its tissue is crushed. Death camas does not. That makes smell a useful warning test: if an onion-like plant does not smell of onion or garlic when freshly crushed, do not eat it. But smell is not a complete identification method. A smell alone does not prove that a plant is an edible Allium. Confirm the species using the whole plant, including its leaves, bulb and flowers, with a reliable regional guide. If the plant is not in flower and you cannot identify it positively, leave it in the ground.

The same caution applies to ramps (Allium tricoccum) and other wild garlics. Lily-of-the-valley (Convallaria majalis) can resemble ramps, and false hellebore (Veratrum viride) can also be mistaken for them. Both are poisonous. Do not identify ramps from leaves alone, and do not assume that a plant is safe because it grows where ramps are known to occur. Crush a fresh leaf and check for the characteristic onion or garlic smell, then confirm the other identification features. If anything does not agree, do not harvest it. Teach the full identification process to anyone you forage beside; do not teach smell as a stand-alone permission to eat.

And the standing rule still stands over the whole shelf: mushrooms remain the advanced class — in person, from a qualified local expert, or not at all. No entry in this chapter changes that.

Medicine plants: traditional, labelled, limited

Read this framing before any entry: these are traditional uses, not proven treatments that can replace medical care. A plant's strength varies with species, growing conditions, season and preparation. A traditional use is not proof that a remedy works or is safe. Where real medicine exists, it outranks the hedge (Chapter 13). Do not use wild plants to treat serious wounds, infection, fever, dehydration, poisoning or severe pain. Extra caution for children, pregnancy and anyone taking prescription medication: plant products can cause harm or interact with medicines. Do not stop prescribed treatment or substitute a plant remedy without qualified medical advice.

Plantain — the common lawn plantain is not the same plant as the banana-like fruit. Learn the exact species. Traditional uses include crushed leaves applied to minor skin irritation, but wild leaves are not sterile and should not be put into open wounds. Do not use a poultice as a substitute for cleaning and dressing a wound. Evidence for treating coughs or other internal complaints is limited.

Yarrow — traditionally used for minor complaints and sometimes applied to skin, but it is not a proven treatment for bleeding, wounds or fever. Correct identification matters: it has look-alikes, including plants with white flower clusters. Some people may have allergic reactions, particularly those sensitive to related plants. Avoid medicinal use during pregnancy unless a qualified clinician advises otherwise.

Willow bark — contains salicin-related compounds, but its content varies and it is not a measured or predictable substitute for aspirin. Do not make a homemade decoction with an assumed safe dose. Willow products may carry risks similar to salicylate medicines, including interactions and bleeding concerns. Avoid use by children and teenagers with viral illness, and by anyone with aspirin allergy, ulcers, kidney problems, bleeding disorders or relevant medication use unless a clinician advises otherwise. If appropriate medicine is available, use the labelled product and its instructions rather than improvising a plant dose.

Fireweed and rose — leaves and flowers have traditional uses in teas and food preparations, but that does not establish medicinal effectiveness or guarantee safety. Correct identification, clean harvesting and suitable preparation still matter. Treat them as optional food or tea plants, not remedies for illness.

What is deliberately not here: anything with a narrow margin. Foxglove contains compounds that affect the heart and can be dangerously poisonous. Comfrey has serious safety concerns, particularly when taken internally. A beginner's book earns trust by what it refuses as much as by what it teaches.

Preparation, in brief

Leaves and flowers may be made into infusions (hot water, steeped, covered). Some roots and barks are prepared as decoctions (simmered, longer), but preparation does not guarantee that a plant is safe or remove its toxins. Fresh leaves are sometimes made into poultices, but wild plant material is not sterile and should not be applied to open wounds. Everything dried goes into labelled containers — exact name, plant part, place, date and preparation — because an unlabelled jar of dried green is a riddle with stakes. Never use a small taste as an identification test. A tiny amount can still cause poisoning, and symptoms may be delayed. If someone may have eaten a poisonous plant, stop use, do not induce vomiting or try to neutralise it, and contact a poison centre promptly even if they feel well. Keep a sample or photograph and record the time and estimated amount. Call 911 for severe symptoms, collapse, seizures or breathing difficulty.

The learning path

Learn three plants a season, in person, until you can identify them reliably through their relevant growth stages. Grow useful plants where you can — a plant you've raised from seed can help you learn its appearance and life cycle, but cultivation does not replace correct identification. Keep the regional field guide where Chapter 18 says to keep the library. Check local harvesting rules before collecting: public land does not automatically mean plants are free to take, and parks or protected areas may prohibit harvesting. Respect local ecological limits and Indigenous harvesting protocols. This chapter is the map; the territory is outside, with a teacher, over seasons.

Chapter 11 — Wildlife: Meat from the Wild

Chapter 10's certainty rule has a brother, and it governs every word below: wild meat is safe only when the animal was healthy, the handling was clean and fast, and the cooking was thorough. Miss any one of the three and the wild stops being a pantry and becomes a lottery. Even all three together cannot eliminate every risk, including environmental contaminants and diseases that cannot be judged by appearance alone. This chapter assumes ordinary readers, not lifelong hunters — if you have never field-dressed an animal, your first time should be beside someone who has, on an ordinary day, not during an emergency.

The standing rules

Never eat an animal found dead. Not "fresh-looking," not "it was cold out." You cannot know what killed it or how long ago, and no amount of cooking makes an animal of unknown cause of death a reliable food source.

Never eat an animal that behaved strangely, looked sick, or was unusually easy to approach. Illness you can see is information; use it. A healthy appearance does not prove that an animal is free of disease, but visible signs of illness are reason enough to leave it alone.

Legality is part of safety while law still stands. Licences, seasons, tags and salvage rules exist in normal times, and Chapter 5's principle applies: behave as a citizen first. In a true collapse the calculus may change, but this book does not teach poaching as a plan, and an animal taken in panic by an amateur is a danger to the amateur as much as to the animal.

Keep it cold, keep it clean, keep it moving. Meat safety is a clock that starts at the kill: cool the carcass promptly, protect the meat from dirt and insects, and keep it at or below 40°F (4°C) as soon as practical. The time spent warm matters; there is no universal number of hours that makes every carcass safe. Do not leave a carcass warm while you finish other jobs. Chapter 12 turns a surplus into stores; this chapter's job ends with safe meat ready to cook or preserve.

Deer, elk and moose: the CWD chapter within the chapter

The large deer family is a realistic source of wild meat for people who know how to hunt and handle it — and the carrier of an important modern warning. Chronic wasting disease (CWD) is a fatal prion disease of deer, elk, moose and related animals. It has been found in many parts of North America. No human case has been confirmed, but scientists have not established that eating infected meat is safe for people. Other animal prion diseases have crossed species, so the official advice is not "don't worry" — it is specific.

Before hunting, check the current guidance from the state wildlife agency where you will hunt. In areas with CWD, testing recommendations and requirements vary by jurisdiction. Strongly consider having the animal tested before eating it, and follow any local testing rules. Results may take days or longer. Keep the meat chilled or frozen while waiting, clearly separated from other meat, and do not eat it until the result is available.

Never eat meat from an animal that tests positive, and do not eat an animal that looked sick, however it tests. If meat from different animals is pooled or mixed during processing, a negative result from one animal does not clear the others.

When dressing a deer-family animal, wear disposable or washable gloves, minimise contact with the brain, spinal cord, eyes, tonsils and lymph nodes, and avoid cutting through the spine. Use separate tools rather than kitchen knives and equipment. Prions are concentrated in certain nervous and lymphatic tissues, but prions have also been detected in muscle tissue. Wash hands and clean tools and surfaces carefully afterwards; ordinary cleaning is not a proven way to remove or destroy all prions.

Cooking does not destroy CWD prions. Heat is your defence against many bacteria and parasites; testing, avoiding sick animals and preventing cross-contamination reduce CWD exposure. That is why the rule is test-and-wait, not cook-and-hope.

For other venison risks, cook whole cuts and ground meat thoroughly. The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) lists 160°F (71°C) as the safe minimum internal temperature for game animals, including venison. Check the temperature with a food thermometer in the thickest part. Do not use colour or the absence of pink as your test: appearance does not reliably show whether meat has reached a safe temperature. Ground meat needs particular care because surface contamination can be mixed throughout during grinding.

Bear and wild pig: the trichinosis rule

Bear and wild pig are hunted for meat in parts of the continent, and they can carry Trichinella, the parasite that causes trichinellosis (also called trichinosis). Other wild animals, including some carnivores, can carry it too. Human outbreaks have been linked to undercooked bear meat, including meat that had been frozen for weeks.

Cook bear and wild pig to at least 165°F (74°C) throughout, verified with a food thermometer. Do not serve it rare or rely on the outside being seared. This is the more cautious temperature recommended by public-health authorities for wild game at risk of Trichinella. The general FSIS chart lists 160°F for game animals, but outbreak investigations and public health guidance specifically recommend 165°F for wild game because freeze-resistant parasites occur in these animals.

And the correction to a common belief: freezing does not make bear or wild pig safe. Some Trichinella species in wildlife are freeze-resistant; the freezing schedules used for domestic pork do not reliably work for wild game. Heat is the dependable kill step. Curing, drying, smoking or microwaving meat alone also does not reliably kill the parasite. Keep raw game and its juices away from ready-to-eat food, and clean grinders and tools thoroughly after use.

The same caution applies to wild feline and other carnivore meats — which this book does not recommend eating. Predators can carry parasites and other hazards, and there is little reason for an inexperienced person feeding a family to add them to the menu.

Small game: rabbits, squirrels and the glove rule

Rabbits and hares can carry tularemia, a bacterial infection sometimes called "rabbit fever." People can become infected while skinning or dressing an animal through cuts, contact with infected tissue, or other exposures; eating undercooked meat is another route. Wear gloves when handling rabbits and hares. Avoid animals that seem sick or unusually easy to approach. Unusual spots on the liver or other organs are a reason not to eat the animal, but their absence does not prove it is healthy. Cook rabbit and other game thoroughly; the FSIS chart lists 160°F (71°C) for game animals. Use a thermometer.

Squirrels are hunted for food in many places, but they are not automatically safe or legal to take. Local species, seasons, parasites and handling conditions matter. Apply the same glove, clean-handling and thorough-cooking rules.

Raccoon and opossum are eaten in parts of the continent, but they are not beginner choices. Both can carry parasites. Raccoons can carry a roundworm, Baylisascaris procyonis, whose eggs pass in faeces and can contaminate latrine areas. The eggs are not visible to the naked eye, and infection can cause severe illness in people. Wear gloves when handling raccoons and keep away from their droppings and contaminated soil. Cooking the meat does not make handling raccoon faeces safe. For an ordinary household with little experience, leaving these animals alone is the sensible choice.

Birds

Wild waterfowl and upland birds need the same temperature discipline as poultry: cook to 165°F (74°C) throughout. Use a food thermometer where possible. Handle raw birds as raw poultry — separate boards and utensils, washed hands, and no cross-contact with food that will not be cooked.

Birds found dead or dying belong to the standing rule: leave them. Avian influenza circulates in wild birds, and sick or dead birds can pose an infection risk. Do not touch or collect them. In normal times, report unusual clusters of sick or dead birds to the appropriate wildlife or animal-health agency. During an emergency, keep your distance and do not bring them home.

Fish: the advisory is part of the recipe

Fish are a wild food many readers may actually catch, and some risks are invisible. Mercury and industrial contaminants can accumulate in fish, depending on the species, size, age and waterbody. Larger predatory fish often carry higher mercury levels, but local contamination patterns vary. Every US state and many tribal authorities publish fish-consumption advisories. Check the advice for the specific water, species and size, with stricter guidance often applying to children and people who are pregnant or may become pregnant. Learn your local advisory on an ordinary day; it is as much a part of the recipe as cooking.

In Canada, check the relevant provincial or territorial fish consumption advisories, along with any local or Indigenous authority guidance for the water you are using.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork when a thermometer is not available. Cooking does not remove mercury or reliably remove industrial contaminants.

Freezing can kill certain parasites in fish when the fish is held at the required temperature for the required time. For example, FDA guidance for fish intended to be eaten raw includes freezing at −4°F (−20°C) or below for seven days, or specific colder, shorter schedules. A household freezer may not maintain the required temperature throughout the fish, and freezing does not remove chemical contaminants or make every fish safe to eat raw. This book's advice is to cook wild fish, full stop; raw preparations belong to controlled supply chains with appropriate parasite-destruction procedures.

Shellfish: the exception that punishes good cooks

Clams, mussels and oysters filter the water they live in, and some of what they filter in cannot be made safe by ordinary cooking. Certain algae produce marine biotoxins that accumulate in shellfish. Paralytic shellfish poisoning is one example. Ordinary cooking does not reliably destroy these toxins, and other shellfish toxins can also survive normal cooking. There is no dependable home test, smell test or preparation technique that tells you contaminated shellfish are safe.

The rule is strict: harvest shellfish only from waters officially open for harvest and covered by the relevant safety monitoring, and obey closures absolutely. Do not assume a beach is safe because the water looks clean or other people are collecting shellfish. During an emergency, monitoring and public health warnings may be interrupted; that makes unknown waters less trustworthy, not more. If you cannot verify that the area is open and safe, do not harvest there.

Raw oysters also carry a bacterial risk, including Vibrio. Cooking reduces many bacterial risks but does not neutralise shellfish biotoxins. For live clams, mussels and oysters, cook until the shells open and discard any that remain closed. Opening is a doneness sign, not proof that the shellfish came from safe water or is free of toxins. Shucked shellfish need thorough cooking as well.

The small print: frogs, turtles, insects

Frog legs and snapping turtle are traditional foods in parts of the continent. They require the same discipline: known species, clean handling and thorough cooking. Check legality before taking them; many jurisdictions regulate harvest, and some species are protected. Do not eat amphibians or reptiles that you cannot identify confidently, and avoid animals from polluted waters. Cooking cannot remove every environmental contaminant.

Insects are eaten safely by much of the world, but that does not make every wild insect edible. The beginner guidance is narrow: do not collect insects from pesticide-treated ground, roadsides or contaminated sites, and do not assume that grasshoppers and crickets are safe merely because they are common examples of edible insects. Remove hard or spiny parts where appropriate and cook thoroughly. People with shellfish or dust-mite allergies may react to insect proteins because of possible cross-reactivity. Do not experiment with wild insects if you have a serious shellfish or dust-mite allergy.

This is a footnote in the continent's food story, not a pillar of it, and the chapter treats it that way.

Roadkill

A deer killed by a vehicle at dawn can be sound meat by noon — or unsafe, depending on the time since death, temperature, injuries and contamination. You cannot reliably determine its safety from appearance alone. A ruptured gut, extensive damage, unknown time of death or warm conditions are strong reasons to leave it. Cooling promptly does not undo contamination that has already occurred.

Many US states have salvage-permit or reporting systems that allow some road-killed game to be collected legally. Rules vary by state and species. In Canada, provincial and territorial rules differ; a road-killed animal is not automatically yours to take. Learn your jurisdiction's rule before you need it.

A road is also a dangerous workplace. Do not stop where you cannot park well clear of traffic, be seen, and approach without risk. Do not stand in a live lane or let the hope of food override the danger. If the location is unsafe, drive on.

Chapter 12 — Preserving: Turning a Glut into Time

Every food in this book arrives with a clock on it. Chapter 1's shelf is the clock beaten in advance — food that was never going to spoil quickly. This chapter is for the other kind: the garden in August, the deer in October, the neighbour's apple tree all at once. Preserving is the set of techniques that trade work today for food in February. It is also the chapter where a mistake can be silent, sealed in glass, and lethal — so it is built, like Chapter 10, backwards: the danger first, the methods second, and one rule over everything.

Use tested recipes and current guidance, exactly. In the US that means the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP); in Canada, Health Canada guidance and the current tested canning guides. Processing times depend on the food, the jar size, the pack, the equipment and your altitude. This chapter teaches you which method a food needs and why — it deliberately does not print a table of times, because a time copied from a survival book instead of the current guide is how careful people get hurt. The guide is free, printable, and belongs on the Chapter 18 shelf next to the field guide.

The bacterium that runs this chapter

Botulism is caused by a toxin made by Clostridium botulinum. Its spores are widespread in the environment and can be present on fresh food. In a sealed container with moisture, low acidity and little oxygen, the spores can germinate and produce toxin if the food is stored under conditions that permit growth. The toxin can be present without changing the food's appearance, smell or taste. A jar can look perfect. Two facts organise everything below:

Spores survive ordinary boiling-water processing. Destroying them in low-acid canned foods requires the temperatures and processing conditions achieved by a properly used pressure canner, typically around 240–250°F (116–121°C). The required time depends on the food, jar size, pack and tested process. A pressure cooker or multicooker is not automatically a suitable substitute for a tested pressure canner.

Acidity limits growth. At an equilibrium pH of 4.6 or below, Clostridium botulinum cannot grow and produce toxin. That number is the key boundary between low-acid and acid foods, but it does not by itself establish a safe canning method. The recipe, ingredients, preparation and processing method still matter.

The toxin itself, unlike the spores, can be destroyed by sufficient heating. CDC advises boiling certain home-canned foods before eating them: at elevations below 1,000 feet, boil for 10 minutes, adding one minute for each additional 1,000 feet of elevation. This is an additional precaution, not a replacement for correct canning. Never taste food to test it, and never try to rescue food that was processed incorrectly or may be contaminated. Discard it safely.

The acid line: which road a food takes

High-acid foods — many fruits, jams and jellies, and pickles made to a tested recipe — can be processed in a boiling-water canner when the recipe specifies that method. The heat destroys many spoilage organisms; sufficient acidity prevents botulism bacteria from growing. The recipe determines whether the food qualifies and how it must be processed.

Low-acid foods — plain vegetables, meat, poultry, fish, seafood, beans, stocks and soups, and mixtures containing low-acid ingredients — must be pressure-canned using a tested recipe designed for that food. No exceptions, no family tradition, no "my grandmother water-bathed green beans." Grandmother's survivors are a biased sample.

Tomatoes stand near the boundary, and their acidity varies. When canning tomatoes by a tested method, add the specified amount of bottled lemon juice or citric acid, even if the tomatoes taste sour. Figs are also low-acid enough to require acidification when they are canned as an acid food using a tested boiling-water method. Follow the exact recipe and processing instructions. Do not decide by taste, and do not assume that adding acid to an untested low-acid recipe makes it safe for a water bath.

Fermented vegetables are a separate case. Fermentation can produce acid, but a ferment is not automatically shelf-stable or safe to can. Keep it refrigerated or process it only by a tested recipe that specifically provides for that product.

Altitude changes processing requirements. For boiling-water canning, higher elevations require longer processing times. For pressure canning, the required pressure adjustment depends on altitude and the type of gauge. Use the current guide's instructions for your exact elevation and equipment. Do not apply a generic adjustment from another recipe or location. Skipping altitude adjustments can leave food underprocessed.

Pressure canning, honestly

A pressure canner is not automatically interchangeable with a pressure cooker. Use a pressure canner recommended for home canning, large enough to hold at least four upright quart jars on its rack, and follow its instructions and the tested recipe. An appliance with a pressure-cook or "canning" setting is not a substitute unless current authoritative guidance specifically approves it for that process.

Dial gauges must be checked for accuracy at least once a year and whenever they may have been damaged. Cooperative Extension offices in the US may offer testing; if local testing is not available, follow the canner manufacturer's guidance for checking or replacing the gauge. In Canada, follow the manufacturer's instructions and current provincial or federal food-safety guidance. Weighted gauges must be used according to the canner's instructions and the altitude requirements for that model.

Use jars made for home canning, inspect them for chips and cracks, and use the correct lids and bands. Flat metal lids intended for one-time canning use must not be reused for another canning process. Reusable screw bands can be reused if they are not rusty, bent or damaged.

After processing, allow jars to cool undisturbed for the time specified by the guide before checking their seals. Check each seal after cooling and again before opening. A lid that flexes, leaks, or has lost its seal means the food must not be treated as shelf-stable. Do not taste it to decide whether it is safe. Store properly sealed jars in a cool, dark, dry place, with bands removed, and label them with contents and date — Chapter 1's labelling rule, with higher stakes.

Drying: the oldest road

Removing enough moisture prevents most microorganisms from growing, but drying does not automatically kill every pathogen. Use a tested method suited to the food and equipment. Fruit can be dried in a dehydrator or another tested setup; fruit leather should be dry enough to peel away and hold its shape. Herbs are generally dried quickly with good airflow and without excessive heat. Many vegetables are blanched before drying to preserve quality and improve the drying process; follow a tested preparation method for each vegetable.

For dried fruit, conditioning can help reveal uneven drying. Cool the fruit first, then loosely pack it in a clean, transparent container for about a week, shaking it daily. If condensation appears, return the fruit to the dryer. Discard food that develops mould or signs of spoilage. This conditioning step is not a substitute for drying food thoroughly or storing it correctly.

Meat is the exception with a rule of its own. For homemade jerky, heat meat to 160°F (71°C) and poultry to 165°F (74°C) before dehydrating. USDA recommends this wet-heat step to destroy disease-causing bacteria before the meat dries. A low-temperature dehydrator does not reliably bring the meat to a safe temperature; as moisture leaves, surviving bacteria can become more heat-resistant. Use a tested method, and do not assume that a dehydrator's dial proves the internal temperature of the meat.

The NCHFP also describes a specific post-drying oven treatment for suitable thin strips, but that method must be followed exactly; it is not permission to improvise a temperature or time for thicker pieces. Lean cuts are preferable because fat turns rancid more quickly. Trim visible fat, keep raw meat refrigerated during preparation, and avoid making jerky from wild game contaminated by gut contents during field dressing.

Properly dried homemade jerky is not automatically shelf-stable for months. NCHFP advises that properly dried jerky stored in a sealed container may keep at room temperature for about two weeks; refrigeration or freezing improves storage life. Do not treat a homemade product as equivalent to commercial jerky unless its process and storage conditions have been validated for that purpose.

Fermenting: managed rot, on purpose

Fermentation is preservation by encouraging desirable microorganisms to produce acids or other compounds that limit unwanted growth. Sauerkraut and similar fermented vegetables depend on the right salt concentration, preparation, temperature and time. Use a tested recipe and measure salt as directed. Some recipes specify salt by weight; others use different tested measurements. Do not assume that a general 2–3% range is suitable for every vegetable or fermentation method.

Keep vegetables submerged beneath the brine using a suitable weight and clean equipment. Follow the recipe's temperature range and fermentation time; "a cool place" is not precise enough for every ferment. Keep hands and utensils clean, and inspect the batch. Visible mould, unusual discolouration or other signs of spoilage are reasons to stop and discard the batch rather than skim the surface and assume the rest is safe. A clean, sour smell is consistent with many successful ferments, but smell cannot establish safety. Do not taste a batch that shows signs of spoilage.

Properly fermented vegetables can be stored for extended periods under the conditions specified by the recipe, often in a refrigerator or other consistently cold storage. Do not assume that a cold cellar is equivalent to refrigeration. Fermentation does not automatically make a food safe to pressure-can or water-bath can. After fermentation, can the food only if a tested recipe specifically provides a safe canning process for that product.

Freezing, curing, smoking, cellaring

Freezing is an effective preserver, but it depends on continuous cold and the grid or another reliable power source. Chapter 7 owns the outage clocks — roughly 48 hours for a full, closed freezer and 24 hours for a half-full freezer, provided the door stays shut. Those are planning estimates, not guarantees. For this chapter, frozen food is a bridge, not a storehouse, in any plan that assumes the power may fail for an extended period.

Curing and smoking need the same honesty. Salt, drying, temperature and time can all contribute to preservation, but smoke alone does not make meat shelf-stable. Smoked meat that was never properly cured or cooked is perishable food wearing a costume. Some tested curing methods use nitrite salts in carefully measured amounts to control hazards, including botulism in certain cured products. Other products use different validated processes. Do not invent a cure, guess at the amount of curing salt, or substitute ordinary salt for a specified curing ingredient.

Follow tested curing and smoking recipes precisely. Refrigerate or freeze the result unless the recipe's process and storage instructions establish that it is shelf-stable. Country-cured hams and similar traditional products require specific knowledge of the process, conditions and local climate. Treat them as a mentored skill, like Chapter 10's mushrooms, not as a shortcut to long-term food storage.

Root cellaring is another method: potatoes, onions, winter squash, apples and root vegetables each have their own temperature, humidity and ventilation requirements. Keep produce in appropriate conditions, inspect it regularly and remove spoiled items before they affect nearby food. Apples often store best separately from produce that is sensitive to ethylene gas. Potatoes need darkness to limit greening, and green potatoes should not be treated as ordinary food. The garden's harvest and Chapter 1's potatoes meet here: the cellar is where the harvest waits, and rotation — eat the oldest first, inspect as you go — is the management system. It works only when the storage conditions suit the crop.

When in doubt, throw it out

The last rule, and the one that makes the others survivable: a jar or package that bulges, leaks, spurts on opening, shows mould, smells off, or has lost its seal is discarded — untasted. Do not nibble to check. Do not feed it to the dog. Botulism toxin may be present without visible or detectable changes, so a normal-looking jar is not proof of safety. Never taste food from a jar that was processed incorrectly, has an unknown processing history, or otherwise raises a safety concern. Follow local guidance for safe disposal, keeping people and animals away from the contents.

The discipline costs a few jars a year, every year, forever. That cost is the method working.

Chapter 13 — Medicines: The Cabinet You Already Own, Audited

Every home already has a pharmacy. It's scattered across a bathroom cabinet, a purse, a junk drawer, and someone's bedside table — half of it expired, some of it unlabelled, and none of it findable in the dark at 3 a.m. This chapter is the audit that turns that mess into a kit: one afternoon, honest labels, and a clear-eyed look at the medicines a household actually depends on. It contains no dosing advice for prescription drugs — those belong to the people who prescribed them. What it gives you is inventory, continuity, and the truth about expiry dates.

The audit: one table, three piles

Gather everything — every bottle, tube, blister pack, and inhaler in the house — onto one table. Read every label. Sort into three piles:

Keep: in date, labelled, stored correctly, and you know what it's for and whose it is. Check that the packaging is intact and the medicine has not changed appearance in a way the manufacturer warns about.

Expired or damaged: set aside for proper disposal. Pharmacy take-back programs are available in many communities; ask your pharmacy how to return unwanted medicines. Do not flush them or put them in household garbage unless local disposal instructions say to.

Mystery: unlabelled, unrecognisable, or inherited from someone else's medicine cabinet. Do not use it. Ask a pharmacist if it can be identified safely; otherwise arrange proper disposal. A drug you can't name is a hazard wearing a cap.

Then count what the keepers actually are, against the people they'll serve, and write the inventory on paper: name, active ingredient, strength, quantity, expiry, and whose medicine it is. Include prescription medicines and important over-the-counter products. That sheet lives with the kit and a copy goes in Chapter 7's documents folder. In a crisis, nobody should be doing archaeology.

The core kit: what a household cabinet should hold

Plain store brands often contain the same active ingredients as name brands, but compare the active ingredient, strength, dosage form and directions on the label. Similar packaging does not mean interchangeable medicines. The working cabinet is built around the household, not a universal shopping list:

Pain and fever: acetaminophen and, where suitable, an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen are different tools with different cautions. Acetaminophen can seriously damage the liver in excessive doses and may be hidden in cold and flu combination products. Check every active ingredient to avoid accidentally doubling up. Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs can cause stomach bleeding and kidney problems, and may be unsuitable for people with kidney disease, ulcers, dehydration, certain heart conditions, those taking blood thinners, or during some stages of pregnancy. Ask a pharmacist which option is appropriate for each person. Children's products require the correct concentration and a dose based on the child's weight and the product instructions. Keep the measuring device and instructions with the bottle.

Allergy: a non-sedating antihistamine, such as a cetirizine or loratadine product, may be useful for ordinary allergy symptoms if suitable for the person. Sedating antihistamines such as diphenhydramine can cause drowsiness, confusion and other side effects; they are not automatically a good night-time choice, particularly for older adults, children or anyone using other sedating medicines. An antihistamine does not treat anaphylaxis. If someone has a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, keep it accessible, in date where possible, and follow their written emergency plan. Do not delay epinephrine to try an antihistamine. Emergency medical care is still needed.

The gut shelf: oral rehydration salts (packets, or Chapter 9's accurately measured recipe card) can help replace water and salts lost through diarrhoea and vomiting. An anti-diarrhoeal such as loperamide may be suitable for some adults with short-term, uncomplicated, non-bloody diarrhoea, but follow the label and do not use it when there is fever, blood in the stool, severe abdominal pain or swelling, or other warning signs without medical advice. It slows symptoms; it does not treat the cause. Antacids or a laxative may be useful for particular household members, but neither belongs in every kit by default. Check suitability and interactions with a pharmacist.

Wound and skin: sterile gauze, suitable dressings, adhesive tape and bandages, disposable gloves, and saline or safe water for rinsing are practical basics. Tweezers and trauma shears can be useful. Choose a skin antiseptic for use on skin according to its label; do not assume it is safe to pour into an open wound. Plain petroleum jelly can help with dry skin and chafing. Hydrocortisone may help some minor inflammatory skin problems, but it is not a general-purpose cream and may worsen some infections. An antifungal product is useful only when it is appropriate for the condition.

Instruments: a working thermometer — check it today; batteries die quietly — plus a notebook and pencil that live in the kit, because Chapter 14's medicine is half record-keeping.

Where it's relevant, said plainly: if opioids are present in the home or are a realistic risk in your community, a naloxone kit can reverse an opioid overdose temporarily. In the US, naloxone nasal spray (Narcan) is sold over the counter — no prescription needed — and many states and cities also hand out free kits. In Canada: naloxone is available without a prescription, and free distribution varies by province, territory and pharmacy. Ask a local pharmacy or public health service where to get it, and learn how to use it. Call 911 after giving it; its effect can wear off before the opioid does.

Prescription continuity: the fragile thread

Over-the-counter gaps are inconveniences. Prescription gaps — insulin, inhalers, heart and blood-pressure medicines, seizure and psychiatric medications — can turn a manageable event into a personal catastrophe. Honest tools, all available today:

Know your medicines properly: generic and brand name, strength, dose, schedule, what it's for, who prescribed it, and which pharmacy supplies it. Include allergies, important reactions, and any special storage or missed-dose instructions. One paper card per person, in the kit and with the person. In an emergency you may be telling this to a stranger, possibly for someone who can't speak.

Build a buffer with professional advice: refill prescriptions before they run out, but do not assume every plan allows early refills or that doing so will build several extra weeks. Coverage rules, controlled-drug restrictions, supply limits and medicine-specific rules vary. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber what lawful emergency supply or refill arrangements are available for each essential medicine.

Have the ordinary-day conversation now: ask your pharmacist and prescriber what to do if a disruption prevents you collecting a prescription. Some pharmacists have emergency-supply authority in defined circumstances, but the rules vary by state (and, in Canada, by province and territory). Do not assume a declared emergency automatically permits an unlimited refill.

Never borrow, share, or substitute. Someone else's similar-looking prescription is a different drug in a trench coat. If it's not yours, it's not medicine for you. Do not change doses, split tablets or substitute another product without checking with a pharmacist or prescriber.

Store it like it matters: follow the label and manufacturer instructions for temperature, light and moisture. A bathroom cabinet is often a poor choice because steam and temperature changes can damage medicines. Keep medicines in their original labelled containers where possible, secure from children and pets. Refrigerated medicines need a specific outage plan; a cooler and ice are not a guarantee that the required temperature will be maintained. Chapter 7 covers the power problem.

When the medicine plugs in

The audit above treats the cabinet as bottles and boxes. Some households keep a second pharmacy that runs on electricity: the CPAP on the nightstand, the oxygen concentrator in the corner, the powered wheelchair charging by the door, the insulin in the fridge. For those households a power cut is not Chapter 7's inconvenience. It can interrupt equipment or treatment that a person depends on, and it gets planned here, in the medicine chapter, with the same honesty as the antibiotics.

Start with the inventory — every device, written down: what it is, what it draws, what it runs on when the wall stops working, and how long that lasts. Do not plan from a generic wattage chart. Power draw and battery runtime vary by model, settings, accessories, battery condition, and how the device is used. A CPAP's heated humidifier or heated tubing can substantially increase its power demand; ask the supplier whether an approved setting or configuration can reduce consumption, and what that means for treatment. Do not change prescribed settings without the clinician's or equipment provider's advice. An oxygen concentrator is a different kind of load: it may need to run continuously, and a small battery pack may provide only a limited bridge. For oxygen, the plan may involve approved backup cylinders, a compatible battery system, a generator used safely, or relocation to a place with reliable power. Ask the oxygen supplier and prescriber, in writing, what your household's backup plan is, how long each source lasts at the prescribed flow, and what to do when it is running low. Never reduce oxygen flow to stretch a cylinder unless the prescriber has specifically approved that setting for you. The FDA publishes a plain- language booklet on preparing for and handling power outages for home medical devices. Print it and keep it with the device records, not just bookmarked.

Then do the three pieces of paperwork that only work in advance. Register with the power company: some utilities maintain medical-priority or life-sustaining-equipment registries. Registration may help the utility identify a household's needs or provide outage information, but it does not guarantee earlier restoration, advance warning, or continued power. Plan as if the power stays out anyway. Tell the local fire department or emergency registry, where one exists, that a person at the address depends on powered medical equipment. And write the evacuation trigger down before you need it: if the outage is likely to outlast the backup, you leave early — for a relative, a prearranged accessible place with power, or a medical facility when clinically necessary. Do not wait for the low-battery alarm. The household that leaves while the backup still has charge has time to make a safe transfer. The household that waits until the alarm has turned a plan into an emergency. If the person's condition is deteriorating or the equipment is failing, call emergency services.

Refrigerated medicines ride the same planning. Chapter 7 owns the fridge clock — about four hours unopened before the temperature may rise above safe limits — but the clock inside a particular medicine is set by its own label. What does this medicine's label actually allow? Insulin is the common case, and the answer is product-specific. Some commonly used US insulin products may be kept at room temperature for up to 28 days under specified conditions; others have different instructions. The label or pharmacist states the exact terms for yours. Two hard edges: insulin that has frozen must not be used, and heat or direct sunlight can reduce its effectiveness. A medicine whose label requires refrigeration and gives no room-temperature allowance needs a plan based on its actual storage requirements — a cooler, cold packs, and a thermometer may help, but do not let the medicine freeze or assume the cooler will hold the required temperature. When the outage is over, any refrigerated medicine exposed to unknown temperatures is a pharmacist, manufacturer, or prescriber question, not a guess. If a life-sustaining medicine such as insulin is needed and no replacement is immediately available, FDA guidance says it may need to be used until a new supply can be obtained; seek professional advice as soon as possible and replace temperature-compromised medicine when you can.

Mobility is the quiet entry on the list. A powered wheelchair or scooter is a device with a battery, a charger, and a person whose mobility may be severely limited when it dies: charge discipline on ordinary days, a manual chair or other backup mobility arrangement if one is suitable and available, and the vehicle's transfer plan practised, not theorised. The device card ties it together — one card per device, in the go-bag and on the fridge: model, serial number, supplier, their emergency number, the settings that matter, and what the backup is. Chapter 5's records rule, applied to the machines that keep a person breathing: paper doesn't need charging either.

Expired medicines: the honest version

An expiry date is the date through which the manufacturer guarantees the medicine's quality, strength and performance when it has been stored as directed. It is not a switch that turns a pill into poison at midnight. But after that date, you cannot assume the medicine still meets its specifications. Storage conditions matter, and appearance alone cannot prove that a medicine is safe or effective.

Pay particular attention to temperature-sensitive medicines. Insulin, some other refrigerated biologics, and certain liquid medicines can lose effectiveness if stored outside their specified conditions. Epinephrine auto-injectors and inhalers are also important medicines to keep in date and ready for use. Follow the product instructions and ask a pharmacist about a medicine that has been exposed to heat, freezing or other improper storage.

Do not plan around expired stock. The audit's keep pile is in date and properly stored. If an essential medicine is expired and no replacement is available during an emergency, get advice from a pharmacist, prescriber or emergency service as soon as possible. Do not assume it is safe, effective or interchangeable because it looks normal. In a life-threatening situation, follow emergency medical advice rather than simply withholding a necessary treatment.

Rotation keeps the question small: new stock to the back, old to the front, and the whole cabinet checked twice a year. Check storage conditions, packaging, quantities and expiry dates, not just the printed date. Five minutes twice a year can prevent an avoidable problem during a crisis.

Antibiotics: the hard caveat, in full

This is the section where the internet's survival advice goes most wrong, so it gets said plainly:

Antibiotics are not a household stockpile item for self-treatment. The right antibiotic depends on the infection, the person and the circumstances. The wrong drug can fail to treat the illness, cause side effects or delay proper care. Antibiotics do not treat viral infections. Take them only when prescribed for the person and condition, and follow the prescriber's instructions. If side effects or problems arise, ask the prescriber or pharmacist what to do rather than changing the course yourself.

Leftovers are not a supply. Do not save antibiotics for a future illness, take someone else's prescription, or use animal or aquarium antibiotics. Their identity, strength, quality and suitability may be uncertain, and they are not a safe workaround for medical care.

Most everyday illness does not call for antibiotics. Many common respiratory and stomach illnesses are viral, and diarrhoea can make dehydration the immediate danger. Chapter 9's oral rehydration solution is useful for replacing lost fluid and salts; it does not treat every cause of diarrhoea. Some bacterial infections are serious and need prompt medical assessment and appropriate antibiotics. Do not try to diagnose that distinction from a survival checklist. The glamorous drug is rarely the useful one.

Chapter 14 — Medical Emergencies: First Aid and the Edge of Home Care

First aid is a bridge, not a destination. Its job is to keep someone alive and as safe as possible until real medicine takes over — or, when the bridge is out, to carry them across the gap as well as a layperson honestly can. This chapter teaches the big, well-established moves and the judgment about where home care ends. It does not make you a medic, and it says so now: take a real first aid course on an ordinary day (Red Cross, St. John Ambulance — a weekend, once, with hands-on practice no book can give). What follows is what such a course would want already in your head when you arrive.

The first moves, at any scene

Safety first. A rescuer who becomes a casualty has helped no one. Live wires, fire, traffic, unstable structures (Chapters 4 and 15) — look before you touch.

Send for help early. Call emergency services if a phone works. Call 911 — it is the emergency number in both the US and Canada. If you cannot call, send a specific person to a specific place ("You — the fire hall — now"). Vague cries for help evaporate; errands get run.

The order of work: check responsiveness and normal breathing, and look for life-threatening bleeding. If catastrophic external bleeding is present, control it immediately while someone else calls for help. Otherwise, follow the emergency dispatcher's instructions for airway, breathing and CPR. There is no universal sequence that makes bleeding, airway and breathing separate, fixed stages in every emergency.

Bleeding

Direct pressure is the treatment. A clean pad — gauze, cloth, a hand if that's what's there — pressed firmly on the bleed and held. Do not keep lifting it to look. If blood comes through, add more material on top and maintain pressure.

Deep wounds may need packing: wound packing is for certain deep, severe wounds where a tourniquet cannot be applied, such as some junctional wounds. Use a haemostatic dressing or gauze and the technique taught in hands-on training. Do not pack the chest, abdomen or other body cavities. If you have not been trained, keep firm direct pressure and follow the dispatcher.

Tourniquets are for life-threatening limb bleeding that cannot be controlled quickly with direct pressure, or when the situation makes pressure impractical. Use a proper commercial windlass tourniquet if available. Place it above the wound, not over a joint, tighten until the bleeding stops, and record the time. Do not loosen or remove it. That is medical work. Improvised belts and straps are unreliable; use a commercial device and training where possible.

Internal bleeding has no bandage. After a blow or fall, worsening pain, abdominal swelling, weakness, dizziness, pale or clammy skin, confusion or collapse can signal serious internal injury. There may be no visible bruise. Treat it as an emergency and get medical help.

Shock

Shock is a life-threatening failure of circulation or oxygen delivery. It can result from blood loss, severe infection, serious allergic reaction, heart problems and other causes. The signs vary, but may include pale, cool or clammy skin, rapid breathing, weakness, dizziness, a fast pulse, anxiety or confusion. Do not wait for every sign to appear.

The layperson's job is to treat the cause you can safely reach, such as controlling bleeding, call emergency services, and keep the person warm. Help them lie in the position that is most comfortable and safest for their injuries and breathing. Do not routinely raise their legs; that can worsen some injuries or breathing problems. Nothing to eat or drink if serious injury, altered consciousness or possible surgery is involved. Watch for changes and record them in the notebook (Chapter 13). Follow the dispatcher.

Burns

Cool running water for twenty minutes — this is standard first aid for many burns. Do not use ice, butter, oils or creams. Avoid chilling the rest of the person, especially a child or someone with a large burn. Remove rings, watches and loose clothing near the burn before swelling begins, but never pull away anything stuck to skin.

Cover with a clean, non-fluffy dressing or sterile non-stick covering. Cling film can be laid loosely over a burn, but do not wrap it tightly around a limb or place it over the face. Leave blisters alone.

Get urgent medical help for deep, large or white/charred burns; burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals or major joints; burns that circle a limb; and chemical or electrical burns. Any breathing difficulty, hoarseness, facial burns or suspected smoke inhalation after a fire is an emergency. Do not wait for soot around the mouth or singed nose hair. For chemical burns, protect yourself, remove contaminated clothing and brush off dry chemical before flushing with plenty of water, unless the product instructions or emergency professionals direct otherwise. Call emergency services or poison control for advice.

Bones, sprains, and not moving people

Immobilise as found. Support an injured limb in the position found, using padding and a suitable splint only if you can do so without forcing it straight or causing more pain. Check fingers or toes beyond the injury for colour, warmth, feeling and movement. If a splint causes increasing pain, numbness or colour change, loosen it while keeping the limb supported. Do not try to straighten a deformed limb.

Sprains: protect and rest the injury; a wrapped cold pack may help with pain and swelling. Do not put ice directly on skin. Compression and elevation may help when appropriate, but avoid tight bandaging and stop if there is numbness, colour change or increased pain. An injury that cannot bear weight, looks deformed, or has severe pain needs medical assessment.

Suspected spine injury after a fall, crash or dive: avoid moving the person unless there is immediate danger, or movement is needed to protect their airway or provide lifesaving care. Support the head in the position found if possible, and keep talking to them. Do not let concern about a possible spine injury prevent necessary airway care. Chapter 4's road rules and Chapter 15's shutoffs run on the same caution.

Head injury

After a blow, worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, confusion, unusual behaviour, weakness, poor coordination, unequal pupils, loss of consciousness or difficulty waking are warning signs. Clear fluid from the nose or ear, or bruising behind the ear, also needs urgent assessment. Symptoms can appear or worsen later.

A suspected concussion or head injury deserves medical advice, particularly for a child, an older adult, or anyone taking blood thinners or with a bleeding disorder. Do not rely on a rule to simply watch someone overnight or wake them at fixed intervals. Follow professional advice about observation and return precautions. If they become difficult to wake, deteriorate, have a seizure or show other serious warning signs, call emergency services.

Choking

If they can cough forcefully or speak: encourage them to keep coughing and watch closely. Do not interfere with an effective cough.

If they cannot breathe, speak or cough effectively: call for emergency help. For adults and children over one year, give five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts, repeating until the obstruction clears or the person becomes unresponsive. If abdominal thrusts are not possible or appropriate, follow current first aid training for chest thrusts. If they become unresponsive, lower them safely and begin CPR. Remove an object only if you can see it; never perform a blind finger sweep.

Infants under one year are different: use five back blows and five chest thrusts, with the infant supported face-down for back blows and face-up for chest thrusts. Never use abdominal thrusts on an infant. Learn the technique in person.

Alone and choking: use self-administered abdominal thrusts or press your upper abdomen against a firm object such as the back of a chair. Call emergency services as soon as you can.

Heart attack and stroke

Heart attack: chest pressure, pain or discomfort may spread to the jaw, arm, back or shoulder. Shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, light-headedness or unusual fatigue can also occur. Symptoms vary; do not wait for the classic picture. Call 911. Follow the dispatcher's instructions. They may advise aspirin in some circumstances, but do not give it if the person is allergic, has been told not to take it, or the dispatcher advises against it. Do not delay the call to find aspirin.

Stroke: BE FAST. Balance trouble, Eyesight changes, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency services. Note when symptoms began or when the person was last known to be well. Sudden severe headache can also be a warning sign. Do not wait to see whether symptoms improve.

Unconscious but breathing normally: place them in the recovery position if it is safe to do so, and monitor their breathing. If a serious spine injury is possible, avoid unnecessary movement, but keeping the airway open takes priority. If they are not breathing normally, begin CPR and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

CPR, honestly framed

For an adult who suddenly collapses and is unresponsive and not breathing normally, call emergency services and start hands-only CPR if you are not trained or are unwilling to give rescue breaths. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, at about 100–120 compressions a minute. Let the chest come back up between pushes. Use an AED as soon as one is available: turn it on and follow its voice instructions. It will analyse the rhythm and only advise a shock when appropriate.

Hands-only CPR is not the whole answer for every casualty. Children, infants, drowning, choking and other breathing-related emergencies may need rescue breaths as well as compressions. If you are trained, follow your training and the emergency dispatcher's instructions. If you are not trained, the dispatcher can guide you. Do not delay starting compressions to find a perfect technique. This paragraph is not the training. The course is the training.

Anaphylaxis

Severe allergic reaction can become life-threatening quickly. Symptoms may include swelling of the lips or tongue, throat tightening, breathing difficulty, collapse, or widespread hives together with symptoms affecting breathing or circulation. Hives are not always present.

Use prescribed epinephrine immediately when anaphylaxis is suspected. Inject into the outer thigh according to the device instructions; it can generally be given through clothing. Call 911. Do not delay epinephrine to try an antihistamine. If symptoms persist or worsen, a second dose may be needed according to the person's emergency plan and device instructions, often after about 5–15 minutes. Keep the person lying flat if possible; if breathing is difficult, they may need to sit with legs extended. Do not let them stand or walk. Monitor breathing and be ready to begin CPR if needed. Emergency medical assessment is required even if they improve.

Diabetes and seizures: the two famous unknowns

Low blood sugar: shakiness, sweating, irritability, confusion or behaviour that looks like intoxication can be signs. If the person is awake and can swallow safely, give fast-acting sugar. About 15 grams is a common adult starting amount; recheck blood glucose after 15 minutes if a meter is available, and repeat treatment if it remains low. Children's needs may differ; follow their diabetes care plan. Give nothing by mouth if the person is drowsy, having a seizure or unconscious. If they cannot swallow safely, call emergency services. Use prescribed glucagon only if available and you know how to administer it. Do not give insulin to treat suspected low blood sugar.

Seizure: protect the head, move nearby hazards, cushion it if possible, and time the seizure. Do not restrain the person and put nothing in their mouth. When the seizure ends, check breathing and place them on their side if appropriate. Stay with them as they recover. Call emergency services for a seizure lasting five minutes or more, repeated seizures without recovery, a first known seizure, injury, breathing difficulty, a seizure in water, pregnancy, or diabetes.

Poisoning

In the US, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 for suspected poisoning. Keep the number with your emergency contacts. In Canada: contact your provincial or territorial poison centre; the number varies by location. In Quebec, call 1-800-463-5060. If the person is unconscious, having a seizure, struggling to breathe or in immediate danger, call 911 instead.

Do not induce vomiting unless poison control or emergency professionals specifically tell you to. Do not give food, drink, medicines or home remedies unless instructed. Have the product container available and be ready to provide the person's age and weight, what was taken, how much, and when. If fumes are involved, move to fresh air only if you can do so without exposing yourself. Carbon monoxide is the poisoning that hides as flu — Chapter 8's section owns it.

When home care is no longer enough — the list

Print this one in your head; it's the chapter's spine. Get emergency help for: bleeding that will not stop; chest pain or suspected stroke; anaphylaxis; severe or large burns; serious head-injury signs; shock; severe dehydration or a child who is becoming unusually sleepy, limp or difficult to wake; poisoning with serious symptoms; deep animal or human bites; serious eye injuries; amputations and crush injuries; and any patient getting worse despite appropriate care.

Seek prompt medical advice for injuries or symptoms that are not immediately life-threatening but still need assessment, including suspected fractures, possible concussion, persistent vomiting or diarrhoea, and wounds that may need closure or infection prevention. When you hand over, hand over the notebook: times, symptoms in order, what you did, and the medication card. A written timeline is real medicine's favourite gift.

When help is delayed

Call anyway. Tell the dispatcher where you are, how many people are injured, what happened, and what access problems responders may face. Follow their instructions and keep the line open if they ask you to. If it is safe, send someone to meet responders and guide them in.

Keep monitoring the person. Record changes, treatments and times. Keep them warm and as comfortable as possible. Do not give food or drink to someone with a serious injury, altered consciousness or a possible need for surgery. Do not move someone with a serious injury unless there is immediate danger or movement is necessary for airway care or lifesaving treatment. Do not attempt invasive procedures or treatments beyond your training. If the person's condition changes, tell the dispatcher.

Chapter 15 — Fire, Flood, and Storm

Forget the exotic disasters for one chapter. These three — fire, water, and wind — arrive every year in ordinary postal codes. They can force people out of their homes, sometimes with little warning. They share one spine: the same first decision (leave early when it is safer to do so), the same knowledge of household shutoffs, and the same disciplined way of coming back. One chapter, three weathers.

Fire: the two-minute disaster

The house fire. Smoke and toxic gases can incapacitate people before flames reach them. Modern furnishings can produce dangerous smoke and gases, but every fire behaves differently. Fire services warn that you may have as little as two minutes to escape after an alarm sounds. That is a warning about how quickly conditions can become unsurvivable, not a guaranteed countdown. The rules are built for speed:

Out, then out stays out. Everyone, immediately, by the nearest safe exit. Plan two ways out of each sleeping area where possible, and make sure everyone knows how to use them. A window is only a second exit if it can actually be opened and reached safely. Nothing is fetched. Do not re-enter for pets, phones or belongings. Tell firefighters if someone or an animal may still be inside.

Close doors behind you if you can do so without delaying escape. A closed door can slow the spread of heat and smoke. Keep bedroom doors closed at night where practical.

Feel a door for heat before opening it. If it is hot, do not open it; use another exit if one is safe. Stay low under smoke. Once out, go to the meeting point (Chapter 6) and call 911 from outside or a neighbour's phone.

Smoke alarms: install them on every level and outside sleeping areas, test them monthly, and replace batteries or the alarm according to the manufacturer's instructions. Check the alarm's manufacture or replacement date. Do not assume an alarm will give you two full minutes.

The wildfire. Wildfire risk varies by region and season, but when one threatens, the language matters. An evacuation alert means prepare to leave; an evacuation order means leave immediately. Follow the instructions from the authority responsible for the evacuation. An order is not a suggestion, and do not delay departure to finish preparations. The craft is in preparing before either stage:

Leave early if anyone in your household needs extra time — people with mobility limits, small children, older adults, animals, or a vehicle or trailer that takes time to prepare. Do not assume an alert gives you a reliable window. Roads can close, conditions can change, and authorities may issue an order without much notice.

Embers can travel ahead of a wildfire and ignite roofs, decks, vegetation and other materials. Keep gutters clear, move firewood and combustible clutter away from walls, and maintain screens and vents as recommended by local fire authorities. These steps reduce risk; they do not make a home fireproof.

Smoke is its own emergency (Chapter 8). A well-fitting N95 respirator can reduce exposure to smoke particles, but it does not filter harmful gases and is not a substitute for leaving an unsafe area. A portable HEPA air cleaner can reduce indoor particles when used in an appropriately sized room. A box-fan filter may help in some circumstances, but its effectiveness depends on the fan, filter, fit and room. Reduce outdoor exertion when smoke is heavy and follow public-health advice.

One-road towns: know the alternate routes identified by local emergency authorities. Do not assume a logging road or private road is passable or safe. Officials may close roads or direct traffic elsewhere (Chapter 4). Keep enough fuel to leave when conditions require it; do not wait until a fire is approaching to refuel.

Flood: the patient disaster

Never walk or drive through floodwater — Chapter 4 has the figures, and they are warnings, not safe-depth thresholds. Water can hide washed-out roads, debris, open drains and electrical hazards. Turn around.

Electricity and water are enemies: if flooding threatens, switch off power only if the electrical panel is dry, safely accessible, and you can reach it without entering water or approaching an electrical hazard. Once water is present around the panel or electrical equipment, stay away and contact the utility or emergency services. Do not touch wet electrical equipment.

Rising water brings Chapter 9 with it. Floodwater may contain sewage, chemicals, fuel, sharp debris and other contaminants. Keep it off skin where possible, cover wounds, wash with soap and safe water, and keep children and pets away. Treat food, medicines and porous materials exposed to floodwater as contaminated. Follow public-health instructions for cleaning and disposal.

Sandbags are a wall of time, not of safety — they can help divert shallow water from doorways when correctly placed, but they are not a reliable barrier against serious flooding. They are heavy, slow work. Do not delay evacuation to build one, and never let them persuade you to ignore an order to leave.

The return has a mould clock: wet materials can begin supporting mould growth within a day or two, depending on conditions. Photograph damage before cleanup if it is safe to do so (Chapter 5's insurance file). Start drying and cleanup promptly when authorities say it is safe. Do not assume a wall or floor is dry because its surface looks dry. Flood-damaged buildings may need professional assessment.

Storm: wind, lightning, and the winter kind

Lightning: when thunder roars, go indoors — into a substantial building or a hard-topped enclosed vehicle — and stay there for at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. Avoid plumbing, wired electronics and corded phones during the storm. A vehicle is a fallback, not an invitation to shelter under a tree or in an open shelter.

Tornado / extreme wind: move to a basement or the lowest floor, into a small interior room away from windows. Protect your head and neck with your arms, a mattress or sturdy covering. Mobile homes offer poor protection; go to a designated sturdy shelter before the storm arrives if authorities advise it and you can get there safely. Do not open windows. Do not shelter under an overpass. If caught outside with no sturdy shelter, follow local emergency guidance; a vehicle is not reliable tornado protection.

Winter storm: protect exposed pipes when freezing conditions threaten. Insulate vulnerable pipes and follow local utility advice. A tap left dripping can keep a pipe from freezing. It does not replace insulation, and it is no substitute for knowing where the main water shutoff is. If a pipe bursts, shut off the water if you can do so safely and arrange repairs.

Stranded in a car: if there is no immediate danger from traffic, fire or another hazard, staying with the vehicle usually makes you easier for rescuers to find and gives some shelter. Keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow and debris. If you run the engine for heat, do so intermittently, check the tailpipe repeatedly, and keep a window slightly open. Carbon monoxide can build up even when the vehicle appears to be ventilated. Never run the engine in an enclosed or partly enclosed space. Follow Chapter 8's carbon monoxide rules.

The shared spine: shutoffs and the grab order

Every adult in the house should know, from an ordinary-day tour, where the electrical panel, water main, and gas shutoff are. Know which shutoffs can be operated safely and when to leave them alone. If you smell gas or suspect a leak, leave immediately. Do not operate switches, appliances or anything that could create a spark. Call the gas utility or emergency services from a safe location. Do not delay evacuation to shut off gas. Do not turn natural gas back on yourself; the utility or a qualified professional must handle restoration. Follow local utility instructions.

The evacuation grab order starts with people. Take animals, essential medicines and medical equipment if they can be collected without delaying escape. Documents, chargers and the go-bag come next only when there is time. During a house fire, leave immediately; do not stop to collect belongings or work through a checklist.

Before any of it: hardening and the temporary repair

Everything above this section is response. This section is the work done in the calm season that can make the response smaller — an afternoon of hardening may save a great deal of recovery work, and it is the part of this chapter you can schedule.

Defensible space, for the fire side, in the zones used by Firewise and many fire agencies. The first five feet from the house is the immediate zone: keep it as ember-resistant as practical. Remove combustible mulch, firewood, dead vegetation, and other easily ignited material from this area. Gravel, stone, or bare soil can be useful choices. Do not treat any one surface as a guarantee; attached decks, fences, steps, and other features can carry fire toward the building. From five to thirty feet, reduce the amount and continuity of vegetation and other combustible material. Keep grass maintained, remove dead material, and space plants so fire is less able to travel from one to another. Prune branches where appropriate, but do not use a universal six-to-ten-foot pruning height as a rule for every tree. From thirty to a hundred feet, where you have that much property, continue to reduce accumulated dead material and manage vegetation and spacing. Adapt the work to local vegetation, slope, property boundaries, and fire-agency guidance.

Then the house itself: clear leaves and needles from gutters and roof valleys, and keep debris away from vents. Embers can enter through vulnerable vents and openings. Some agencies recommend noncombustible metal mesh with openings between 1/16 and 1/8 inch, or listed ember-resistant vents. Do not simply cover every vent with fine mesh: it can reduce required attic or crawlspace ventilation. Check local building requirements and use a qualified contractor where needed. Mesh reduces some ember entry risks; it does not make a house ember-proof.

The temporary-repair kit is bought in the same calm season, because the hardware store may sell out of useful supplies before a major storm: a tarp large enough for likely small repairs, rope, suitable hold-downs, and the fasteners or battens recommended for the tarp and roof type. Staples and nails alone may tear out under wind load. In storm country, plywood cut and labelled for likely openings can be useful if it can be stored flat and dry. Buckets, towels, and a wet vacuum are the indoor half of the same kit. Leak isolation starts with the shared spine above — you cannot stop water you cannot shut off — and continues with knowing which rooms sit below which pipes, and what moves up, off the floor, first.

The safety law of temporary repairs is short. Never get on a wet, icy, or damaged roof. Never work alone. Never during the storm. A tarp in wind can catch like a sail, and a fall from even a low roof can cause serious injury or death. Do not climb onto a roof to make an emergency repair. If the damage cannot be reached and secured safely from the ground, the repair waits, buckets go under the leak where safe, and the insurer from Chapter 5 hears about it promptly. Use a qualified roofing contractor for roof work.

And the list of what is not a household repair at all: a smell of gas (leave the house, avoid switches and flames, and call from outside — this chapter's fire rules own that response), anything inside the electrical panel beyond simple operation of a breaker when the area is dry and safe, structural damage — sagging ceilings, cracked supports, a wall that has moved — sewage contamination (Chapter 9's territory), and any tree touching a power line, which belongs to the utility and qualified line crews, not you.

Last, the honest limit of the whole section: a tarp is a temporary barrier, not a fix. It may reduce further water entry until a proper repair can be made, but it does not guarantee protection for weeks. Water that has already entered a wall or floor assembly can support mould growth; drying and remediation depend on the materials, amount of water, temperature, and how quickly the area is dried. Chapter 9 covers the sanitation and cleanup side. A temporary repair that becomes permanent by neglect is how a storm in October becomes a demolition in spring. The hardening hour and the tarp are how a household buys time. What the time is for is the real repair.

Coming back, all three weathers

Wait for the official all-clear from the responsible authority (Chapter 5). That does not automatically mean every building or utility is safe. Return only when permitted, and stay away from downed power lines, damaged electrical equipment, gas leaks, unstable structures and contaminated standing water. Assume downed lines are live. If you smell gas, leave and call from a safe place. Do not enter a flooded or fire-damaged building until authorities say it is safe; a qualified inspection may still be needed.

Photograph damage before cleanup when it is safe. Do not touch or switch on wet electrical equipment. Do not restore gas service yourself. Nothing you own is worth the second accident — the one that happens to tired people cleaning up the first. And expect the strange flat feeling after; Chapter 22 is about that weather too.

Chapter 16 — Fallout: Time, Distance, and Shielding

Here is the best news hidden inside the worst scenario in this book: radiation from nuclear fallout decreases with time. The dust that is dangerous soon after a detonation generally gives off much less radiation by day three and less again by week two. That does not create a safe exit time. The practical priorities are getting inside quickly, staying sheltered, and following incident-specific official instructions. Ordinary people in ordinary buildings, doing unglamorous things correctly, can reduce their exposure substantially. That is not comfort-talk. It's physics, and this chapter is the arithmetic.

What fallout actually is

Nuclear fallout is radioactive material — including pulverised earth and weapon debris — drawn into the atmosphere by a nuclear detonation and falling back to the ground. After a surface or near-surface burst, fallout can begin arriving within minutes to hours, depending on the event, weather and distance. It may settle over the hours that follow. The particles can look like dust, but you cannot identify radioactive material by sight, smell or touch.

A nuclear detonation and a nuclear power plant accident are not the same event. A reactor accident can release radioactive material through different pathways, over a different period, and with a different mixture of radioactive substances. The 7:10 decay rule in this chapter applies approximately to the radiation dose rate from nuclear weapon fallout after a detonation. It is not a universal rule for every radiological emergency. Follow instructions specific to the incident.

There are three related dangers: external exposure, when radiation reaches your body from material outside it; external contamination, when radioactive material is on your skin, hair or clothing; and internal contamination, when radioactive material is inhaled, swallowed or enters through a wound. The actions overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Getting inside reduces external exposure. Removing contaminated clothing and washing reduces contamination. Avoiding contaminated food and water helps prevent internal exposure.

The three defences, in order of power

Time. Radiation dose rates from fallout generally decrease quickly at first. That's the next section, and it is the most hopeful arithmetic in this book.

Distance. Put as much distance as practical between yourself and radioactive material outside. The simplest household version is to get into the building's interior, away from exterior walls and the roof. A basement usually offers better protection than an exposed upper floor.

Shielding. Mass between you and the radioactive material reduces exposure. Earth, concrete, brick, water and books can all contribute. A basement surrounded by earth and the rest of the building is usually a strong shelter. In an apartment without a basement, choose an interior room away from the roof and outside walls. You can add useful mass with existing furniture or stored supplies, but do not stack heavy objects overhead or create a risk of collapse. You're not building a bunker. You're borrowing the mass your building already has.

The 7:10 rule — the chapter's namesake

For nuclear fallout, a useful approximation is that for every sevenfold increase in time after the detonation, the radiation dose rate falls by about tenfold. This is a rule of thumb, not a measurement of your particular location.

Worked plainly from the one-hour dose rate:

At 7 hours, the dose rate is about 1/10 of the one-hour rate.

At 49 hours, about 1/100.

At 343 hours — roughly 14 days — about 1/1,000.

These figures describe the dose rate at those times, not the total radiation dose accumulated by a person over the interval. Actual conditions vary with the weapon, burst, weather, fallout distribution and location.

Read it carefully: the clock tells you fallout is weakening. It does not tell you when it is safe to go outside. The first hours matter greatly, but there is no universal safe exit time. This is why the standard public guidance is simple: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.

The first hour: getting in clean

If you are outside when a nuclear detonation has occurred or an official warning says fallout may be approaching:

Get inside the best substantial building reachable quickly. Do not wait outside to see dust or work out which way the wind is blowing. If home is far away, use the nearest substantial building. A basement or a central room in a large building is preferable when available, but getting indoors is the immediate priority.

Before you settle in: remove outer clothing. Take off your outer layer and shoes carefully near the entrance. Removing the outer layer can remove up to about 90% of radioactive material carried on clothing, reducing contamination — it does not undo radiation exposure already received. Do not shake the clothing. Put it in a plastic bag, seal it, and keep it away from people and animals, preferably outside the main living area.

Wash. Shower with lukewarm water and soap or shampoo if available. Be gentle; do not scrub hard or abrade the skin. Do not use conditioner, which can make radioactive material harder to remove from hair. Avoid getting contaminated water into cuts. If there is no shower, gently wipe exposed skin and hair with a damp cloth. Put used cloths in a bag with the clothing. Follow official advice about water use for washing.

Pets come in too. Bring them indoors promptly. If their fur may be contaminated, wash them gently with soap or shampoo and water, rinse thoroughly, and prevent them from grooming themselves until clean. Do not delay getting yourself and others inside to carry out an elaborate pet-cleaning routine.

Setting up inside

The shelter spot: choose the most central, best-shielded room available — a basement area first, then an interior room on a lower floor, away from exterior walls and the roof. Use existing mass around you. Move heavy supplies or furniture only if it can be done safely; do not pile heavy objects overhead.

Everything you need comes into the room now: stored water and food (Chapters 1 and 2), the sanitation kit — yes, the bucket toilet of Chapter 9, because nobody should go outside for a toilet while sheltering — the radio (Chapter 6), lights, medicines and notebook.

Reduce outside air while fallout is passing: close windows and doors, shut fireplace dampers where safe, and turn off fans or ventilation systems that draw outdoor air inside, if you can do so safely. Use tape and plastic to reduce obvious drafts if needed. This is not a reason to make a room dangerously airtight or to ignore ventilation instructions for days. Follow official advice about when to ventilate or use heating and cooling systems again.

The stay: plan to remain inside for at least 24 hours unless officials give different instructions. They may advise sheltering longer, depending on measurements and conditions. Do not leave simply because 24 or 48 hours have passed. Fire, structural damage, medical emergencies or another immediate life threat may require you to move; otherwise, follow official instructions. This is Chapter 3's 72-hour supply plan doing its finest work.

Food and water under fallout

Radioactive contamination is not the same as food becoming radioactive through exposure to radiation. Fallout particles can settle on food, surfaces and water, and radioactive material can enter the food chain through plants, animals and water. Sealed, stored food and water kept indoors are generally the safer choice. Wipe or wash the outside of packages before opening them.

Cans and packages: clean the outside before opening so contamination does not transfer to the food. Do not eat fresh produce or other food that was exposed outdoors during fallout unless authorities say it is safe. Follow official advice about locally produced milk, meat, vegetables and other food.

Open water sources — rain barrels, open tanks, ponds and surface water — may be contaminated. Use protected stored water and follow local instructions about drinking water. Boiling does not remove radioactive material. Do not assume ordinary household filters make contaminated water safe.

Potassium iodide: the honest paragraph

Potassium iodide (KI) protects the thyroid from radioactive iodine. It does not protect the rest of the body, does not block other radioactive materials, and does not remove radioactive iodine that has already been absorbed. Taking it when it is not needed can cause harm, particularly for people with certain thyroid conditions or other medical considerations.

The rule is simple: take KI only when public-health or emergency officials specifically advise it, and use the dose they recommend for your age and circumstances. It is not a general radiation shield and should not be taken pre-emptively on rumour. If you keep it, store it with its instructions in the medicine cabinet (Chapter 13), like any other serious medicine.

Going out again

Do not plan routine trips outside after 24–48 hours. The dose rate may have fallen substantially, but local conditions can still be dangerous. Stay sheltered until officials say it is safe to leave, or until an immediate threat makes remaining inside more dangerous. Follow announced evacuation routes and instructions rather than guessing from elapsed time or a map.

If officials direct you outside before conditions are fully clear, cover exposed skin and use a suitable mask or respirator if advised and available. An N95 can reduce inhalation of particles; it does not shield your body from external radiation or guarantee protection from every contaminant. Keep trips as short as practical. When you return, remove outer clothing carefully, bag it, and wash exposed skin. Children should remain sheltered with everyone else unless official instructions or an immediate danger require movement.

Signs, records, and myths

Acute radiation syndrome is not simply the flu. After a high dose, symptoms may include nausea, vomiting, weakness and other effects, with timing that varies by dose and exposure. Vomiting can have many causes; its timing may help medical professionals assess exposure, but it cannot diagnose radiation sickness by itself. Feeling well does not prove that exposure was harmless. Record the exposure timeline, symptoms and times in the notebook (Chapter 13), and seek medical advice when available.

Myth: you're doomed if you were caught outside. No. Getting inside quickly, removing contaminated clothing and washing can reduce further exposure or contamination. They cannot erase a dose already received, but they still matter.

Myth: a consumer Geiger counter is essential. It is not necessary to follow the basic shelter instructions. A suitable, functioning radiation meter can provide useful information, but readings require interpretation and the instrument must be appropriate for the radiation being measured. Do not leave shelter because of an unfamiliar reading. Follow official monitoring and instructions.

Myth: special suits, special diets, special anything. The unglamorous triad — time, distance and shielding — is the practical foundation. No consumer product replaces sheltering and official guidance.

Chapter 17 — Something Hits Us

This is an unlikely chapter to be needed, but it deserves its place in the book. Movies have taught this scenario almost completely wrong — all fireball, no aftermath. The real event can create two very different problems an ordinary person can understand: near the impact, it is a natural-disaster problem; far from it, a sufficiently large impact can become a climate and food-supply problem. The chapters you already own still apply, but the scale and duration may be very different. Between those two, there is one rule demonstrated by a real modern event that could have prevented many of its injuries.

The rehearsal: Chelyabinsk, 2013

A space rock roughly 17–20 metres across entered the atmosphere over Russia and exploded high above the ground, at roughly 23 kilometres altitude. The airburst damaged buildings and injured around 1,500 people, mostly through flying glass and other debris. It did not strike the ground. The blast wave broke windows; people who had gone to look at the bright flash were injured by the glass.

The flash arrived before the pressure wave. Depending on distance, that gap can be seconds or minutes, but there is no guaranteed warning interval. So, rule one of this chapter, worth remembering:

A flash like a second sun means get AWAY from windows, not toward them. Move away from glass, drop low, cover your head and eyes, and wait for the pressure wave and falling debris to pass before moving. Do not stand at a window to watch what happens. It is the same flash rule again, because the same instinct — get away from the flash and protect yourself — matters.

Near the strike: a familiar disaster in a strange costume

An airburst and a ground impact are not the same event. An airburst can produce intense light, heat, a pressure wave and flying debris, without a crater. A ground impact can produce a crater, extreme heat, ejecta, blast damage, fires and ground shaking. The effects depend heavily on the object's size, speed, composition, angle and where it arrives.

Near a major impact, the hazards can arrive together: collapsed structures and ground damage (Chapter 15), fires (Chapter 15 again), dust in the air and on everything, and infrastructure disruption (Chapters 6 and 7). Your chapters still apply, but no household plan can guarantee survival close to a sufficiently large impact. The damage zone may be extensive, and the consequences depend on the event and the geography. The book will not pretend that preparation can overcome every scale of disaster.

The dust: ash discipline

Impact dust and volcanic ash can both create serious problems, but their composition and hazards depend on the event. Do not assume impact debris is harmless or that it is radioactive. Follow local health and emergency instructions.

Keep it out of lungs and eyes: use a well-fitting N95 or other suitable particulate respirator if available, and goggles or protective glasses. Fine dust can irritate or damage the eyes and respiratory system. An N95 filters particles; it does not protect against gases or every chemical hazard. Avoid stirring dust up unnecessarily.

Keep it off roofs: ash and debris can accumulate, and wet deposits can add substantial weight. Whether a roof can carry that load depends on its construction and the amount and type of material. Do not climb onto a roof to clear it unless authorities or a qualified professional have determined it is safe. A fall or roof collapse is not worth the attempt.

Keep it out of water: if ash fall is forecast and you collect roof water, disconnect the downspout before the material arrives, if you can do so safely. Keep stored water covered and sealed. If ash or debris gets into a water supply, use another safe source and follow public-health instructions. Boiling does not remove chemical contamination or radioactive material.

Machines hate it: fine dust can clog air filters, damage engines and interfere with cooling systems and solar panels. Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions. Avoid dry brushing or using high-pressure water where that could drive particles into equipment or damage it.

Far away: the long grey — impact winter

A sufficiently large impact can throw dust, aerosols and other material into the atmosphere, depending on the impact site and the material involved. Some scenarios could reduce sunlight, disrupt rainfall and temperatures, damage growing seasons and put food supplies under severe pressure. The duration and severity would depend on the event. It is not honest to promise that every large impact means years of cold summers, or to predict the exact consequences from a household handbook.

No household out-prepares that alone, and this chapter won't lie to you about it. But look at what the far-field could demand, because you've been reading it all book:

Calories, managed with arithmetic, not panic (Chapter 1): stored staples stretched deliberately, food stocks tracked on paper, and household needs calculated early. Sprouting can provide fresh greens, but it does not replace calorie-rich food. Raw sprouts also carry a foodborne illness risk; cook them thoroughly, especially when serving children, older people or anyone medically vulnerable.

Growing under a dim sky (Chapter 10): cold frames and greenhouses can protect plants from wind and cold, but they do not create sunlight. Grow lights may help, but they require reliable electricity and can consume substantial power. Seed saving matters when plants can still be grown successfully and allowed to produce viable seed. A saved seed is a possible future crop, not a guarantee of next year's food.

Firewood and warmth at scale (Chapter 8), protected water (Chapter 2), and disease prevention (Chapter 9) — the unglamorous essentials, sustained for much longer than a few weeks.

And the honest centre of it: at this scale the unit of survival is not just the household. It is the street, the town and the region: who has seed, who has a well, who can repair equipment, and how food and other essentials can be produced and shared. Chapter 19 is next for a reason. A prolonged food crisis is a community problem, and the time to build those relationships is before the sky does anything.

What the sky-watchers are doing while you read

One more honest calibration: astronomers have found and tracked more than 90% of near-Earth asteroids estimated to be larger than one kilometre. Smaller objects — including those large enough to cause serious regional damage — are less completely catalogued. NASA has reported no known asteroid larger than 140 metres with a significant chance of hitting Earth in the next century, but that does not mean every object has been found or that the risk is zero.

In 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately changed the orbit of the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos. It worked: the orbital period changed by about 33 minutes. That demonstrated a method of planetary defence, not a universal solution. Deflecting an object depends on detecting it early enough and having the time, technology and mission capability to act.

The realistic personal case is the small, unwarned object — a Chelyabinsk-sized arrival with little or no useful warning. For that one, the whole of your immediate preparation is: away from the glass, then your chapters.

Chapter 18 — When the Machines Are the Problem

The earlier chapters have covered disasters caused by weather, water, fire, disease and human decisions. This one is about the layer we have quietly moved into our houses: the assistants, the automations, the feeds, the maps, the payment systems, the portals that hold our medical records and our money. That layer can fail, it can mislead us, and it can be turned against us. Its worst failures share one shape: the tool everyone reaches for stops deserving trust at exactly the moment everyone reaches for it.

This chapter is not about killer robots. It is about dependency, deception and one rule that governs both.

1. The machines we depend on

Do the inventory honestly and it's longer than anyone expects: navigation, banking, two-factor codes, work logins, health records, prescriptions, the smart lock, the thermostat that sulks without its server, the car that wants an update.

Most of it runs on the cloud — a plain-spoken way of saying that your data and services depend on computers, networks and organisations somewhere else, under someone else's control. The fragility isn't that any one of these is necessarily weak. It's that they can share dependencies — the same networks, platforms, identity systems, update channels and service providers — so several can fail together, on an ordinary Tuesday, with no villain at all.

In July 2024, a faulty software update from a cybersecurity company caused millions of Windows systems to crash. Airlines cancelled flights, hospitals experienced disruption, and businesses and public services around the world struggled to operate. It wasn't an attack. Some systems recovered quickly; others took days to restore. That is the rehearsal for this chapter, and it needed no AI to happen. Add AI — faster, more widely used, and woven into more services — and the same dependencies can acquire new ways to fail.

2. When AI becomes the problem

Four flavours, in rising order of strangeness:

AI in the hands of attackers. A cloned voice on the phone — "Grandma, it's me, I'm in trouble, don't tell Mom" — can be built from a short sample of someone's voice and used in a convincing scam. This is happening today, not a prediction. The countermeasures are old-fashioned: a family code word, a hang-up and a callback using a number you already know, and a household rule that money, account access and secrets never move on a voice alone. AI can also help attackers write convincing phishing messages, impersonate organisations and create fraudulent websites.

AI exploited sideways. Attackers can try to poison the information people find during an emergency: flooding search results and feeds with fabricated instructions, fake evacuation directions, false closures or bogus public notices. These are plausible ways to exploit a crisis, not a claim that every disaster is already full of them. The fake page looks like help. That is the point of it.

AI errors with consequences. A language model can invent a mushroom species, misread a medicine dosage chart, omit a warning or combine incompatible instructions. It may present the result with perfect grammar and total confidence. It does not reliably know when it is wrong. This is Chapter 10's certainty rule at machine scale: for anything where wrong can hurt someone, the AI's answer is a lead, never a verdict.

Cascades. Systems trusting systems: trading algorithms amplifying a dip, logistics software rerouting deliveries around a phantom closure, or an automated system acting on a bad forecast. These are possible failure patterns, not a prediction that every automated system will fail together. Real systems may have human review, limits and manual overrides; the danger grows when those safeguards are missing, ineffective or overwhelmed, especially when services share the same failed infrastructure. Your defence at household scale is the whole book: the less of your survival depends on a single digital route, the more options you retain when that route fails.

3. When nobody knows how to do it manually

The quietest failure is skill fade. Drivers who cannot navigate their own town without a voice telling them. Clerks who cannot make change when the register dies. Offices where the manual process was deleted as inefficient and the one person who remembered it retired. Institutions call this efficiency until the day it's called an outage.

The household version of the fix is woven through this book and worth naming here: paper fallbacks for the ten critical things — a contact list, medication cards (Chapter 13), a paper map that covers home, important documents and instructions (Chapter 7), account recovery information stored securely, and this book itself — plus manual reps: drive a familiar route without the voice sometimes; cook from the paper recipe; keep the skills in Chapters 8 and 10 alive by using them on ordinary weekends.

Keep the practical things that still work without a screen: a way to unlock the door if its smart system fails, instructions for operating essential equipment manually, and a way to reach important people without relying on one app or account. Do not put passwords, PINs or account-recovery codes in an ordinary documents folder. Store them securely, with access arrangements your household understands.

A skill you only own through a screen is a skill you're renting.

4. The information disaster

When any voice, face, document or video can be fabricated convincingly, the scarce resource stops being information and becomes trust. A fake evacuation order in a cloned official's voice, a fabricated boil-water notice from the "city," a manufactured rumour about a bridge being closed — each could be cheap to make and expensive to unmake.

The defences are Chapter 6's, hardened:

Channels chosen in advance beat channels found in the moment. Know which local radio stations, official websites, alert systems and emergency agencies are responsible for information in your area. Use channels you identified before the emergency, not an unfamiliar page that appears during it.

Verify when time allows; don't delay urgent safety action. Follow an official emergency alert or direct instruction from the responsible authority. For rumours, unusual requests, unofficial instructions or information that could cause a major decision, look for independent confirmation through a channel you already trust. Check where the information originated, not just how many places repeated it.

Radio is another route, not a truth detector. A broadcast can remain useful when internet access is down, but radio stations can also make mistakes or relay incomplete information. Know which broadcasters carry local emergency information and compare instructions with the responsible authority when possible.

The scam shape has familiar notes: urgency, secrecy, and a demand for money, credentials, account access or personal information. Those are warning signs, not a mathematical test. Whatever costume it wears — disaster, romance, authority — stop and verify through a channel you already know. Chapter 20 picks this up from the human side; Chapter 22, from inside your own head.

5. Build your offline library now — the constructive heart

Here is the chapter's turn from warning to building, and it is one of the most useful afternoons in this book. The internet's knowledge is available now. Take a copy.

What to gather: an offline encyclopedia — Wikipedia's offline editions, available through readers such as Kiwix, can put a large reference library on a computer or storage device. Choose an edition that fits your available storage; a complete version with images can be very large. Add a proper medical reference written for laypeople, such as Hesperian's Where There Is No Doctor. Include regional field guides (Chapter 10), first-aid and repair manuals, the manuals for your own furnace, stove and vehicle, local emergency plans, water and food-safety guidance, maps, and this book in paper form. Add current information for nearby pharmacies, clinics and hospitals. Save useful material as PDFs or plain text where possible, and label files with their edition or publication date.

The AI's place in it — librarian, never oracle. A modest model running locally on a capable computer, with a retrieval system set up to search your documents, can be a useful tool: it may find relevant passages, summarise instructions and help translate. But it must actually be connected to the documents, and its answers still need checking against the original text. A local model is not automatically able to read everything on your device. Nor will it know about new closures, advisories or updated guidance after the internet is gone. The documents are the asset; the model is the card catalogue. If the model vanished tomorrow, the library must still work — and a library that only works through a chatbot is a dependency wearing a costume.

Two copies, separate places. Keep a working copy on your main device and a second copy on a USB drive or other storage in a different safe location. Two copies in the same bag are not much protection against losing the bag. Use formats that will outlive their apps — PDF and plain text over anything proprietary where practical. And print the life-critical pages: the instructions you may need with a dead battery at 3 a.m. — medicine information, the oral rehydration solution recipe, shutoff instructions and emergency procedures — belong on paper in the kit. Check that the printed material is current and appropriate to your country, equipment and situation.

6. Powering your knowledge

A library is only as useful as the power available to read it. The knowledge station can be modest: an e-reader uses little power and can hold a large collection; a phone can read documents; an older laptop offers more flexibility but usually draws more power. Actual battery life depends on the device, battery condition, screen brightness, workload and age.

A small solar panel and battery bank may help, but they do not keep a one-hour-a-day routine running forever. Output depends on panel size, sunlight, season, weather, shade, orientation, battery capacity and competing household needs. In prolonged cloudy weather or winter, the available power may be very limited. Test the equipment under realistic conditions and decide what gets priority.

Medical devices and essential communications come before optional computing. Keep reference files on more than one device if possible, and print the pages you cannot afford to lose access to. Test the whole setup periodically — boot the machine, open the files, check the battery and run the librarian if you have one — because untested backups are a genre of fiction.

7. Using AI with the internet gone

Practical rules for the librarian on your shelf:

Ask it about your documents. "Find the section on water storage and walk me through it" — grounded questions can help you find useful material. Ask it to show you the passage it is summarising, then open and read that passage in the original document. A librarian who won't produce the book is telling stories.

Never rely on it for an identification or a dosage. No plant, mushroom, medicine or chemical verdict from the AI alone — Chapters 10 and 11's rules, repeated here because this is where they'll be most tempting to bend. The reference helps you find the page; it does not make an unsuitable or outdated reference safe.

Expect fluency, not knowledge. It can sound just as sure when it is wrong. That is not malice; it's the machinery. Price it in.

8. When the AI is wrong — and the rule that governs it all

It will be wrong: calmly, plausibly, sometimes about things that matter. The disagreement protocol is the rule this whole chapter hangs on, and it belongs in a box, because boxes are for rules you follow when you're too tired to improvise:

THE AI RULE AI is allowed to be wrong. It is never the only copy of anything. It is never the only source for a decision where being wrong can hurt someone. When the AI disagrees with the original reference, trust the reference until the disagreement is resolved.

An honest footnote, because this book practices what it preaches: this chapter was itself drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed against references, with the author named on the cover making the final call. That is not proof that every claim is independently verified; the source list and editorial checks matter too. The method is simple: the machine drafted, the documents decided, and the author answered for it.

Chapter 19 — People: The Infrastructure That Isn't Made of Machines

Time to correct the movies' biggest lie, which isn't the fireball or the fallout — it's the crowd. Film says disaster turns people feral by Tuesday. The record is considerably less cinematic. In disaster after disaster, the same pattern holds: widespread panic is rare. People commonly look for family and neighbours, help strangers, share resources and organise themselves while formal responders are still making their way in. In many emergencies, the people already on the street are the people available to act first.

The lone wolf is not the survivor archetype. The lone wolf is the one with no one to notice he's missing. This chapter is about the actual infrastructure: each other.

Know your street before you need it

Preparedness that fits in a head: the social map of your street. Who might need help checking in. Who has the baby, the generator, the truck, the chainsaw, the well, the nursing experience, the radio licence (Chapter 6), the garden that feeds half the block (Chapter 10). You don't build this map with a clipboard and an intense stare. You build it the way it has always been built — names learned, waves exchanged, a ladder borrowed and returned, snow shovelled one driveway past your own. The ordinary courtesies are the preparedness; the disaster just reveals the account balance.

Groups that already exist are the fast lane: the street's group chat, the hall committee, a faith community, school parents, the American Legion, a volunteer fire department or local community organisation. Joining one in peacetime is a social act. Discovering you needed one in a crisis is a logistics problem. Choose the social act.

A social map is not a public register. Ask before recording or sharing personal details. Do not circulate someone's diagnosis, medication, absence from home or equipment needs without their consent. Share emergency information with the people they have chosen to involve. Knowing a neighbour may need a hand is useful; exposing their private life is not.

Everyone is carrying something

A working street runs on the discovery that nobody is only a burden. That is a statement about human worth, not a demand that everyone prove their usefulness. People contribute in different ways, and some will need help without being able to offer a task in return.

The retired nurse may have valuable experience, but she is not a clinic, and experience does not replace professional medical care. An older neighbour may have decades of gardening, preserving and doing-without in their head — Chapter 10's knowledge with a face — and may be able to teach or advise from a chair. Teenagers may be able to help with age-appropriate tasks, but they are not emergency personnel. The person who checks everything twice may be excellent at keeping records, if they want the job. Nobody gets assigned a role because of their age, disability, job title or personality.

In an event, the organised households do this deliberately: a skills-and-needs round in the first days — who needs what (medicines, formula, a ride, a safe way to keep temperature-sensitive medication like insulin — Chapter 13), who has what (tools, transport, training, time), and what people are willing and able to do. Ask rather than assume. Keep the information limited to what the group needs, and share personal details only with consent. It feels formal for exactly ten minutes, and then it feels like the lights coming back on.

The second pair of hands

The skills-and-needs round above finds out who needs medicines, formula, a ride. This section is about the households where the need is not a thing but a person — where someone is fed, lifted, turned, suctioned, calmed, or kept safe by another human being's hands, usually the same hands every day. That arrangement is the most load-bearing and least redundant system in this book. It has one part, and the part gets sick, injured, stuck across town, and — in the version nobody plans for — becomes the patient.

So every cared-for person gets a named second pair of hands: a backup caregiver who has agreed, out loud, on an ordinary day, before any emergency. Not "the family will figure it out" — a person, with a name, who has been inside the routine at least once while the regular caregiver watched. They do not need to provide every part of the usual care independently. They need to know what they can safely do, what they must not attempt, when to call for help, and how to keep the person safe while arranging the next layer of support. For some care, that requires training or more than one backup. A promise to help is not the same as knowing how.

The sheet is one page, written with the person it describes — they direct their own care, and the sheet is their instructions in their words wherever they can give them: medicines and when, devices and how (Chapter 13's cards are the annexes), lifting and transferring the way that works for this body, food and swallowing, communication — how this person says yes, no, pain, and fear — and what calms and what frightens. Include the practical warning signs that mean the backup should call the regular caregiver, clinician, or emergency services. The sheet lives where the backup can find it at 3 a.m. without asking anyone: a copy with the backup, a copy in the house, a copy in the go-bag. It is also, handled honestly, private medical information; it is shared only with the person and the people they have authorised, except where disclosure is necessary to obtain emergency help or otherwise required by law.

And the plan runs in both directions. The backup sheet exists for the day the caregiver is the one in the ambulance — but it also exists for the ordinary version, the caregiver with flu, because care that cannot pause for a sick day is care that fails in week one of anything. Chapter 22 covers what the long haul does to the person giving the care; this section is simpler and earlier: nobody is the only person who knows. That sentence, made true on paper and in practice, is the whole section.

Running a street: the small machinery

The check-in round. Agree in advance how neighbours want to be checked on and how often. A walk down the street, a phone call, a radio check or a note may work, depending on the household and the circumstances. Some communities use a simple window card to show that someone is OK or needs help, but do not assume everyone knows the system or wants private information displayed. No response is a reason to try the agreed contact or seek help if there are signs of immediate danger — not permission to force entry.

Shared work, split properly. Water runs, wood, cooking and practical checks: rotas, not vibes. Communal cooking can save fuel compared with several separate cooking setups, depending on the appliances, meal and number of people. Plan it safely: combustion appliances belong outdoors or in the location specified by the manufacturer, never inside a home, tent or garage. And the cookpot is where a street becomes a community (Chapters 1 and 7 already did the fuel arithmetic; this chapter collects the dividend).

A communication tree (Chapter 6): one person receives an update from officials, then passes it to a few agreed contacts, who pass it along. Trees can reach the last hundred metres when broadcasts cannot. But the tree is a delivery system, not an authority. Pass on the original source and time of the message, and send corrections through the same tree if information changes.

The dependents plan, made with people, not for them. The person with a disability, medical device or mobility limit is the expert on their own needs. Ask them, in peacetime, what support they want and write down their plan: backup power, a safe evacuation option, a neighbour who has agreed to help, or a second set of supplies somewhere accessible. Utility registries for customers who depend on powered medical equipment may provide an additional notification or planning route, but eligibility and services vary, and registration does not guarantee power or priority restoration. Make a backup plan that does not depend on one neighbour or one vehicle. The same applies to older people: share medication details only with their consent and the person they choose. Dignity is a design requirement, not a garnish.

Giving, receiving, and the boundaries of both

Two truths that hold at once. First: generosity is load-bearing. Households and streets can become more resilient when people share skills, time and resources, because cooperation turns neighbours into a crew. Second: give from surplus, and keep the household's core intact. A family that gives away its essential medication has not performed a kindness; it has created another emergency. The grown-up version is quiet, sustainable generosity — the extra meal, the charged battery, the spare hands — plus fairness machinery when things get scarce: portions based on need, discussed openly and handled by people the group trusts.

Keep track of shared supplies and contributions. Decide how essential items will be allocated before tempers rise. Medically necessary medication and equipment are not general communal stock. And receiving is a skill too: the person who cannot accept help without shame may hide an emergency until it becomes a recovery. Letting others give is a contribution; on a long enough timeline everyone is both.

Children, briefly, because they're people in this chapter too: routine helps — meals, learning time, play, familiar responsibilities and honest answers in small pieces (Chapter 22 will say more). Give them tasks that are safe and appropriate to their age, but do not make them responsible for adult emergencies or household survival. A child is not luggage, and childhood is not an unpaid emergency service.

When the street argues

Scarcity plus fear plus rumour (Chapter 22's weather) can create friction: whose generator, whose turn, whose cousin got extra. The street craft is small and early: talk face-to-face while it's still about the thing, not the person; explain the decision; write down shared agreements; and, where possible, ask someone acceptable to everyone to help keep the discussion fair.

Not every street has a trusted neutral, and not every disagreement is a simple information failure. Some conflicts involve competing needs, unequal power, prejudice or exploitation. Do not force a mediation process on someone who feels unsafe. If no one nearby can help, look beyond the street: a tenants' group, community centre, local volunteer organisation, school network or established mutual aid group may offer a safer point of contact.

And when a situation stops being a dispute and starts being a threat, that's Chapter 20's territory, and it begins with distance, not debate.

Chapter 20 — Other People, the Dangerous Kind

Chapter 19 was the rule; this is the exception, and it gets a chapter for one reason: so that if the exception ever knocks, nobody in your house has to improvise. Read the proportion first and keep it: disasters do not automatically turn neighbours into predators. Treat the most repeated stories of rampant violence after Hurricane Katrina as a warning about stories: several of the worst proved exaggerated or unsupported when they were checked afterwards. Meanwhile, mutual aid and ordinary acts of help received less attention. Fear can make the exception look like the rule. The dangers covered in this book's other chapters — water, cold, infection, accidents — deserve at least as much attention. But rare is not never, and the strategy for the dangerous kind is unglamorous and nearly total: don't be a target, don't be there, and don't turn an incident into a war.

Seeing trouble at the right distance

Trouble worth the name announces itself as a pattern, not an incident. But a pattern is a reason to reassess, not proof that you know what someone intends.

Unofficial control points. Someone collecting "tolls" or "taxes" at a bridge, a well, or a road junction may be imposing control without lawful authority. Real officials may not always have tidy paperwork or an obvious chain of command, especially during an emergency. Don't try to settle that question by challenging them. Demands for money, supplies, personal information, or access in exchange for passage are warning signs. Keep your distance, avoid arguing, and verify through an established channel when it is safe.

Escalation markers: repeated visits, demands becoming more intrusive, attention shifting from what you have to who is inside, or contact becoming threatening. Visits at night or involving intoxication can add concern, but no single behaviour proves dangerous intent. Look at the pattern and make decisions based on safety, not on winning an argument.

Rumour is not pattern. One story about a gang three towns away is Chapter 22's weather, not intelligence. Verify rumours and unofficial claims when time allows (Chapter 6). But don't delay an immediate safety decision while trying to confirm a threat. Multiple people repeating the same story are not necessarily independent sources.

The discreet household

The master strategy is never becoming interesting:

Don't advertise. Supplies are private: no bragging, no posts about the stockpile, no trading from a visible mountain of goods. Deliveries and errands stay small and ordinary-looking where practical.

Blend, don't blackout. A house glowing with light and generator noise among dark ones can attract attention. But a single dead-dark house on a lit street can look unusual too. Don't run a generator unnecessarily, and never compromise its safe placement or operation to hide it. Noise and exhaust remain hazards whatever the hour. Keep ordinary household lighting where practical; don't treat camouflage as more important than safety.

Your garbage testifies (Chapter 9's staging, seen from the other side): packaging from plenty, read by strangers, can advertise what came into the house. Discreet waste discipline is security discipline, but don't let it create a sanitation problem. Keep waste contained and follow local collection or public-health instructions.

The friendly questions. "So who all's staying here? Got a generator in there? You folks set for food?" — asked warmly, by a stranger, in week two. Curiosity is human and most of it is innocent; the answers still aren't owed. Practise the pleasant non-answer now: "We're getting by, like everyone. How's your street doing?" Warmth offered, inventory declined.

Movement and the leave decision

Risky errands — water, medicine, the far food source — deserve planning. Daylight, batching errands, and travelling with someone you trust can be sensible defaults, but they are not guarantees. A busy route is not automatically a safer route. If the street's pattern has changed, reassess before leaving; don't follow a routine just because it worked yesterday.

Chapter 4's framework decides the hard one: a threat that is approaching while you still have a safe destination and a usable road may be a reason to leave early. But don't turn one alarming detail into an automatic departure rule. Someone knowing your name, stores, or address is not, by itself, proof that you must leave immediately. Consider what has actually happened, whether the threat is credible, whether you can leave safely, and where you would go. If departure is the safer choice, take it while it remains a choice, not a sprint. If the route is unsafe or blocked, secure the household as best you can, seek help through a safe channel, and reassess when conditions change. An official evacuation order still takes precedence; if you cannot comply safely, contact emergency services or local authorities for help.

If it happens anyway: the confrontation craft

Everything here serves one ranking: people, then dignity, then property — in that order, never reversed.

Give the thing. If someone is robbing you for property, don't fight over objects. Police safety advice commonly favours avoiding resistance over replaceable goods. Keep your hands visible, move slowly, and say what you're doing before reaching for anything. Avoid sudden movements, lectures on fairness, or attempts to win the encounter. Leave when it is safe. No advice can make a robbery predictable, and protecting property is not worth gambling with a person's life.

At the door: speak through it if you can. One household voice. Don't open up just to let someone look around, and don't confirm who or how much is inside. You don't owe a stranger an explanation or access. Be polite if it helps keep things calm, but don't mistake courtesy for an obligation to comply with an unsafe demand.

An armed group, if it ever comes to that, is not a negotiation exercise to practise from a book. Don't challenge, threaten, or bargain aggressively. Keep your movements slow and visible, avoid sudden gestures, and follow instructions where doing so does not create an immediate danger. Don't volunteer information about other people or households. Disengage or leave if a safe opportunity presents itself. No technique makes an armed encounter predictable. What matters is protecting people, not pride.

Afterwards: write it down — times, what happened, and descriptions, but only if doing so is safe (Chapter 13's notebook habit). Report it to the appropriate emergency service, authority, or aid organisation when available and safe. Tell trusted neighbours what they need to know, without spreading unverified identities or details that could put someone at risk (Chapter 19's machinery). Adjust the household's posture. And the rule that closes every incident in this chapter: no retaliation. No revenge, pursuit, threats, or attempt to settle the score. Reporting an incident and protecting your household are not retaliation. They are sensible steps. The contest you decline is the one you win.

The disaster con

Predation wears a clipboard more often than a mask. Fake officials collecting "registration fees," contractors demanding deposits for repairs that evaporate, charity collectors for charities that don't exist, the too-helpful stranger offering to "guard" your empty house. All of it runs on Chapter 18's chord — urgency, secrecy, money — and all of it deserves the same response: slow down, verify identity and authority through a channel you choose independently, and don't hand over money or personal information just because someone appears at your door. A badge, uniform, logo, or caller ID is not proof. A legitimate contractor may require a deposit, but pressure to pay immediately, unverifiable identity, no written agreement, or demands for cash are reasons to stop and check. Don't let urgency choose the person you trust.

What this chapter refuses to teach

The next chapter takes up self-defence, weapons, and life under an occupying force — as law, safety, storage, and judgment, because those are household subjects. What neither chapter teaches is fighting technique — and no traps, here or anywhere in this book: booby traps can maim the wrong people, including children, neighbours, responders, and the person who set them. They can also expose you to serious legal consequences. The law varies by jurisdiction; don't assume a trap is lawful because someone is trespassing. No castle doctrine cosplay. Not because the book is squeamish, but because the strategy here is discretion, distance, de-escalation, and departure. The households that "won" confrontations make the documentaries. The households that never had one make it home.

Chapter 21 — When Force Comes: Defending Your Household and Living Under Occupation

Every chapter before this one assumes the threat has no plan. Weather doesn't want anything from you. A fire isn't negotiating. Even Chapter 20's dangerous people are individuals — frightened, greedy, or cruel, but making it up as they go.

This chapter is about the other kind of threat: force that is organized. It comes in two sizes. The small size is the crew, the gang, the armed group that has decided your street is a resource. The large size is a state — a foreign government exercising authority over your town after its forces have taken control, or any army that stays and governs. The household answers to both with versions of the same craft, and the craft is not combat. It is law learned early, a home made harder to enter, a household that is hard to hurt and boring to watch, records kept for a later accounting, and bright lines decided on an ordinary day instead of improvised at a checkpoint.

Two honest limits before anything else, because this book does not sell fog. First: a book cannot teach you to fight, any more than Chapter 14 can teach you surgery. Where skill matters, this chapter points at in-person training and says so. Second: nothing here makes a household a match for an army, and any book that implies otherwise is selling you a way to get your family killed. What a household can be is prepared, lawful, uninteresting, and difficult to exploit. That is a great deal more than nothing. History shows that ordinary civilian choices have sometimes protected people and preserved communities under organized force. They have never made civilians invulnerable.

Part one: defending your household

Know the law on an ordinary day

Self-defence law is a Chapter 5 subject that people insist on learning during the worst minute of their lives. Learn it now, as orientation, not advice — the details are your jurisdiction's, and they differ more than you'd expect.

The shape of it in the United States: the law generally asks whether you reasonably believed force was necessary to defend yourself or another person against an imminent unlawful threat. The amount and type of force must be justified by the circumstances. Deadly force is subject to a much higher threshold, generally involving a reasonable belief of imminent death or serious bodily harm. The exact tests, terminology, and defences vary by state.

Many states recognize some form of the castle doctrine for the home. At least 31 states recognize, by statute or court decision, no general duty to retreat before using force in a place where a person has a legal right to be. Other states retain a duty to retreat in at least some circumstances outside the home, where retreat can be done with complete safety. These categories are not a complete map of the law. The rules can differ for deadly and non-deadly force, vehicles, workplaces, guests, and people who provoke or participate in a confrontation. Do not treat a general summary as permission to use force.

The home is treated specially in many states, but there is no single national rule that makes every use of force against an intruder lawful. A forced entry may create a legal presumption in some jurisdictions; the conditions and exceptions matter. Nowhere does a general self-defence rule give you permission to use force over property alone once the threat to people has passed. The legal question is not simply whether you were allowed to own or carry a weapon. It is whether the force used, at that moment, was lawful and necessary.

Learn your own state's exact rules from its statutes, current official guidance, or a local lawyer. Do it before an emergency, not afterward from an interview room. A useful starting point is the National Conference of State Legislatures' current overview of self-defence and retreat laws. It is an overview, not a substitute for local legal advice.

In Canada: Criminal Code section 34 allows a person to use force to defend themselves or another person when they reasonably believe force or a threat of force is being used against them. The act must be for the purpose of defending or protecting someone, and the response must be reasonable in the circumstances. The law lists factors such as the nature and imminence of the threat, whether other means were available, each person's role in the incident, and the proportionality of the response. There is no simple rule that every Canadian situation requires retreat, nor a blanket right to use force because someone is on your property. The circumstances matter. Get advice based on the province or territory and the actual facts.

The ladder

Defence in this book is a ladder, and the weapon — any weapon — is the last rung, not the first.

  1. Be a poor choice of target. Chapter 20's whole craft: don't advertise, don't testify with your garbage, be visibly connected to your neighbours. Predation often involves choosing among available opportunities. People who can move on may do so.
  2. Leave, or make leaving possible. Distance was Chapter 20's first principle and it stays first here. A household that can get out a back way, through an agreed neighbour's access, or to a running car has options a barricaded one doesn't. Don't fight over replaceable things. There is no object in your house worth a life in your house, including the ones in this book's checklists.
  3. Be heard. Voice, light, noise, a phone call in progress. Witnesses and the prospect of attention can discourage some confrontations. They will not stop everyone, and a person who is already violent may not be deterred.
  4. Resist, if resistance is what's left. If the threat is to a person and the rungs above are gone, the law above is the frame and training is the preparation. What resistance looks like in detail is learned in person, from qualified instructors, with practice — the same rule as the medicine chapters. Anyone who tells you a book or a video made them ready is mistaken about which of them is ready.

The defended home

The home itself does much of the work, and Chapter 15's hardening is the foundation. The defence-specific layer is short: sound locks fitted into sound frames, longer screws in the strike plate where appropriate, light where an approach happens, and a dog if your household genuinely wants a dog. Add the oldest security system in the book — neighbours who know your name and your car (Chapter 19).

Sightlines matter more than expensive hardware: a door you can assess without opening, and a way to speak to someone outside without letting them in. Decide, on an ordinary day, who answers the door, what the children do when there's a knock at a strange hour, and where everyone goes if someone forces entry. A household with a plan uses the first confused seconds on the plan. A household without one spends them deciding.

Do not turn the home into a trap. Keep exits usable. Do not block fire escape routes, create hazards for household members, or install devices that could injure a visitor, neighbour, child, or emergency responder. A defensive measure that prevents your own family from getting out is a bad measure.

Tools, honestly ranked

Ranked by their role in a household, not by what they cost:

  • Light, locks, and noise can discourage opportunistic entry and make it easier to notice and report a problem. They are useful while you sleep and do not require you to confront anyone.
  • Pepper spray, where legal for use against people, is a regulated defensive product. Its legality, permitted size, formulation, carrying rules, and lawful use vary by location. It can affect the person carrying it as well as the intended target, particularly in wind or enclosed spaces. Products expire, and a label does not tell you whether possession or use is lawful where you are. Read the local law and the manufacturer's safety instructions before buying or carrying one.
  • A firearm is the grave end of the list, and this book treats it the way it treats the chainsaw and the pressure canner: a real tool with a real safety discipline, not a personality. If there is one in your house, the rules are not optional and not original to this book.

Treat every firearm as loaded. Keep it pointed in a safe direction. Keep your finger outside the trigger guard until you have made the decision to fire. Know what you are looking at; never assume you have identified a target correctly. A bullet can pass through a target or a wall. Know what is beyond it.

When a firearm is not being handled by an authorized person, store it unloaded and locked in a secure safe or lockbox. Store ammunition locked separately. Keep keys and combinations inaccessible to children and anyone who should not have access. Hiding a firearm is not safe storage. A trigger or cable lock can provide another layer of protection, but it does not replace secure storage.

These are minimum household safety principles, not a complete firearms course or a statement of every jurisdiction's legal requirements. In the United States, state and federal rules may add requirements. In Canada, firearms must be possessed and stored in accordance with the Firearms Act and its regulations; storage requirements differ by firearm classification. A Canadian firearms licence is generally required to possess or acquire firearms, subject to specific legal exceptions. Check current RCMP guidance and the applicable regulations.

And — this is the part the gun-counter leaves out — an untrained household with a firearm has added a hazard, not automatically gained a defence. Training must be in person, hands-on, and repeated, from a qualified instructor. It must include safe handling, secure storage, legal responsibilities, and the limits of what a firearm can solve. Decide about firearms the way this book decides everything: on an ordinary day, with the whole household's reality on the table — including who in the house should never have access, and whether everyone who might use it will actually train. If those conditions cannot be met, do not keep one.

In Canada: a device designed or intended for use against people to incapacitate, injure, or otherwise interfere with them may be a prohibited weapon. Bear spray is not a loophole for carrying a weapon against people; it is intended for animal deterrence, and misuse can still lead to criminal consequences. Check current federal and provincial rules before carrying any spray.

The aftermath

If force is ever used in your home, the emergency is followed by a legal event, and it has its own rules. Call emergency services as soon as it is safe. Ask for medical help for anyone who may be injured, including the person against whom force was used. Do not approach a person who may still be dangerous.

Expect the incident to be investigated. That does not mean you have been found guilty; it means the authorities need to establish what happened. Give immediate information needed to deal with ongoing danger and medical emergencies. Do not lie, guess, speculate, destroy evidence, or coordinate accounts with other witnesses. When the immediate emergency has passed, ask for legal counsel before giving a detailed formal statement. Local law and the circumstances determine how that process works.

Do not tidy the scene, move objects, or try to improve the appearance of what happened. If something must be moved to protect life or prevent further injury, tell the responding authorities what you moved and why. Otherwise, leave the scene alone.

And expect the event to move into your head and live there — Chapter 22 is about that weather, and it applies to people who did everything they reasonably could.

Part two: occupation

What occupation is

An occupation is not simply what happens when a war ends and the force stays. Under international humanitarian law, occupation exists when hostile armed forces have established authority over territory and can exercise that authority. It can begin while fighting is still happening elsewhere, and it does not depend on the occupying power announcing that it is an occupation.

Your country's government may have fled, lost control, or been displaced. A foreign army may administer the town directly or work through local institutions. Soldiers may be at the junctions, officials in the offices, and new rules on the radio. Daily life continues inside someone else's authority. That is the defining feature, and the disorientation of the whole thing. The shops open. The curfew is real. Both are true on the same morning.

The law of it — and its honest limits

Occupation is not lawless in the legal sense, and the law is worth knowing precisely because your household can understand it, refer to it, and document violations. The rules do not depend on whether the occupation itself is lawful. Once the facts amount to an occupation, the law of occupation applies.

Under Article 43 of the 1907 Hague Regulations, the occupying power must take measures within its power to restore and ensure public order and safety, while respecting the laws already in force unless absolutely prevented. This does not give the occupier sovereignty over the territory. Occupation is a temporary form of authority, not a transfer of ownership.

Under the Fourth Geneva Convention, civilians who meet the Convention's definition of protected persons receive protections that include the following:

  • Article 27: Protected persons are entitled, in all circumstances, to respect for their persons, honour, family rights, religious convictions and practices, and manners and customs. They must be treated humanely and protected against violence, threats, insults, and public curiosity. The Convention also recognizes that security measures may be taken in certain circumstances, but those measures must remain within the Convention's limits.
  • Article 33: No protected person may be punished for an offence they did not personally commit. Collective penalties, measures of intimidation or terrorism, pillage, and reprisals against protected persons and their property are prohibited. Hostage-taking is prohibited under Article 34.
  • Article 49: Individual or mass forcible transfers and deportations of protected persons from occupied territory are prohibited, regardless of motive. There is a narrow exception for temporary evacuation when the security of the population or imperative military reasons demand it. Evacuated people must, as far as practicable, be returned to their homes as soon as hostilities in the area have ceased. The Convention does not make every movement of people unlawful; the circumstances and legal conditions matter.
  • Article 53: Destruction by the occupying power of private or public property is prohibited, except where that destruction is rendered absolutely necessary by military operations. This is not a general licence to destroy property by claiming convenience or administrative need.
  • Article 55: To the fullest extent of the means available to it, the occupying power has a duty to ensure the food and medical supplies of the population. If local resources are inadequate, it should bring in necessary supplies. Its ability to requisition available food and medical supplies is restricted; civilian needs must be taken into account, and fair value must be paid under the Convention.

These are not the only rules. The Fourth Geneva Convention contains further protections on treatment, detention, labour, medical services, and relief. The Hague Regulations and other applicable international humanitarian law also matter. The details depend on the legal status of the people and the situation. This chapter is a household guide, not a legal opinion on a particular occupation.

Now the limit, stated as plainly as the law: these rules bind the occupying power. They do not guarantee that it will obey them. Their practical enforcement depends on the circumstances, the ability to monitor violations, the availability of international mechanisms, and whether those responsible can eventually be held to account. The law is not a shield you can raise at a checkpoint.

It is three other things: a standard your own conduct can stay inside, a language for dealing with officials administering a system, and the foundation of the record described below. A clear reference to a legal duty may help when dealing with an office or requesting essential supplies. It may also make no difference. Documentation can support later investigation and accountability, but it cannot promise either. Occupations end. Some violations are prosecuted; others are not. A record is worth keeping because the truth matters, not because justice is guaranteed.

How it lands on a household

The instruments are boring, which is what makes them effective: identity papers and registration; curfews; checkpoints on the roads you need for food and work; searches, sometimes of houses; requisition — your vehicle, your fuel, a room in your house, taken with a receipt or without one; rationing and queues; new offices where permits come from; and informers, because occupying authorities may recruit locally and some of that recruitment is driven by fear, coercion, or the promise of advantage.

The pressure on a household is rarely a dramatic raid. It can be a hundred small transactions, each with a small price in money, dignity, or information, and the prices compound. The exact pattern varies. Some occupations preserve much of the existing administration; others replace it. Some impose strict controls; others rely more heavily on local authorities. That is why the next section is a craft of small things done consistently.

The civilian craft

Be grey. Avoid attracting attention you do not need. Ordinary clothes, ordinary routines, and calm, brief answers may reduce unnecessary friction. Do not advertise wealth, weapons, foreign contacts, or access to useful supplies. None of those things is shameful; they can simply make a household more interesting to people looking for leverage. Chapter 20's "don't advertise" becomes, here, a whole way of life.

Papers in order. In an occupied town, your documents are your Chapter 7 folder: complete, current, and consistent with the truth. Keep identity papers, permits, medical information, and essential household records organized and accessible. Know which documents must be carried and which should be kept secure. At a checkpoint, the practical aim is to produce the required paper, answer direct questions briefly, and avoid unnecessary argument. Do not invent a false identity, forge documents, or create contradictory records. Those acts can expose you and other people to serious danger.

Small compliances, bright lines. No household can safely assume it can refuse every demand. Some orders will be routine administration; others may involve serious violations of rights or direct danger. The craft is deciding your lines before they're tested: what you can comply with, what you will not voluntarily do, and what circumstances would make you leave.

You may decide to register, queue, carry required papers, and comply with ordinary administrative demands. You may decide not to inform on neighbours, denounce people, volunteer information, or lend your name to propaganda. Some demands will be made under threat, and a person forced to comply to protect life is not the same as a willing collaborator. Do not make promises about what you will do under coercion. Decide what you can, understand that circumstances can change, and do not judge another household's survival decisions from the outside.

Protect people first, then records, then things. Registration lists, censuses, school and workplace records, and information supplied by neighbours can all be used to identify people. The risk depends on the authority, the purpose of the list, and the way the information is used. Do not assume every official list is a roundup list, but do understand that personal information can become dangerous when authorities use it to target people.

Communities under occupation have used warning networks, trusted contacts, temporary relocation, and shelter to protect people at risk. These choices can also expose the people offering help, the people being sheltered, and their neighbours to severe consequences. Hiding someone is not a simple household trick. It can create risks involving food, medical care, discovery, separation, and reprisals. Do not improvise it alone or assume that one person's courage can safely carry the whole burden. Any decision must take account of the person at risk and everyone who may be endangered by the choice. This is neighbourhood work (Chapter 19), and it requires honest agreement about the risks.

Do not falsify records or put other people's names into invented paperwork as a general survival strategy. False documents can be discovered, and the consequences may fall on the people whose names appear in them. Protecting people sometimes requires privacy and discretion; it does not make every form of deception safe.

Keep the record. The notebook habit of Chapters 5 and 13 becomes, under occupation, evidence. Dates, times, locations, names and ranks if safely known, what was taken, what was ordered, who was harmed — written plainly, close to the event, with no embroidery, because embroidery is what discredits a true record.

Do not take risks to collect details. Do not confront armed people to get a name, photograph, or serial number. Do not keep a conspicuous record where its discovery could endanger the household or the people named in it. If you can safely preserve a record, keep it factual and distinguish what you witnessed from what someone else told you. Store copies securely, and consider whether a trusted person or reputable humanitarian or human-rights organization can preserve information outside the immediate area. A record containing names, locations, or allegations can itself put people at risk if it falls into the wrong hands.

You are not keeping it for revenge. You are keeping it because an accurate record may help establish what happened, support a missing-person inquiry, or contribute to later investigation. It may never reach a court. It is still worth preserving when it can be done safely.

Mind what you carry and say. Assume telephones and messages may be monitored or accessed (Chapter 18's posture, now with an adversary who has an office for it). Do not assume ordinary communications are private. The communication tree of Chapter 19 should share only the information each person needs. A network that does not expose every contact to every other contact may limit the damage when one device or account is compromised.

Children are briefed the way this book briefs them for everything: simply, truthfully, at their size. Explain the rules for checkpoints and strangers, what information is private, how to find a trusted adult, and what to do if separated from the household. Tell them not to volunteer family details or discuss private plans with strangers. Do not put the burden of protecting adults or keeping secrets under pressure on a child. Children may be frightened, confused, or questioned; they need clear safety instructions, not a responsibility they cannot carry.

Non-cooperation, at its real price

Between obedience and armed resistance there is a country, and much civilian history under occupation happened there: refusing particular demands, strikes, work stoppages, slowdowns, administrative non-cooperation, maintaining independent community institutions, and quietly helping people targeted by the authorities.

These actions have sometimes obstructed an occupier, protected people, preserved institutions, or raised the political and administrative cost of control. Their effects have varied enormously. They have not reliably shortened occupations, and there is no household formula that can guarantee a political result. The outcome depends on the occupier, the local population, outside pressure, the military situation, and the risks people are willing or able to bear.

The price can be severe. Collective punishment is prohibited under international humanitarian law, but it has occurred. Hostage-taking and reprisals have been used against civilians. People who pay are frequently not the people who acted. So the rule of this section is moral as much as practical: no one may spend someone else's courage. What your household risks is your household's decision, made by the adults who will bear it, with the costs to neighbours weighed as honestly as the costs to yourselves. A community that freely agrees on a limited act of non-cooperation is making a different decision from one household imposing risk on everyone else.

The collaboration traps

Occupations often rely on local people to keep administration running, and recruitment rarely arrives looking like treason. It arrives as a favour economy: the permit that comes faster, the ration supplement, the job in the office, the request to "just tell us if strangers move in" from a man who was kind about your mother's medicine.

Not every person who works in an occupied administration is a collaborator. Some are keeping hospitals, water systems, schools, or food distribution operating. Some are coerced. Some make choices that help the occupier harm other people. The difference lies in what they do, the choices available to them, and the consequences — not simply in whether they have a job.

The danger is the gradual exchange of information and favours for access or protection. Each step can become leverage for the next. The defences are the book's usual ones, sharpened — decide your lines in advance; do not volunteer information about other people; be careful about what you promise; and remember that the end of the occupation is a real day with real neighbours. A town may have to live with the consequences of those choices long after the occupier leaves. Do not assume you can judge every person's conduct from the outside.

Knowing when the answer is leave

Everything in Part Two assumes staying is viable. Sometimes it stops being. The warning signs that may mean it is time to leave include deportation or "resettlement" orders naming your household or category; roundups beginning in your town; credible information that your name or street appears on a list used to target people; deliberate denial of food or medical supplies; and a household member becoming a specific target of the authorities.

These are not universal triggers with a guaranteed timetable. Some orders may be lawful temporary evacuations under the rules of armed conflict; others may be unlawful forced transfers or deportations. The danger is not always obvious until it is advanced. Consider the actual conditions, available routes, the safety of the destination, and whether leaving now is safer than waiting. Do not wait for certainty if the evidence already points to an immediate threat.

The pattern to avoid is the oldest one in this book: leaving one month too late, because each individual week still looked survivable. Chapter 4's framework decides it — the same questions, with a state's power on the other side of the scale — and the go-bag, the documents, and the cached supplies of the earlier chapters are what make "go now" a sentence your household can actually obey.

What this chapter does not teach

It does not teach fighting technique, weapons handling beyond the safety rules above, tactics, ambush, sabotage, or the making of anything that explodes. That is not coyness and it is not a gap the next edition will fill. Those are military arts and specialist skills, learned through formal training and discipline. A household that improvises them against organized force is not made safe by enthusiasm or a few videos. It is exposing itself to risks it may not understand.

This book's promise is narrower and keeps: that an ordinary household can be lawful, prepared, uninteresting, and difficult to exploit; that it can protect its people, keep a record when safe, hold its lines, and know when to leave. Under occupation, civilians have survived through a wide range of choices, often under conditions no one would choose. This chapter does not pretend there is one answer that fits every household.

Chapter 22 — Your Head: Fear, Rumour, and Grief

Every chapter so far equipped your hands. This one is about the organ that aims them. Fear, grief, boredom, and rumour are not mood trivia in a collapse — they are survival variables, as physical as water: they can influence what a household does at 3 a.m., and households make important decisions at 3 a.m.

One boundary, stated before anything else, in the same spirit this book has kept all along: this chapter is first aid, not treatment. If you or someone in your house is in crisis — thinking of suicide, at immediate risk of self-harm, or unable to manage basic needs — real help is available. In the US, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time. If someone is in immediate danger, has recently attempted suicide, or may act on a plan soon, call 911 or get emergency medical help. In Canada: call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline. For other urgent medical needs, use Chapter 14's guidance and seek the appropriate care available. Needing help is not a failure of character. It's a reason to get help.

Fear: what it does, and what it's for

Fear is information, not orders. It can arrive with side effects worth knowing in advance: tunnel vision (the mind deletes the edges of the picture — which is where exits and options live), time distortion, catastrophic looping (the same three thoughts, louder), and the adrenaline comedown — exhaustion, shakiness, or tears after the danger passes. These reactions vary. They are not weakness, and they do not mean something is wrong with you.

The working disciplines are small on purpose, because small is what a frightened mind can hold:

Shrink the decision. Not "how do we survive this" — "what needs doing next?" Choose one safe, manageable action, do it, and reassess. A ten-minute question can be useful when a big question feels impossible, but ten minutes is a tool, not a rule.

Slow the body to slow the mind. Some people find slow, comfortable breathing helpful. Let the exhale be easy and unforced; don't force deep breaths or long holds. If focusing on breathing makes things worse, stop and try something grounding instead: notice the room, name what you can see, or feel your feet against the floor. No single technique works for everyone.

Name it to the room. "I'm scared and I'm having trouble thinking" said out loud can invite help and costs nothing. Do it with people you trust and when it is safe to speak openly. Nobody needs to perform calm for everyone else.

Sleep is a supply. Exhaustion can make judgment harder. Rotate the night watch when one is genuinely needed (Chapter 19), protect sleep where possible, and delay irreversible decisions until someone rested can help — unless immediate danger requires action now.

Rumour: the infection model

A frightened mind is hungry for certainty, and rumour is certainty's counterfeit — it spreads exactly like Chapter 9's diseases, by contact, in crowds, through dirty information hygiene. The household rules are the book's old ones wearing new clothes: label what you pass on ("I saw it" is a different substance from "I heard it"); check important claims against reliable, independent sources when time allows; and consider the civic version — don't forward fear you haven't checked.

There is one important exception: an urgent instruction from an official emergency alert or responsible authority does not need a second source before you act. Follow it. Verification is for rumours, unofficial claims, and major decisions when there is time to check. Two people repeating the same message are not necessarily two independent sources.

On a frightened street, the person who verifies before repeating becomes infrastructure, like the nurse and the generator. Children overhear everything, by the way — including the tone. Rumour discipline is also how the adults in a house teach the kids what kind of event this is going to be.

Decision hygiene

Most of this book's checklists are, if you look at them sideways, decisions made in advance by calm-you, for scared-you. That's the whole trick, and it generalises: decide what you can in peacetime; in the event, prefer the safe, reversible choice when one is available; write big decisions in the notebook with one line of reasoning, so tomorrow-you (fed, slept) can audit tonight-you instead of relitigating him.

If a decision can safely wait, set a time to revisit it. "We revisit this at breakfast" is a complete and respectable decision. Don't put off urgent care, an evacuation, or another necessary safety action just because the clock says you planned to reconsider later.

Grief, because it's coming

A real collapse contains losses that don't fit the word disaster: deaths without funerals, homes seen through a fence, the ordinary world itself. People can grieve all of these. Grief is a human response to loss, not a malfunction. It does not arrive in orderly stages or on a reliable timetable. It can come in waves, or show up as numbness, anger, relief, exhaustion, or something else entirely.

Children may show grief through behaviour — clinginess, sleep changes, irritability, regression, or changes in play — because they may not have the words for what they feel. Some children show very little outwardly. Give honest, age-appropriate answers, keep familiar routines where possible, and expect questions to come back around.

Improvised ritual can help: say the words, mark the day, plant something, share a memory, or make a safe and culturally appropriate ceremony. There is no prescribed ritual, and no need to burn or bury anything. A household that can mourn together may find it easier to face the next thing together.

And survivor guilt — why them, why not me, I should have — is grief wearing a prosecutor's coat. It is not evidence. Feeling responsible doesn't prove you were responsible. Answer it the way you'd answer it for the person you love most, because that's the assignment.

The long middle: boredom is an operational problem

Here is the secret texture of long emergencies: after the dramatic week, the enemy can be monotony — grey days, same food, no news that's news, the slow drift of people going flat and strange. Structure can help, and it is embarrassingly domestic: fixed mealtimes, the check-in round, work that matters (Chapter 19's rotas), learning something (Chapter 18's library is a morale engine as much as a reference), and — do not skip this line — celebrations kept. The birthday observed with a candle stuck in the rice. The song. The good tin saved for the anniversary of the bad day.

Morale supports survival; it does not replace food, water, medical care, or safety. In a long emergency, small scheduled things can give people something to look forward to and a reason to keep going. Keep the information diet lean for the same reason: two planned check-ins a day (Chapter 6) may be enough for routine updates; urgent alerts and changing conditions override the schedule. A continuous drip of dread just metabolises into the room.

One honest paragraph on the bottle: stress can make alcohol more tempting, and alcohol can make a difficult situation harder. It can impair judgment and coordination, disrupt sleep, and worsen conflict. No sermon: pay attention if drinking is increasing or becoming a way to get through every day. One important caution: someone who drinks heavily and regularly, or is dependent on alcohol, can become seriously ill if they stop suddenly. Withdrawal may require medical care. Arithmetic, not morals.

Watching each other

Be your people's early-warning system, but don't turn observation into diagnosis. Changes from someone's usual baseline can be clues: withdrawal, hopelessness, not eating, agitation, increased substance use, reckless behaviour, unusual anger, changes in sleep, or giving away treasured things. No single sign predicts suicide, and some people show few outward signs.

If you are wondering whether to ask someone directly if they're thinking about suicide, ask. The evidence does not support the fear that asking plants the idea. A plain question can open a door: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Then listen without judgment. Don't shame, minimise, argue, or promise secrecy if their immediate safety is at risk.

If there is immediate danger, a recent attempt, or a plan they may act on soon, call 911 or get emergency help. Stay with them if it is safe to do so, and don't leave them alone while waiting for help if you can safely remain. If the danger is not immediate but they need support, offer to call or text 988 with them in the US, or connect them with a clinician or crisis service. In Canada: call or text 9-8-8. A person whose distress is persistent, worsening, or interfering with basic daily life deserves support too; they do not have to reach a crisis before asking for help.

You are allowed to not be okay. The goal was never serenity; it's function and kindness, sustained. Courage, in a long event, mostly looks like the next meal cooked, the next round walked, the next person asked a plain question. Which is to say it looks like a household. Which is to say it looks like Chapter 19, done by people who have read this chapter too.

Chapter 23 — The $100 Start, Give or Take

If you read this whole book and do nothing, this chapter is your refund. Everything in twenty-two chapters compresses into one shopping trip and one weekend of setup — because the truth the whole book has been circling is that preparedness is not a hobby with a catalogue. It's a shelf, a folder, a plan, and a household that has had one conversation. Here is the trip, the weekend, and the order to do them in.

The title is a starting point, not a promise that every household can buy a complete emergency kit for a hundred dollars. Prices vary by store, region, brand, and season. Check local prices before you go, use sales and substitutions, and build in stages if the budget is tight. The order matters more than the exact brands — the order is the book's own order of danger.

The trip (about $100)

Water first — Chapter 2.

Three 10-litre water containers (or one 20-litre and one 10-litre) — allow about $15, depending on the container. That is 30 litres: two people for three days at Chapter 2's four litres per person per day, with a little to spare.

One small bottle of plain, unscented household bleach, 5–9% sodium hypochlorite — allow about $2. Check the label and Chapter 2 before using it to treat water; not every bleach product is suitable.

Bar soap, a multi-pack — allow about $3, and it will appear in three chapters.

Food — Chapter 1's starter shelf.

Rice, 4 kg — price-check locally.

Red lentils, 2 kg — price-check locally.

Rolled oats — price-check locally.

Peanut butter — price-check locally.

Cooking oil, 1 L — price-check locally.

Sugar and salt — price-check locally.

A few tins of what you'll actually eat — price-check locally.

A manual can opener, if you don't already own one.

These are ingredients, not instant meals. Rice and dried lentils need water, fuel, and cooking time. Include foods you can prepare with the equipment and fuel you actually have. Raw sprouts are not part of the plan: warm, moist sprouting conditions can also support harmful bacteria, and rinsing does not guarantee safety.

Light and information — Chapters 6 and 7.

An LED lantern or headlamp, plus batteries — allow about $10.

A battery-powered or hand-crank AM/FM radio — allow about $20, depending on the model. Check which local stations carry emergency information and test reception where you live.

The radio is useful, but there is no universal rule that it outranks food. If you already have a reasonable food supply, a radio may be the next sensible purchase. If you don't, build the basic food and water supply first, then add the radio.

The medicine shelf — Chapters 13 and 14.

Store-brand acetaminophen and ibuprofen, if suitable for the people in your household — allow about $6 for both. Check active ingredients, labels, and contraindications; neither is right for everyone.

Gauze, adhesive bandages, medical tape, and disposable gloves — allow about $5. A basic thermometer may be a later addition.

Oral rehydration salts (ORS), if available within the budget. Otherwise, the sugar and salt above can be used for Chapter 9's carefully measured home recipe. Follow the exact water quantity.

Prescribed medicines, essential medical devices, glasses, and other household-specific needs may matter more than any generic medicine purchase. Ask a pharmacist or clinician about suitable supplies and storage.

The unglamorous rest — Chapters 8, 9, and 15.

Two sturdy buckets with lids — allow about $6.

Heavy-duty garbage bags — allow about $3.

Matches or a lighter — allow about $2, stored safely and kept away from children.

A notebook and pencil — allow about $2.

Cash — not from the store. Draw a modest supply in small bills from the bank and keep it with the documents folder (Chapter 7): enough for a few days of essentials if the card machines are down. It is not part of the hundred — it is money moved, not money spent.

Those are rough allowances, not a verified shopping basket. The original sample prices do not add up to $100: water is about $20, food about $31, light and radio about $30, medicine about $11, and the remaining items about $13 — roughly $105 before tax, even on these estimates. Some of those estimates, especially food, may be far too low in your area. Price-check before treating the list as a budget.

The buckets are starter components, not a complete sanitation system. They need suitable liners, stable placement, lids, a plan for keeping waste away from food and living areas, and a safe disposal method consistent with public-health instructions. Containment is not disposal.

That's the hundred-dollar start, give or take — and it may cost more. Build from what you already own. If you have suitable containers, a can opener, a working light, or basic first-aid supplies, don't buy duplicates just to complete a list.

What is not on the list is as designed as what is: no gas masks, no tactical anything, no freeze-dried bucket at a panic markup. Safety theatre is the most expensive genre in the store, and this book refuses it on every page.

The weekend

Saturday morning is the trip. Saturday afternoon is the putting away, done properly the first time: containers washed and sanitised by Chapter 2's steps and filled; the shelf stacked with Chapter 1's rotation in mind (eat from it, top it up — a pantry you use is a pantry that works); the two-bucket toilet assembled once, dry, in daylight, so the first time is a rehearsal and not a 3 a.m. debut; photocopies made and the documents folder (Chapter 7) started; med cards (Chapter 13) written at the kitchen table.

Sunday is the household itself, and it's the most valuable half. Set aside time when everyone can take part. Choose the out-of-area contact and meeting points, fill in the communications card (Chapter 6), and keep a copy where household members can find it. Pick a family code word (Chapter 18) that everyone understands and knows how to use.

Walk through the shutoffs — electrical panel, water main, and gas (Chapter 15) — but don't operate anything you're not sure how to use. Know when to leave rather than investigate. Never delay an evacuation to shut off utilities, and never restore gas yourself. Point out the fallout shelter location (Chapter 16) and test the smoke alarms (Chapter 15 again).

Make the household plan fit the household you actually have: prescriptions, glasses, hearing aids, mobility needs, medical devices, infants, children, and pets. Decide who needs help getting out and what happens if the usual helper is unavailable. Choose an evacuation destination and route, plus an alternative. Introduce the household to the street, if it hasn't been — a knock, a name, a "we're the house with the blue door if you ever need anything."

Then, Sunday evening, cook one meal from the shelf — rice, lentils, the good oil — and eat it together. That meal is the proof of work. Preparedness that has never been dinner is still a theory.

Keeping it alive

If you can spare five dollars a month, use it to top the shelf back up as you eat from it. If you can't, add what you can when you can. Little and often is the whole idea.

Set two reminders a year, perhaps when the clocks change: check water storage and refresh it according to the guidance for your containers and local water authority; audit the medicine cabinet (Chapter 13); test batteries and equipment according to manufacturer instructions; and rotate food by eating what is nearing its date and replacing it. Separately, test the smoke alarms monthly — that one does not wait for the clocks to change. The calendar is a reminder, not a substitute for checking the actual supplies.

Once a year, on the rotation weekend, the policy gets its ten minutes: declarations page read, exclusions checked, flood and backup questions answered in writing, coverage limits and deductibles reviewed, the photo record refreshed, one new serial number added. Insurance bought and never re-read decays exactly like food stored and never rotated.

The send-off

You cannot control the event. Nobody could ever control the event. What you control is smaller and it is entirely yours: the first seventy-two hours, the state of one shelf, the steadiness of one household, and whether the people on your street know your name. Emergency agencies in many countries give the same advice Chapter 3 opens with: be ready to manage on your own for the first days. They are not telling you the world is ending. They're recognising that help and normal services may not be immediately available.

You are not now invulnerable, impossible to panic, or guaranteed to manage every emergency. You have a better starting point, more options, and a household that has talked about what to do. And — this is the part no pamphlet can issue — you may be useful to the people around you. That was always the whole idea.

If this book helped, lend it to a neighbour. Chapter 19 explains why that's not a figure of speech.

Glossary {: .backmatter }

Anaphylaxis — A severe allergic reaction that can close the airway. A medical emergency, not a wait-and-see.

Areole — The small cushion on a cactus pad that the spines and glochids grow from.

Aril — A fleshy covering around a seed. On a yew, the red aril surrounds a poisonous seed.

Basal — At the base of the plant, at ground level.

Biennial — A plant that grows leaves in its first year, flowers in its second, and then dies.

Blanching — Dipping food briefly in boiling water, then cooling it, before storing or eating.

Botulism — Poisoning by a toxin from bacteria that grow in sealed, low-acid food kept without air. Rare, and it can be fatal.

Cardiac glycoside — A plant compound that acts on the heart's rhythm. Foxglove is full of them; the dose makes the poison, and there is no safe home dose.

Catkin — A dangling flower cluster: the spring tassels of willows, birches and alders.

Conifer — A cone-bearing tree with needle or scale leaves: pine, spruce, fir. Yew looks the part and bears no cones.

Criminal Code — Canada's federal criminal law. Section 34 is the self-defence provision.

Curfew — An official order requiring people to stay off the streets by a set hour.

Cyanogenic glycoside — A plant compound that can release cyanide when the tissue is chewed or digested. Found in elderberry leaves, stems and unripe fruit.

Deciduous — Trees and shrubs that drop their leaves for the winter.

Dehydration — Losing more water than you take in. In an emergency it kills faster than hunger does.

Duty to retreat — A rule in some jurisdictions: where you can safely leave a confrontation, the law expects you to leave before force is justified.

Electrolyte — The salts the body uses to hold and move water — sodium, potassium and others. Sweat, vomiting and diarrhea drain them.

EMP — Electromagnetic pulse: a burst of energy that can disable electronics and power systems across a wide area.

Fallout — Radioactive dust and debris that settles back to earth after a nuclear detonation.

Foraging — Gathering wild plants for food. This book's standard: the whole plant, identified, or not at all.

Frostbite — Tissue damaged by freezing. It can kill skin and deeper tissue if it is handled badly.

Geiger counter — A handheld instrument that detects radiation, usually by clicking faster as levels rise.

Geneva Conventions — The international agreements setting the rules of war, including how an occupying force must treat civilians.

Genus — The first word of a scientific name, grouping close relatives. Quercus is the oaks.

Glochid — The tiny barbed bristle in a prickly pear's areoles. Nearly invisible, miserable to remove; the plant's real hazard.

Go-bag — The packed bag that lets a household leave in minutes: documents, water, medicine, clothes.

Husk — The outer covering of a nut, removed before the nut is eaten.

Hypothermia — The body's core temperature falling too low. It clouds thinking before it stops the heart.

Invasive — A species spreading aggressively beyond its native range, crowding out what belongs there.

Language model — The kind of AI described in the note at the end of this book: a system trained to produce fluent text, whether or not the text is right.

Leaching — Soaking or boiling a food in water and discarding the water, to draw out bitterness or toxins. Acorns are the standing example.

Leaflet — One small blade of a compound leaf. An elderberry leaf is several leaflets on a single stalk.

Look-alike — A plant close enough in appearance to another to be mistaken for it. The dangerous half of every pair in Chapter 10.

Looting — Theft from unguarded property during a disaster or disorder.

Mylar — A reflective plastic film, used in emergency blankets and food-storage bags.

Naturalized — Brought from elsewhere, and now growing wild on its own.

Occupation — Control of a territory by a foreign force, or the period of that control.

Oxygen absorber — A small packet sealed into a food container to remove oxygen and slow spoilage.

Perennial — A plant that lives for years, dying back and returning with the seasons.

Phishing — A forged message or website built to steal passwords, money or access by imitation.

Pinnate — A compound leaf with leaflets arranged along a central stalk, like a feather.

Potassium iodide — A salt that fills the thyroid with stable iodine so it takes up less radioactive iodine. Only useful in a radiation event, and only as officials direct.

Power grid — The network that generates and delivers electricity. Much of this book begins at the moment it stops.

Proportionality — The legal measure of force: no more than the threat in front of you justifies.

Rhizome — A horizontal underground stem that spreads a plant and stores its energy. Cattails and irises both grow from them.

Rolling boil — A full, continuous boil that stirring cannot stop. When this book says boil, this is what it means.

Rosette — A flat circle of leaves at a plant's base, hugging the ground. Burdock's first year; foxglove's trap.

Rotation — Using stored food oldest first and replacing what you use, so the stores never quietly expire.

Safrole — A compound in sassafras root, restricted in food after cancer findings in animal studies.

Salicin — A compound in willow bark, a chemical relative of aspirin. A relative is not a dose; this book gives none.

Seizure — Uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain. Water hemlock poisoning can produce fatal ones.

Shelf-stable — Food that keeps safely at room temperature for an extended period.

Shielding — Material between you and radiation: earth, concrete, water, distance. Chapter 16's trio is time, distance, shielding.

Species — The second word of a scientific name, the exact plant. Quercus alba is the white oak.

Sprouting — Germinating seeds in water and eating the shoots: the cheapest fresh vegetable, with the warnings in Chapter 1.

Supply chain — The linked system that moves goods from makers to shelves. A break anywhere shows up everywhere.

Tannin — The bitter, astringent compound in acorns and bark that leaching removes.

Tendril — A thin, curling grip a climbing vine grows. Grapes have them; Canada moonseed does not.

Tourniquet — A tight band applied above a wound to stop life-threatening bleeding from a limb.

Tuber — A swollen underground stem or root where a plant stores food. The Jerusalem artichoke's harvest is tubers.

Umbel — A flower cluster whose stalks spread from a single point, like umbrella ribs. The carrot family's signature — and its warning label.
