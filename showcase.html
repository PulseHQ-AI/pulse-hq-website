The rules of the house

Approver, not babysitter

That's the operating doctrine, in the founder's words. The office is built so a human touches a job at the start and at one decision point — and nowhere in between.

Step 1

You drop a brief

One box: what needs doing. A post series, a research question, a design. That's your whole workload.

Step 2

The crew chains it

Remmy routes it — research to Tom, words to Johnny, visuals to Nik, clients through Maria, hard questions up to the lab. Research feeds writing; writing feeds design.

Step 3

You make one call

Manual jobs surface once: Approve, Rework, or Delete. Silence is never treated as a yes — unapproved work does not publish, ever.

Step 4

Standing orders skip the queue

Work you've already approved as a routine — the daily hunt, the weekly report, the Monday post batch — runs straight to Done. It never waits on your eyes twice.

Second floor · Research

The Research Department

Content is easy to generate; research you'd actually trust is not. So the office has a whole floor for it — a lab laid out like a pipeline, because that's what honest research is. Every question that matters goes up the stairs and comes back as a case file with its evidence attached.

A case, in motion

Real screenshots from a live case — “Research agentic workflows and how we can improve ours” — as it moved through the department.

Station 1

Case Intake

Every question enters here, logged and queued by Astrid, Walter's assistant. Nothing starts as a rumour; it starts as a case.

Station 2

Walter, the Reader

Walter works the stacks and pulls everything in — sources, papers, claims and counter-claims. A wide net, and nothing gets filtered at this stage.

Station 3

Peter, the Verifier

Walter's pile goes to Peter's two blackboards: the Evidence Wall on one side, False Claims on the other. Peter checks every claim against the sources Walter gathered — the checking is the process — and every claim leaves his desk with a label that reports what the checking found and how much uncertainty remains: documented, mainstream interpretation, fringe claim, or not verified. No exceptions.

Station 4

Tom, the Synthesizer

Tom commutes between this floor and the main office, and writes the case file using only what survived Peter. Failed claims go on a do-not-publish list — they don't quietly sneak into the write-up.

Station 5

The Case Archive

Every finished case, filed and kept — so the next question starts from source-checked work, not from scratch.

  • Documented
  • Mainstream interpretation
  • Fringe claim
  • Not verified

What the labels mean

  • Documented — supported by sources we can point to.
  • Mainstream interpretation — the standard expert reading of those sources.
  • Fringe claim — asserted, but outside the consensus and without the evidence to carry it.
  • Not verified — we could not check it against a source, so it stays out of publishable copy.
  • “Verified” — checked against sources. A checking process, not a guarantee that the sources are correct.

The lab also answers mid-job, from anywhere in the building: when a claim gets tricky while a post is being drafted, Tom escalates it up here — Walter reads, Peter rules, Tom writes the verdict back — and the job waits for the answer. It has already fired live, twice: Dyatlov Pass came back 21 documented / 1 not verified; the Carl Higdon case, 8 / 1. The not-verified claims went on the do-not-publish list, and the writer obeyed it.

Down in the lab's south nook sits Olivia, who drafts the venture's X posts (@PulseHQOffice) into a Post Queue from the department's finished work. A draft can't be saved without an image or a link; she never mentions the founder; a human copy-pastes and posts — nothing in this office logs into anything. Her standing batch runs every Monday. And like every finished post here, hers end with their strongest topic hashtags — no filler. That's a rule now, enforced by the server, not a habit.

Fair questions

Before you ask

Can anything get posted without me seeing it?

No. Every draft waits in your approval queue until you approve it, send it back for rework, or delete it. Silence is never treated as a yes — unapproved content simply doesn't publish. Standing routines you've already approved as a package are the one exception, and you set those.

Am I buying the office itself?

No — and that's deliberate. The office stays ours; it takes on clients. You receive the deliverables — posts, images, research, reports — packaged for your business. You never have to run, host, or maintain anything.

Do you need my passwords?

Never. Access is delegated through official tools — Meta Business Suite partner access and a scheduling tool — and it's tested during onboarding so the start doesn't stall on credentials.

Will it sound like me?

That's what the pilot is for. The first batch is a voice-calibration set: every correction you give — wrong fact, off-voice, wrong offer — becomes a standing brand rule for your crew, so month two sounds more like you than week one.

How do I know the facts in my posts are right?

Anything that matters goes through the Research Department: one agent gathers sources, a second exists purely to distrust them, and every claim comes back labeled — documented, mainstream interpretation, fringe claim, or not verified.

How much of my time does this take?

Two touchpoints: drop the brief, then make one approval call per piece. The crew, the routing, and the chasing happen inside the office. Approver, not babysitter.
